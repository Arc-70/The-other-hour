## Chapter 72: Malfoy Manor

The wood was called Tollard's Hanger on the Ordnance Survey map Hermione had bought in a petrol station outside Salisbury, and it ran along the top of a chalk ridge two miles north of Malfoy Manor, and at ten past four on the afternoon of Saturday the tenth of January, in a thin grey drizzle, Harry's hold on Ron's face slipped.

He felt it go.

He had been holding faces for nine days. Not on the screen. On people. They had been out of the Majestic on four raids since Christmas, one after another, because Theo's coin had begun to come every other night now and every message was worse. A house in Wigan with a family in the cellar. A warehouse at Tilbury. A Snatchers' camp in the Forest of Dean. And this, the fifth: two lines, at two in the morning on the seventh, which had taken Harry an hour and a quarter.

*LOVEGOOD AND OLLIVANDER IN THE MANOR CELLAR. HE IS AWAY. BACK SUNDAY.*

He had held four faces on four raids in nine days, and slept, according to a red notebook he was not supposed to know about, eleven hours in all. He had held Ron as a ginger-bearded hiker with a walking pole all the way up the side of the ridge from the lane, and Hermione as an older woman in a waxed jacket, and himself as a thin young man with a long nose, and he had made every bird and every rabbit and every Snatcher in the wood see four Muggles out for a walk in the rain.

And at ten past four, under a beech tree, with the drizzle coming down through the bare branches and the Manor's chimneys just visible through the trees below, the face on Ron went out.

Not all of it. One side. For one second the ginger beard was on the left of Ron's face and not on the right, and the right was Ron, freckled and long-nosed and pale.

It was enough.

"Oi," said a voice behind them. "Oi. You four. Stand still."

* * *

There were six of them.

They came out of the trees on three sides, wands up, in a loose ring, and they were not Muggles out for a walk. They were filthy and cheerful, in a mixture of old robes and Muggle coats, and they were pleased, and Harry read the pleasure in all six of them at once and knew exactly what it was. It was the pleasure of men who were paid by the head.

"Well, well," said the one in front. He was thin and pale, with a red neckerchief and a lot of oily hair. "Walking, are we? In the rain? On the Malfoys' land?"

"Footpath," said Harry. "It's a public footpath."

"Is it." The thin man smiled. "Funny. 'Cause I could've sworn your mate's face just moved."

Harry put it back.

He put Ron's beard back on and held it there, hard, and made all six of them see it, and he felt three of them believe it at once, and two not care, and the thin one with the neckerchief not believe it at all. The thin one was suspicious. It was a small, sharp, greedy suspicion, aimed at Ron, and it did not go away when the beard came back. It got sharper.

"Names," said the thin one.

"Dudley," said Harry. "Dudley Dursley. This is my uncle Vernon, my aunt Marge…"

He had perhaps a second. Perhaps less.

He did the thing Lockhart had taught him on a clifftop. Not one perfect lie. A hundred bad ones, fast. He threw a shout into the trees on the left, a dog barking on the right, a running figure going away between the beeches behind, and three of the Snatchers turned, and the thin one did not.

He had nothing left for the thin one.

He knew it at once. He reached for the next illusion and it was not there. It was like reaching for a step in the dark and finding the stair had ended. He had held too many for too long, and his nose was bleeding, and his hands were shaking, and the thin man with the neckerchief was raising his wand, and the beard on Ron's face went out again, both sides, and stayed out.

"*Weasley*," said the thin man, delighted. "That's a *Weasley*. I'd know that hair in the dark."

Hermione's wand moved.

Not at the Snatchers. At Harry.

He felt it hit him in the face, a hot hard sting like a hornet's, and his whole face went up at once, swollen and burning, his eyes closing to slits, his lips going thick, and he understood half a second later, with the part of him that was still thinking, what she had done and why. His own face was coming through the illusion. She had seen it come, before he had. And she had made it into a face that nobody would know.

Then there were wands in their backs and somebody had his arms, and the ring of Snatchers closed.

* * *

"Name," said the thin one again.

Harry was on his knees on the wet leaf mould with his hands tied behind him and his face burning. He could not see well. He could see the thin man's boots.

"Dudley," he said, through his thick lips.

"Dudley what?"

"Dursley."

"Never heard of him." The boots turned. "Her?"

"Penelope Clearwater," said Hermione, at once. "Half-blood. I can prove it."

"Course you can, love. Him? The Weasley?"

"Stan Shunpike," said Ron.

Somebody laughed. "Stan Shunpike's one of ours, you thick git."

"Barny Weasley, then. Cousin. Half-blood."

The boots turned again, and stopped in front of Evelyn.

Harry could see her, just. She was standing between two Snatchers with her hands tied in front of her, and her face was exactly as it always was, and he had not been able to put a face on her because nobody ever could, and none of the six had looked at her twice. They looked at her now.

"And you?"

"Hopwood," said Evelyn. "Mary Hopwood. I work in Records."

"Do you." The thin man looked at her. He looked at her for about two seconds, the way people always looked at Evelyn Selwyn, and then his eyes slid off her face as though there were nothing on it to hold them, and went back to Harry. "Never mind her. Nobody's ever going to pay for her. It's him I want." He bent down. "What's wrong with your face, Dudley?"

"Stung," said Harry.

"Stung." The thin man looked closer. He took hold of Harry's hair and pushed it back off his forehead, and looked, and Harry felt his greed sharpen to a point. The swelling had gone right up into his hairline. There was a scar under it, somewhere, stretched and blurred and lost in the puffed skin. The thin man could not quite see it. He could not quite not.

"Greyback'd know," said one of the others.

"Greyback's in Bristol." The thin man let go of Harry's hair. "Doesn't matter. Doesn't matter." His voice had gone very soft. "We'll take them down the hill. Let the family have a look. If it's him, that's a lot of gold, lads. That's more gold than any of us has ever seen." He straightened up. "And if it's not him, well. The lady of the house has got a sister who likes company."

They took them down the hill.

* * *

Malfoy Manor stood at the end of a long straight drive between high yew hedges, and it was lit in every window, and Harry read it as they came up the drive in the rain and found it full of fear.

Not the Ministry's fear. That had been grey and quiet and everywhere, like water in a tank. This was smaller and sharper and much closer to the top. There were only a few people in the house, and every one of them was frightened, and every one of them was frightened in a different way.

The iron gates opened by themselves.

The front door was opened by a house-elf Harry did not know, small and old and shaking, and the Snatchers pushed them through it into a great hall, and across the hall, and through a pair of tall doors into a drawing room.

It was very large. It had a crystal chandelier the size of a cart, and portraits on the dark purple walls of pale people with pale hair, and a fire burning in a marble fireplace, and a carpet so deep that Harry's feet went into it. There were three people in it.

"What is this?" said a cold voice. "What are you…"

It was Narcissa Malfoy.

She was standing by the fire. She was tall and fair and very thin, and Harry had seen her four times in his life, at King's Cross and at the World Cup, and she had always been beautiful in a way that looked as though it had been arranged. She was still beautiful. It had stopped being arranged. She looked at the four of them on the carpet, and at the Snatchers, and Harry read her, and found her afraid.

Not of them. Of everything.

It was a fear that had been in her a long time. It was the fear of a woman who has made tea at four o'clock every day on the good china for a man who says *no thank you, Narcissa*, and has stood in her own corridor afterwards, not moving. It was aimed, Harry found, almost entirely at one person, and it was not herself and it was not her husband.

Lucius Malfoy was sitting in a chair by the window.

He had been in Azkaban until August. Harry had read it in the *Prophet*: *released on review*. He looked as though he had been in Azkaban for a great deal longer than that. His long fair hair was dull and uncombed. His face was grey and his hands lay on the arms of the chair like things somebody had put down there and forgotten. He did not have a wand. Harry saw that at once. He had no wand, and there was nothing in him to read but a kind of dull grey shame, so old and so constant that it had stopped being a feeling and become a temperature.

And the third person was Draco.

He was standing behind his father's chair. He had grown taller since June. He was thinner. The scars on his face, the three white lines from temple to jaw and the one across the bridge of his nose, had gone silvery and flat, and they caught the firelight when he turned his head.

He turned his head.

He looked at the four of them on the carpet, and at Harry, and Harry, on his knees, with his face swollen and his eyes nearly shut, read him.

Draco knew.

He knew at once. It was the first thing Harry felt in him, before the fear, before anything. Recognition. Total, instant, absolutely certain. Draco Malfoy had sat in the same common room as Harry Potter for six years and been read by him every day of them, and he knew him the way you know the back of a person's head across a crowded room, by the way they hold their shoulders, and a swollen face was not going to make any difference.

He knew it was Harry. He knew it was Weasley. He knew it was Granger. And he knew it was Evelyn Selwyn, standing at the end of the line with her hands tied, looking at him with no expression at all.

"Draco," said Narcissa. "Come here. Look at him."

Draco came.

He came round his father's chair and across the deep carpet, slowly, and stopped a yard from Harry and looked down.

"Well?" said the thin Snatcher. "Is it? Is it him? It's Potter, isn't it? Say it's Potter."

Draco looked at Harry for a long time.

Harry read him. He read everything. He read the recognition, total and certain, at the top. He read the fear under it, which was enormous, and which Harry had known in Draco for six years and could have drawn with his eyes shut. He read something else under the fear, and something else under that, a long way down, and he could not tell what they were.

He did not know why he could not tell. He had always been able to tell with Draco. It was the one thing between them that had never once changed.

He could not tell now.

There was the recognition. There was the fear. And under them there was a kind of tangle, three or four things knotted round one another very tight, and none of them would come loose. There was something that might have been hatred and might have been a memory of a bathroom full of red water. There was something that might have been his mother. There was something that might have been the Dark Lord's face, very close, saying *I am disappointed, Draco.* There was something that was very like what Harry had once felt in him, crying, in front of a mirror, before he saw who was behind him. And there was something else, very small, that Harry had felt in him once in the Room of Hidden Things, when Draco had asked *what did it feel like, when I was going?* and Harry had said *like Cedric* and Draco had looked at him, and had not known what to do.

They were all there. They were all knotted together. Harry could not tell which one was on top.

"I can't," said Draco. "I can't tell."

"*What?*"

"I can't tell. His face is all… I can't see. It could be." He looked away from Harry, at his mother. "I'm not sure."

"Draco," said Narcissa.

"I'm not *sure*, Mother. I'm not going to say it's him if I'm not sure. If I say it's him and we call…" He did not finish. Everybody in the room knew who they would call. "And it's not him. If it's not him."

He did not look at Harry again.

Harry knelt on the carpet with his swollen face, and read Draco Malfoy walk back across his mother's drawing room to stand behind his father's chair, and could not tell, then or ever, why he had done it.

* * *

Bellatrix came in four minutes later.

Harry felt her before the door opened. He had felt her once before, in a stone chamber under the Ministry, duelling Sirius beside an arch, both of them laughing. He had put a second Sirius one step to the left in her head that night, and her red light had gone into the arch instead. She came down the stairs into the hall like something let off a chain, and she was the loudest thing in the house by a very long way, and what she was was glad.

Not pleased. Not cold, like her master. Glad, and hot, and hungry, the way a child is glad on Christmas morning.

"Visitors," she said, in the doorway. "Cissy, you didn't tell me we had *visitors*."

She was tall and dark and her heavy-lidded eyes went over the four of them on the carpet, and over the Snatchers, and over her sister and her sister's husband and her nephew, and stopped on Hermione's beaded bag, which one of the Snatchers was holding.

"What's that?"

"Hers," said the thin man. "Haven't looked in it yet. We were waiting on…"

Bellatrix had the bag out of his hand before he had finished. She looked into it. She put her arm in it up to the shoulder, and her face changed, and she took it out again, holding a silver-backed hairbrush and a tin of beans and a copy of *Hogwarts: A History*.

"Undetectable," she said. "Extension Charm. Clever girl." She turned round very slowly and looked at Hermione. "Clever, clever girl. Who are you, clever girl?"

"Penelope Clearwater."

Bellatrix laughed.

"No," she said, "you're not," and pointed her wand at the Snatchers. "Take the others down. The cellar. Not her. I want to talk to this one."

"Hold on," said the thin man. "Hold on. What about our gold? If it's Potter…"

"If it's Potter, he'll keep. In the cellar. And if it's Potter," said Bellatrix, very sweetly, "and you've brought my Lord's enemy into my sister's house and then stood about arguing with me about *gold*, I will feed you to Fenrir in very small pieces, and he will thank me." She smiled. "Take them down."

* * *

The cellar was dark and cold and smelt of damp stone and old wine, and there were two people in it already.

"Harry," said a dreamy voice out of the dark. "Is that you? Your face looks very odd. Have you been stung by something? There are Plimpies in the moat here, you know. Or there were. They might have died."

"Luna," said Harry.

"Hello." She was sitting against the far wall with her knees up and her arms round them, in a jumper with radishes on it. She had been there since the week before Christmas. She had a bruise on her cheekbone, going yellow. She sounded exactly as she always had. "This is Mr Ollivander. He's not very well. He's been here since August. Mr Ollivander, it's Harry Potter. He's been stung."

There was an old man lying on a pile of sacks under the stairs. He did not look up. Harry read him and found him very weak and very frightened and, under the fear, very faintly, interested, the way he had been interested in Harry at eleven years old across a counter in Diagon Alley, a long time ago.

The door at the top of the stairs closed. A key turned.

Ron sat down against the wall. Evelyn did not. She stood at the bottom of the stairs in the dark with her tied hands in front of her, quite still, and Harry could not read her.

Above them, through the floor, Bellatrix began.

* * *

He felt it before he heard it.

He would remember that, afterwards. That he felt it a second before the sound came through the stone. Hermione was lying on the floor of the drawing room above them, on the deep carpet, under the chandelier, and Bellatrix was standing over her, and Bellatrix said one word, quite softly, and Hermione's pain came down through the floor of Malfoy Manor and into the cellar and into Harry, all at once, and filled him.

He had felt the Cruciatus Curse before.

He had felt it on a spider in a classroom, in his fourth year, when a false Moody had done it to show them, and he had thought it was the worst thing he had ever felt from a living creature. He had felt it on Cedric in a maze, and in his own body in a churchyard. He had thought he knew what it was.

He had not felt it on somebody he loved.

It was pure. That was the thing he could not get past. It was pure, and endless, and it had no bottom, and Hermione was in it, all of her, with nothing left over, nothing that was still Hermione and still thinking and still clever, just the pain, going on and on, and through it, very faint, like a voice through a storm, she was holding on to one thing. She was not saying anything. Bellatrix was asking her, over and over, *where is the Cloak, where is Potter keeping it, who are the others, where is the Cloak*, and Hermione was not saying anything, and the one thing she was holding on to, under the pain, at the very bottom, was that she would not.

Then she screamed.

It came through the stone. Ron was on his feet. He was shouting. He was throwing himself at the cellar door at the top of the stairs, with his tied hands, over and over, shouting Hermione's name, and Luna was saying something, and Ollivander was weeping on his sacks, and above them Bellatrix laughed, and said the word again.

Harry stood in the middle of the cellar.

He did not throw himself at the door. He did not shout. He stood quite still in the dark with his hands tied behind him and his swollen face and his eyes shut, and he reached up.

Through the stone. Through the floor of the drawing room and the deep carpet and the chandelier, up, to the hot loud glad thing standing over Hermione with its wand out.

He found Bellatrix.

He found the gladness first. It was enormous. She was glad. She was glad the way he had read Dudley in a play park and Rita Skeeter in a broom cupboard and Umbridge in a pink office, glad all the way down, glad in a way that went on for ever with no floor. He went down through the gladness, fast, the way he had gone down through the grey in twelve people in Courtroom Ten, and at the very bottom of her he found what she was made of.

It was cruelty.

Not anger, and not hatred. Cruelty, plain, old, tended, the cruelty of a person who had looked at it years ago and liked what she saw and decided to keep it, and had fed it every day since, and loved it more than she had ever loved anything except one man.

Harry took hold of it.

He had done this once before. He had done it at eleven, in a room under the school, in terror, without knowing what he did, to a man who had been kind to him. He had taken the thing Quirrell was most ashamed of and given it back to him, larger, and Quirrell had died.

He was not in terror now.

He knew exactly what he did.

He took Bellatrix Lestrange's cruelty, the whole of it, all the years of it, every scream she had ever enjoyed and every face she had ever watched change, and he turned it round, and he gave it back to her.

Louder.

The laughing stopped.

There was a silence above them. One second. Two. Then Bellatrix screamed. Nothing like Hermione's scream. Higher, and much longer, and it had something in it that Harry had never heard come out of a person before, a kind of astonishment, as though the thing that was happening to her was a thing she had done to so many people that she had forgotten it could happen to her.

Ron had stopped hitting the door.

Harry did not stop.

He held it. He stood in the dark of the cellar and held Bellatrix's own cruelty inside her, and made it louder, and louder, and felt her feel it, every scream she had ever enjoyed coming back at once, and it was easy. It was the easiest thing he had ever done. It was easier than the twelve in Courtroom Ten and much easier than a face on two thousand people. It was just holding a door open and letting the wind come through.

He liked it.

He did not notice that he liked it. Not then. He would notice later. He stood in the cellar with his eyes shut and Bellatrix screaming above him, and there was something in him that was not the reading and not the push, something that was quite warm and quite steady and quite pleased, and it was aimed at the screaming, and it wanted more, and he gave it more.

"Potter."

It came from very close. From in front of him. Sharp.

He did not stop.

"*Potter.*"

Louder. Much louder. It was the voice of somebody who very rarely raised it.

Harry opened his eyes.

Evelyn was standing in front of him in the dark. Her tied hands were up between them, flat against his chest, pushing. Not hard. Just enough that he could feel them. Her face was a foot from his, and he could not read it, and it was white.

He let go.

Above them the screaming stopped. It did not die away. It stopped, all at once, and then there was a sound like somebody falling, and then a long ragged sobbing breath, and then Narcissa's voice saying *Bella, Bella, what is it, what happened*, and Bellatrix's voice, very high, saying *I don't know, I don't know, something, something in me*, and then nothing.

Evelyn took her hands off his chest.

She did not say anything else. She stood in front of him in the dark of the cellar and looked at him, and Harry looked back at her, and he did not know what was in her face, and he found that, for the first time in six years, he did not want to.

* * *

There was a crack in the middle of the cellar.

"Harry Potter," said a high squeaky voice, out of the dark, very fast. "Harry Potter, sir. Dobby has come. Neville Longbottom's coin is hot in the kitchens and Neville Longbottom is saying to Dobby, *Malfoy Manor, the cellar*, and Dobby is knowing the cellar, sir, Dobby is knowing it very well, Dobby was scrubbing it for eleven years." The house-elf was standing in the middle of the floor in a tea cosy and a great many odd socks, with his tennis-ball eyes shining in the dark and his long ears trembling. "Dobby is come to rescue Harry Potter."

Harry looked at Evelyn.

She had one shoe off. She was standing on one foot on the cold stone floor of the cellar, and in her tied hands, held up, there was a fake Galleon with its rim still glowing faintly. She had had it in her shoe since the Burrow.

"I sent it while you were busy," said Evelyn.

* * *
It went very fast after that.

"Can you take people?" said Harry. "Out? Through the wards?"

"Dobby can go where Dobby likes, sir. Elves is not like wizards. Dobby can take three. Maybe four, if they is small."

"Luna," said Harry. "And Mr Ollivander. Somewhere safe."

"Shell Cottage," said Ron. He was still at the foot of the stairs, with blood on his hands where he had hit the door. His face was grey. "Tinworth. On the cliff. Bill's. Dobby, Bill's house, by the sea, you can't miss it, it's got shells in the wall."

"Dobby knows it, sir." Dobby snapped his fingers and the ropes fell off all of them at once, Harry's and Ron's and Evelyn's and Luna's. "Dobby is coming back. Dobby is coming straight back for Harry Potter and the others. Wait, sir."

He took Luna by one hand and stooped and took Ollivander by the other, and Luna said, "Thank you, Dobby, that's very kind," as though she were being offered a biscuit, and there was a crack, and they were gone.

Above them, a door opened.

"What was that?" It was Lucius's voice, dull and frightened, at the top of the cellar stairs. "What was that noise? Wormtail? Is somebody down there?"

Nobody answered. Harry stood at the bottom of the stairs in the dark with his hands free and his face still swollen and reached up, and found Lucius at the top of the stairs, alone, grey and wandless and afraid, and he could have made him see anything. An empty cellar. A rat. His own son lying on the floor.

He did not have to. There was another crack.

"Dobby is back," whispered Dobby, at his elbow. "Miss Lovegood is at the house with the shells, sir. And the old wandmaker."

The cellar door at the top of the stairs swung open.

* * *

They went up the stairs together, Harry and Ron and Evelyn and the house-elf in the tea cosy, and through the door, past Lucius Malfoy, who saw them come and put his hands up in front of his face and backed away into the hall wall, and into the drawing room.

Hermione was on the carpet.

She was lying on her side under the chandelier with her hair across her face and one arm flung out, and she was not moving. Bellatrix was on a sofa by the fire with her sister kneeling beside her. She was holding her own head in both hands, and rocking, and her face had gone a dirty yellow-white, and her heavy-lidded eyes were open very wide and fixed on nothing. Two of the Snatchers were standing by the window, arguing. Draco was by the fireplace with his wand in his hand.

They all turned at once.

"*Expelliarmus!*" shouted Ron.

The thin man's wand flew out of his hand and Ron caught it, and went for Hermione, straight across the carpet on his knees, and Dobby snapped his fingers, and the great deep carpet rolled itself up from the far end like a wave and took the other Snatcher's legs out from under him, and he went down with a crash into a little table full of china.

Draco raised his wand.

He raised it at Harry. Harry saw it come up. He saw it point at his chest from six feet away across a drawing room, the hawthorn wand he had seen in Draco's hand for six years, in the common room and the corridors and a bathroom full of red water and a moonlit tower. He read Draco, and the knot in him was still there, all four or five things tangled tight, and he could not tell what was on top, and he did not wait to find out.

He did not use magic.

He crossed the six feet in two steps and took hold of Draco's wrist with one hand and the wand with the other, and twisted, hard, the way Dudley had once twisted his arm behind the garage at Privet Drive when they were nine, and the wand came out of Draco's fingers into his.

Draco made a small sound. Not pain. Something else.

Harry stepped back with Draco's wand in his hand.

It was warm. He noticed that. It was warm from Draco's palm, and it was hawthorn, and it was ten inches, and it did not feel like his own wand at all, and he did not care. He pointed it at Draco's chest.

Draco looked at him.

His face was very white. The scars stood out on it, silver. He did not move. He did not try to get the wand back. He stood by the marble fireplace in his mother's drawing room with his hands empty at his sides and looked at Harry Potter, who was holding his wand, and Harry read him, and the knot was still there, and he still could not tell.

"*Harry!*" Ron had Hermione in his arms. She was limp. Her head was on his shoulder. "*Harry*, come on, *now*, Dobby, *now*…"

Bellatrix was on her feet.

Harry had not seen her get up. She was standing beside the sofa with her hair wild and her face still that dirty yellow-white, swaying, and her eyes were on him. On his swollen face. On the wand in his hand. And Harry saw her understand. He did not need to read it. He saw it go across her face, the thing he had done to her in the dark, and the person who had done it, and the knowing came up through her like a fish coming up through weed.

"*You*," said Bellatrix.

Dobby had Ron's arm. Ron had Hermione. Evelyn had Dobby's other hand, and with her free one she reached across and took Harry by the back of his collar, quite hard, like a mother taking a child out of a road.

"*Now*," said Evelyn.

Bellatrix's arm moved.

Harry saw the silver go across the room. He saw it turning in the firelight, end over end, very fast, a small bright thing coming at them from the sofa. He saw it, and he could have done something. He could have put something in her eyes, a second ago, before she threw. He could have put Dobby a yard to the left in her head, the way he had put Sirius a step to the left beside an arch. He had done it before. He knew how.

He did not.

He would think about that for a long time. He did not, because he was holding Draco's wand and looking at Draco, and because he had nothing left, and because the knowing was still going across Bellatrix's face and he was watching it with a small warm pleased feeling that he did not notice he had. And because he was too slow.

The drawing room turned and went out.

* * *

They landed on wet grass in a strong wind.

It was dark. There was the sea. Harry could hear it, very loud, below and in front of him, and smell it, salt and cold, and see, a long way down, white lines of it breaking on rocks in the dark. There was a cottage behind them with lit windows and shells set in its walls. There was a door opening, and light coming out across the grass, and voices.

Ron was on his knees with Hermione. Evelyn was on her feet.

Dobby was standing in the grass between them, holding Evelyn's hand, and looking up at Harry.

"Harry Potter," said Dobby.

There was a knife in his chest.

It was a small knife, very fine, with a silver handle. It had gone in just below where the tea cosy ended. Dobby looked down at it. He looked at it with his great tennis-ball eyes as though it were a thing that somebody had given him and he was not sure whether he was meant to keep it.

Then he looked back up at Harry.

Harry read him.

He had read him once before, in a small bedroom at Privet Drive when he was twelve, and found terror and devotion and a grey grinding misery. He had read him in a hospital bed. He had read him on a staircase at Hogwarts, holding a sock. He read him now, on a clifftop in Cornwall, in the dark, with a knife in his chest, and the misery was gone. It had been gone for five years. And the terror was gone too. What was left was very small and very clear, and it was aimed at Harry, and it was what it had always been, under everything, since the first night.

It was devotion. Plain and whole. And under it, so quiet he nearly missed it, Dobby was pleased. Pleased with himself. Pleased that he had come.

"Dobby came," said Dobby. He sounded surprised, and very proud. "Dobby came, sir."

He sat down in the wet grass, quite slowly, as though he had decided to.

Then he was not anything.

* * *

They buried him in the morning, on the top of the cliff, at the end of Bill's garden, where the grass ran out and the wind came straight off the sea.

Bill wanted to do it with magic. He came out at seven with his wand and said he would dig it, it would take a minute, and Evelyn said, "No."

She did not say anything else. Bill looked at her. He looked at her for a long moment, in the grey dawn, with the wind blowing his long red hair across his face, and then he went back into the cottage and came out again with a spade from the shed and gave it to her, and went in again.

She dug.

She dug for a long time. The ground at the top of the cliff was hard and stony under a skin of turf, and full of the roots of sea-pinks and gorse, and she was not strong, and she had never dug anything in her life. She took the turf off first, in squares, very carefully, and put them to one side in a pile, in order. Then she dug. She put her foot on the spade and leaned on it and it went in an inch, and she leaned on it again, and it went in another, and she levered up a clod and put it on the other side. Then she did it again.

Harry watched her.

He was sitting on the bench by the cottage wall with his back to the shells. His face had gone down in the night. Fleur had put something on it that smelt of seaweed. Hermione was inside, asleep, with Ron sitting on the floor by her bed and not sleeping. Luna had gone out at six and come back with a handful of sea-pinks and put them in a jam jar on the windowsill and gone out again. Ollivander had not woken.

Harry sat on the bench and watched Evelyn dig a grave.

She did not stop. She did not look up. She did not say anything. She dug with the spade until the hole was a foot deep, and then she hit a stone, a big one, and could not get the spade under it, and knelt down on the edge of the hole and got it out with her hands. It took eleven minutes. He counted. Her hands were bleeding when she had done it, at the nails. She did not look at them. She stood up and went on digging.

He did not help.

He sat on the bench in the wind with his hands on his knees and Draco Malfoy's wand in his pocket, and watched her, and did not get up. He could not have said why. He would try to say why, later. He would sit in a dead cinema in Leeds a month from now and try to remember what he had been thinking on the bench at Shell Cottage on the morning of the eleventh of January, watching a plain girl with bleeding hands dig a grave for a house-elf on a clifftop without magic, alone. And he would not be able to remember thinking anything at all.

She dug until a quarter to ten.

When the hole was deep enough she put the spade down and went into the cottage, and came out with Dobby in her arms, wrapped in a blanket of Fleur's, and knelt down at the side of the grave, and put him in.

She did not say anything over him.

She stood up and filled it in again, with the spade, clod by clod, the way she had dug it. Then she put the turf back. She put each square back in the place it had come from, in order, very carefully, pressing it down with her bleeding hands so that it would take. When she had done, you could hardly see where it had been, except that the turf was a little higher, and there was a line round each square where the grass had been cut.

Luna came and put the sea-pinks on it, out of the jam jar.

Evelyn went and found a white stone on the edge of the cliff, about the size of a loaf, and carried it back in both arms, and put it at the head of the grave. Then she took Bill's penknife, which he had left on the bench beside Harry, and knelt down, and scratched on the stone, slowly, with the point of the knife, for a long time.

When she stood up, Harry could read it from the bench.

*DOBBY. A FREE ELF.*

She put the penknife back on the bench beside him, without looking at him. Her hands had blood on them. Her face was exactly as it always was. She went into the cottage and shut the door.

Harry sat on the bench and looked at the grave.

It was very neat. The turf was pressed down. The sea-pinks were blowing a little in the wind. The white stone stood at the head of it with the letters cut into it, rough and careful, and the sea was very loud below.

He looked at it for a long time.

He looked inside himself for what he felt about it, the way you look for a coin in a pocket, carefully, in every corner.

There was nothing there.
