## Chapter 53: The Vision

The Defence Against the Dark Arts practical was on the afternoon of Friday the fourteenth of June, in the Great Hall, and Harry's examiner was the oldest witch he had ever seen.

She was tiny and bent, and her face was so lined it looked like a walnut, and she wore a hat with a feather in it that had gone grey too. Her name was Professor Griselda Marchbanks. She was Head of the Wizarding Examinations Authority, and she had said, at the start of the week, loudly, in the entrance hall, to Professor Umbridge, that she had examined Albus Dumbledore himself in his Transfiguration and Charms, and that she would be very glad to know where he was, because she had not heard from him since Easter and he owed her a letter.

Professor Umbridge had not enjoyed that.

Harry had read her not enjoy it from across the entrance hall. It had been one of the better moments of a long term.

"Potter," said Professor Marchbanks. "Is it? Yes. The famous one. Well, don't stand there being famous, it doesn't count for marks. Show me a Disarming Charm."

He showed her a Disarming Charm.

He showed her a Shield Charm, and a Stunner, and a Reductor Curse on a target at the end of the Hall, and a Hex Deflection, and a Banishing Charm on a cushion that went so far that it hit the doors. He showed her a Riddikulus on a boggart in a wardrobe, which became a dead boy on the grass with his eyes open and then, with a crack, became Professor Umbridge in a pink tutu, and Professor Marchbanks made a sound that Harry thought might have been a laugh.

He read her.

She was old and bored and very sharp. She had examined, Harry thought, about forty thousand children in her life, and most of them had been ordinary, and she had a long dry patience for ordinary that did not expect anything else. Under it, very small, there was a hope. It was the hope of a woman who has done a thing for a very long time, and has seen the very best of it only once or twice, and keeps turning up in case.

"Patronus," she said. "If you can. Extra marks. Don't worry if you can't."

He thought about the lake rock.

He thought about a jam jar, and a girl in a grey cardigan saying *I signed for it*, and himself laughing into his knees until his eyes were wet. He thought about a terrier going round and round a room, and Ron's face. He thought about the whole of Hogwarts laughing, floor after floor, with the dragons going over.

"*Expecto patronum*."

The stag came out of the end of his wand and kept coming.

It was bigger than it had been in the alley. It was bigger than it had been on the West Tower stairs in his third year. It was not as big as it had been on the lake shore, the first time, when it had been made out of somebody else's love. It was his own. It was made out of a rock, and a jar, and a terrier, and a staircase, and it walked out into the middle of the Great Hall, silver and enormous, and lifted its head, and the light from it filled the Hall to the rafters.

Every examiner in the room stopped.

Every candidate stopped. Harry read them stop. Ron, at the next table, with his wand half up. Hermione, at the end, with her mouth open. Draco Malfoy, three tables down, who had been in the middle of a Shield Charm and had let it drop.

The stag walked the length of the Hall. It went past the staff table and turned at the end, under the high windows, and looked back at him across the whole room.

Then it went.

Professor Marchbanks looked at the place where it had been for some time.

"Well," she said.

She wrote something on her parchment. She wrote quite a lot. Then she put her quill down and looked up at Harry, and he read her, and the small hope at the bottom of her had come up all the way to the top.

"I've seen one other like that," she said. "In this Hall. In eighteen ninety-six." She sniffed. "He was showing off as well. Off you go, Potter."

* * *

Professor Umbridge caught him on the Monday night, alone, on the grass between the lake and the forest, at twenty to eleven.

He had been at the lake. He had been sitting on the flat rock under the beech, where Evelyn had told him about a jam jar, because it was the last night of the exams but one and he could not sleep, and because the scar had been worse every night since Friday. He had been sitting there with the Cloak folded beside him and not wearing it, because he had not thought anybody would be out.

He had not been reading. He had been trying not to. The castle behind him was full of four hundred sleeping people and their dreams, and he had turned it all down to nothing so that he could listen to the water.

He read her a second before she spoke.

She was alone.

She was alone because she had wanted to be. The Squad was in bed. The castle was asleep. She had seen him from her window, a figure going down to the lake, and she had come out by herself, in a pink cardigan, with her wand, and she had walked down the grass without a light.

"Mr Potter," she said.

He stood up.

He read her, properly, all the way down, standing on the rock in the dark. He had read her a hundred times. Over tea, and across a classroom, and in a courtroom, and in another world over a boiled egg. He knew every floor of Dolores Umbridge.

There was a new floor tonight.

It was right at the top. It was very small, and very bright, and it had been decided. He could feel the shape of the deciding. It had happened in her office, at the window, a few minutes ago, when she saw him walking down to the lake alone.

"I have tried everything," she said pleasantly. "Detentions. The Squad. I've written to the Minister. I've written to your guardians, such as they are. I have asked you, Mr Potter, very nicely and for a very long time, to tell me where Albus Dumbledore is, and who in this school is still meeting in secret, and what you and your friends are planning. And you have lied to me." Her smile did not move. "Every time. So nicely."

"Yes, Professor."

"I am going to ask you once more," said Professor Umbridge, "and then I am going to stop asking."

She raised her wand.

He read what she was going to do. He read the word that was in her. It was not one of the three that he had heard in Moody's classroom in his fourth year, demonstrated on spiders. It was one of them. The second.

She was going to do it. She was not going to enjoy it, very much. That was what surprised him, even then. Under the deciding there was something that was almost reluctant, almost sad, and it was sincere. She was going to hurt him because it was necessary. Because the Minister needed to know. Because there was a great deal at stake. Because he had made her.

And under the reluctance, at the floor of her, where it had always been, warm and sweet and patient, the enjoyment was waiting to see.

"Where is Dumbledore?" said Professor Umbridge.

Harry looked at her.

He thought about Neville's hand. He thought about *I must not tell lies*, in his own writing, two hundred times a night. He thought about the white letters on the back of his own left hand, in the dark.

"In the forest," he said.

* * *

She did not believe him. He read her not believe him.

So he showed her.

He did it very gently. He had learned, over two years, that the best illusions are the ones that ask for very little. He did not give her Dumbledore. He did not give her anything she would not have expected. He gave her a light.

It was a small yellow light, a long way off, at the edge of the trees. It moved, very slightly, the way a lamp moves when somebody is holding it.

He read her see it.

"There," said Harry. "That's him. He comes at night. To see Hagrid. He's been coming since April."

He read her want to believe it. She wanted it more than she had wanted anything all year. She wanted to walk into the Minister's office with Albus Dumbledore in chains, and she wanted it so much that the light at the edge of the trees went into her, all the way down, and fit.

"Show me," she said.

He showed her.

He walked in front of her across the grass. He walked into the trees. And at the edge of the trees, without her noticing, he stopped walking, and let her go on walking behind a Harry who was not there, following a light that was not there, into the forest.

He went with her. A little way behind. In the Cloak.

He kept the light just ahead of her. He kept the other Harry just ahead of the light, walking steadily, not looking back. He took her deeper. He took her along a path that was not a path, past an old oak with a split trunk, past a stream, past a ring of flattened bracken, and he knew where he was taking her, because he had read them from the edge of the forest in his first year, and his third, and every year since, faint and fierce and proud, a long way in.

She did not notice when the trees changed.

She did not notice when the ground began to shake, very slightly, with something heavy coming through the undergrowth on every side. She did not hear it. He did not let her. He gave her quiet, and the light, and the boy ahead of her, and the smell of Dumbledore's pipe, which Dumbledore did not smoke, and which she believed in anyway.

Then he let it go.

* * *

He let it go all at once.

The light went out. The boy walking ahead of her went out. The quiet went out, and the forest came in, very loud, and she was standing in a clearing deep in the trees at eleven o'clock at night, alone, in a pink cardigan, with her wand up.

And there were centaurs.

There were thirty of them. Perhaps forty. They stood in a ring round the clearing, between the trees, with their bows in their hands and the moonlight on their flanks, and they had heard her coming for a long time, and they were not pleased.

Harry stood at the edge of the ring in the Cloak, and read them.

They were angry. It was an old anger and a proud one, and it was very cold, and it was aimed at her. She had walked into their forest with her wand raised. She was a witch from the Ministry. She was the Ministry. He could feel them know it.

He read her.

She was afraid.

She was so afraid that for a moment there was nothing else in her at all. The certainty was gone, and the stone, and the warm sweet patient thing at the bottom. It was all fear. It filled her up from the floor to the top and it was the plainest thing he had ever read in her, plainer than the enjoyment, plainer than anything.

Then she spoke.

He had known she would. She did not know how not to. She looked round the ring of centaurs with her wand up and her face white, and she said, in her high sweet voice, that she was a senior official of the Ministry of Magic, and that they lived in this forest by the Ministry's leave and at the Ministry's pleasure, and that their status was a matter for the Department, and that she had read the regulations, and that if they did not stand aside she would see to it…

He read the centaurs hear her.

He read the anger in the ring go up all at once, like a fire when somebody opens a door. He heard a bowstring.

He did not feel sorry.

He stood at the edge of the ring and looked for it. He looked for it carefully, the way he had looked for gladness in a frightened common room. He went down himself as far as he could, which was not very far, because he had never been able to read himself, and he looked for anything that was sorry.

He did not find it.

He found Neville's hand. He found two hundred lines a night. He found a woman who had been going to use the second of the three on him, sincerely, with regret, for the Minister.

He found that he was very calm.

The centaurs closed in.

He did not stay to watch. He turned round, in the Cloak, and walked back through the trees the way he had come, past the stream, past the oak with the split trunk, and behind him in the clearing she was screaming, and then she was not, and there was a great noise of hooves going away, deeper, into the forest.

He came out on the grass at twenty past eleven.

He walked up to the castle. He went in by the side door from the greenhouses, which Evelyn had shown him the trick of in his second year. He went down to the dungeons and through the stone wall and up to the dormitory, and he got into bed, and he lay on his back in the green dark.

He did not sleep. He was not upset. He lay there and listened to the lake, and he was perfectly calm, and he thought, after a while, that perhaps the calm was the thing he ought to be upset about, and he thought about that for some time.

He did not come to any conclusion.

* * *

Professor Umbridge did not come to breakfast.

Nobody remarked on it until lunch. At lunch somebody from Ravenclaw said that she had not been seen since last night, and that Filch was looking for her and was very upset. At tea Professor McGonagall said nothing at all, and ate a scone, and Harry read her, and there was a grim satisfaction in her, very deep, and something under the satisfaction that was a question, and the question was aimed at nobody in particular.

Evelyn looked at him across the table.

She did not say anything. She ate her egg and her toast in four and her tea last, and she looked at him once, at the end, over her cup.

Then she put the cup down and went to the library.

* * *

The History of Magic OWL was on the Tuesday afternoon, the eighteenth of June, at two o'clock.

Harry sat at a single desk in the middle of the Great Hall, one of three hundred, and turned over his paper, and read the first question, which was about the International Statute of Secrecy of 1689, and began to write.

He had not slept since Sunday.

He had not slept because the scar would not let him. It had been worse every night since Friday, since the stag. It came in on him as soon as he closed his eyes now. Feelings, cold, very strong. Impatience. Satisfaction. And under them, every night, larger every night, the thing he had felt at the end of April: the gathering. The careful hands laying things out on a table in lamplight.

He wrote about 1689.

The Hall was very warm. The sun came in through the high windows in long dusty bars, and lay across the desks, and three hundred quills scratched, and somebody at the back was breathing heavily through his nose. Harry wrote about wizarding communities going into hiding, and the reasons for it, and he wrote a paragraph about Gnomes, and he found that he had written the same paragraph twice.

He put his head on his hand.

He would rest his eyes. Just for a minute. He had twenty minutes in hand and the question on goblins was not until the end.

He closed his eyes.

* * *

He was walking down the corridor in the Department of Mysteries.

He knew it at once. The long stone passage with no windows. The plain black door at the end. It opened for him and he went through, into a circular room, and through another door, and into a vast high room full of shelves, row upon row upon row, towering up into the dark, and on the shelves there were small dusty glass globes, thousands of them, glowing faintly in the candlelight.

He was very tall. His hands were long and white.

He walked down the rows. He knew the way. He turned at row ninety-seven, and there was somebody on the floor at the end of it.

It was Sirius.

He was on his knees on the stone floor, and his face was bruised and his hair was wet with sweat, and he was looking up, and he was trying to stand, and he could not.

"Get it for me," said Harry, in a high cold voice that was not his. "Take it down. You will take it down, Black."

"I'll see you dead first."

"You will see a great deal before you die," said the voice. "*Crucio.*"

And Sirius screamed.

* * *

Harry was on the floor of the Great Hall.

He had fallen off his chair. An invigilator was standing over him, a tiny old wizard Harry did not know, saying *Mr Potter? Mr Potter?* and three hundred people were turned round in their chairs, looking, and Harry was saying, quite clearly, *no, no, no*, and he did not know how long he had been saying it.

He got up.

He said he was all right. He said he had been ill all week and it was the heat. He said he would like to go to the hospital wing. The tiny wizard looked at him with enormous concern and said that of course, of course, did he need help, and Harry said no.

He walked out of the Great Hall with his paper half finished on the desk.

He walked very steadily. He walked all the way across the entrance hall and up the marble staircase and along the first-floor corridor before he let himself stop, and put his hand flat on the wall, the way he had on a landing in September after his first cup of tea, and stood there.

He knew.

That was the thing. He knew. He had watched this in another world. He had sat in the Hog's Head at ten to six on the eighteenth of June in another world, with a goat called Mabel, and heard a plain girl in a grey cardigan run in through the front door and say that the other Harry and five friends had flown south on invisible creatures. He had stood behind a statue in an Atrium and watched them come back. He had watched a man with long black hair and James Potter's laugh fall backwards through a curtain in an arch, with a look of surprise on his face, and not come out.

He knew it was a trap.

He knew that Sirius was not in the Department of Mysteries. He knew that a boy who believed he was would go. He knew what happened to the boy and to the man, because he had seen it, and nobody else in this world had.

He stood with his hand on the wall and he breathed, and he made himself think.

* * *

And then, because he was standing still and thinking, and because he had turned the castle down to nothing, and because the door in his forehead had been open since November and had never once been closed, he felt something else come through.

It was not the vision. The vision was gone. The vision had been a picture, made carefully, the way he made pictures for other people. He knew the feel of that now. He knew what a made thing felt like from the inside, because he made them.

This was underneath the vision.

This was real.

It was coming up through the scar, very faint, very far away, and it was the thing from April, the thing from every night since Friday. The gathering. The careful hands. Only the room with the dust sheets had gone, and it was somewhere else. Somewhere high and cold and very old, with stone steps going down, and at the bottom of the steps there was something standing, an arch, very ancient, with a ragged black curtain hanging from it, moving very slightly though there was no wind.

He knew that arch.

He had stood beside it in another world.

And round it, on the steps, he could feel them. Not see. Feel. Small things, set out in a ring, and every one of them was hungry. Every one of them wanted with the mouth-wanting. A locket. A ring. A cup. A tiara he had watched Draco Malfoy take off a bust in a room full of hidden things. And something long and heavy and cool, coiled on the stone at the foot of the arch.

Five.

He counted them. He counted them the way Evelyn would have, without meaning to.

Five, set out in a ring, at the Veil, in the Department of Mysteries, tonight. And at the centre of the ring, waiting, the person who had laid them out. He could feel him wait. He could feel the patience, very cold, very careful, a man at the end of fifty years of work who has only one thing left to do.

The vision of Sirius was not a trap for Sirius.

It was not even really a trap for Harry. Or it was, but not the kind the other Harry had walked into. It was not about a prophecy on a shelf, or not only. It was a door, being held open. It was an invitation.

There were five, on the steps, in a ring.

And something in Harry, low down, the cold small thing he had found in a room full of hidden objects, turned round to face the south, towards London, towards a stone room with an arch in it, and pointed.

It made six.

He understood, standing in the first-floor corridor with his hand on the wall, that it was meant to make six.

* * *

He needed a fire.

The Floo was being watched. Every fireplace in the castle had been watched since March. Every one except one. Professor Umbridge had made sure of that in April, when she had told him, over a cup of tea, that her own fireplace was the only one in Hogwarts that the Ministry did not monitor, because it was hers.

Professor Umbridge had not been seen since Monday night.

He went up to her office.

The door was locked. He opened it with the charm he had learned from a diary in his second year, which made no noise at all. The office was empty. The kittens on the plates looked at him and frolicked and licked their paws. There was a cup of tea on the desk, half drunk, cold, with a skin on it, and a packet of Floo powder on the mantelpiece in a pink pot with a bow.

He took a handful.

He threw it on the fire and the fire went green, and he knelt on the hearthrug and put his head into the flames and said, very clearly, *Twelve Grimmauld Place*.

* * *

The kitchen at Grimmauld Place spun round him and stopped.

It was empty. It was the long cave of a room with the iron range and the stone floor, and the table, and nobody at it. Harry's head was in the fireplace, and the rest of him was a long way away in a pink office, and he could feel the carpet under his knees there and the ash on his face here.

"*Sirius!*"

Nothing.

"*Sirius! Kreacher!*"

There was a sound. A shuffle. Something came out of the dark by the door to the boiler cupboard. It was Kreacher, very old, in his rag, with his ears hanging, and he saw Harry's head in the fire and he stopped.

Harry read him.

He did not have to look far. On the top, very bright, too bright, the glee from Christmas night was back. It was bubbling, the pan nearly boiling over, the way it had been when he came in by no door at all. And under the glee, the old hatred. And under the hatred, the devotion, aimed outside the house, enormous.

"Where's Sirius?"

"Master has gone out," said Kreacher. "Master has gone out, yes. Master will not be coming back, Kreacher thinks. Master has gone to the Department of Mysteries."

Harry looked at him.

He read him say it. He read him enjoy it, all the way down. And he read the lie in it, because a lie had a shape, when you said it out loud, the shape of a thing put on top of another thing, and he had learned that shape in March from Draco Malfoy at a dinner table.

"Where is he, Kreacher?"

"Gone," said Kreacher, and grinned.

"He's upstairs," said Harry. "He's upstairs, isn't he. With Buckbeak. You've hurt Buckbeak. You've done something to Buckbeak so that he'd be upstairs and you could tell me he wasn't here."

Kreacher stopped grinning.

Harry read the glee in him go out, all at once, like a candle, and what came up instead was a very old and very bitter fear, and it was aimed at Harry.

"*SIRIUS!*" Harry shouted. "*SIRIUS, IT'S HARRY!*"

There were feet on the stairs.

There were a great many feet on the stairs, running, and then the kitchen door burst open and Sirius was there, in his shirt-sleeves, with blood on his hand and his wand out, and his face went white when he saw Harry's head in the fire.

"*Harry*. What's… what's happened? Are you…"

Harry read him.

Alive. Alive and whole and frightened, for Harry, enormously, the dog-at-the-door fear, and under it nothing at all that had been in a dark room full of shelves. Nothing of a stone floor or a wand or a high cold voice. Sirius had been upstairs, with a hippogriff with a cut leg, all afternoon.

"Listen to me," said Harry. "Listen. Don't talk. Listen."

Sirius listened.

* * *

He told it fast.

He told the vision, and the trap, and the other world, which Sirius knew about, a little, from a night in Dumbledore's office with a dog's head on Harry's knees. He told him about five things in a ring on the steps of an arch and a man in the middle of them, waiting. He told him about the sixth.

He told him what he thought the sixth was.

Sirius did not interrupt. Sirius's face, in the green light, went from white to grey to something very still, and he did not say anything until Harry had finished.

Then he said, "Right."

He turned round. There were other people in the kitchen now, behind him, in the doorway. Harry could not see them from the fire, but he could read them. Lupin. Tonks. Moody, suspicious of the fire itself.

"Remus," said Sirius, without looking round. "Get word to Albus. Wherever he is. Now. The phoenix, the patronus, I don't care. Tell him tonight, the Department of Mysteries, the arch. Tell him Tom's there and he's going to put himself back together and he wants Harry for the last piece." His voice was very level. "Tonks. Kingsley's on duty at the Ministry tonight. Get to him. Mad-Eye, everybody you can reach. Everybody."

"Sirius," said Lupin, from the doorway. "He's a boy. He's in Scotland. Whatever he thinks he's felt…"

"He's felt it," said Sirius. "I'd bet my life on it. I'm about to."

* * *

"I'm coming," said Harry.

There was a silence in the kitchen at Grimmauld Place. Harry read it, from a pink office four hundred miles away. He read Lupin's fear go up, and Moody's suspicion turn on Harry, and Tonks go very quiet, and Sirius not move at all.

"No," said Lupin.

"I'm the only one who can feel it," said Harry. "I'm the only one who'll know when it starts. When he starts calling them. I can feel the five from here. I'll be able to feel when he reaches for them. You won't. None of you will. You'll be standing in a room with an arch in it and you won't know if you've got an hour or a minute."

"Harry, he wants you *there*…"

"I know what he wants. I know better than anybody what he wants." Harry kept his voice very low. "He's going to try to take it whether I'm there or not. I can feel it in me. It's pointing at London. If I'm four hundred miles away when he pulls, I don't know what happens. Nobody knows. I'd rather be in the room."

"It's not your decision to make," said Moody.

"It's not yours either," said Harry.

There was another silence.

Then Sirius spoke. He did not turn round. He was looking at the fire, at Harry's face in it, and his voice was quite calm.

"If anybody in this kitchen tries to leave him behind," said Sirius, "I'll go with him. I'll walk out of that door and go with him, and you'll have to stop us both. Remus. You know I mean it."

Harry read him mean it.

He read Lupin read it too.

"Who's coming with you?" said Lupin at last. His voice was very tired. "Harry. You're not walking in there with nobody."

"I'll bring who I need," said Harry. "You get the Order there. All of it. Get them in before us. Meet us in the Atrium."

"How are you going to get to London?"

Harry thought about it.

He thought about a fireplace in a small dark room that smelled of goats. A fire that had been on the back wall for forty years for reasons nobody would give. A barman who had sent for his brother even though he hated him.

"The Hog's Head," he said.

"Aberforth won't…"

"Tell him Moody sent me," said Harry. "Tell him it's for his brother. He'll grumble. He'll let us through."

He read Moody, behind Sirius, in the doorway, look at him with his real eye and his magical one, both at once, for a long moment.

"He will," said Moody. "Damn you. He will."

* * *

Evelyn was in the library.

She was at the back table, by the Restricted Section, with *A History of the Wizengamot* open in front of her, and she looked up when he came round the end of the shelf, and she looked at his face, and she shut the book.

"Tonight," he said.

"Where?"

"The Ministry. The Department of Mysteries. The arch." He sat down across from her. His hands were not steady. He put them flat on the table. "He's there. He's got five of them in a ring. He's going to call them back into himself. And something in me…"

"Is the sixth," said Evelyn.

He looked at her.

"You worked it out," he said.

"In February. In the Room. When you told me something in you wanted it back." She did not move. "I didn't say anything because I didn't know what to say, and because Dumbledore didn't say anything either, and he knew. You told me he was holding something. That was what he was holding." She folded her hands on top of the book. "Who else is going?"

"You," said Harry. "If you'll come."

"Obviously I'll come."

"Millicent. Neville. Luna. Ron and Hermione." He looked at the table. "Not Theo."

"Why not Theo?"

"His father will be there."

Evelyn did not say anything for a moment.

"Have you asked him?"

"I'm going to tell him not to come."

"That isn't the same thing."

"No," said Harry. "I know."

* * *

He told Theo in the dormitory, at ten to six, with the curtains drawn round Theo's four-poster and both of them sitting inside, on the bed, the way they had sat in June.

Theo listened. He did not say anything until Harry had finished.

Then he said, "My father."

"Yes."

"You can feel him."

"I can feel a great many of them. I can't tell which. But he was in the graveyard, and he'll be there, and I won't have you in a room with him with your wand out." Harry looked at him. "I won't have you have to choose."

Theo looked at the green curtain.

Harry read him. He read the calm settled sadness at the bottom of him, the water in the well, and he read the boy at the edge of a drop in the dark that he had read in June, and he read something else, new, and it was relief, and Theo was ashamed of it.

"I should come," said Theo.

"You should stay," said Harry. "And if it goes wrong, you should be here. Somebody should be here who knows. Who can tell McGonagall. Who can tell Evelyn's mother, if…"

He stopped.

Theo looked at him for a long time.

"All right," he said.

He did not say anything else. He reached into his robes and took out the travelling chess set, the one with the pegged pieces, and opened it on the bed between them, and set up the white pieces, slowly, one by one, and then the black, and pushed the white king's pawn forward two squares.

"For when you get back," said Theo.

Harry looked at the board.

He did not move a piece. He got off the bed and went out through the curtain.

* * *

They went through the passage behind the humpbacked witch at half past six, seven of them, under the Cloak in two trips and by the Map, and came up in the cellar of Honeydukes, and went down the dark high street of Hogsmeade in a line in the evening, past the post office with its row of owls on the roof, and into the Hog's Head.

Aberforth was behind the bar.

He looked at them come in. Seven children in school robes, at seven o'clock on a Tuesday, in exam week. Harry read him. Irritation, very large. Under it, the old bitterness, which had come up all at once, aimed at somebody far off.

"Moody," said Aberforth.

"Yes."

"He said there'd be one. He didn't say seven." Aberforth looked along them. Neville. Luna. Millicent. Ron. Hermione. Evelyn, last, standing a little apart with her bag over her shoulder. He looked at Evelyn longest, and Harry did not know why, and could not read why, because the thing that came up in Aberforth looking at her was so old and so tangled that it had no name Harry knew.

Then he looked at Harry.

"You're the one," he said. "Potter. The one who nearly called me by my name in October."

"Yes."

"How'd you know it?"

Harry thought about bread and hard cheese and goat's milk. A straw mattress. *Well. That's new.*

"Somebody told me," he said. "A long time ago."

Aberforth looked at him a moment longer. Harry read the irritation and the bitterness and, very deep, under both, gruff and buried and old, the thing that had made him take two children in from the dark in another world.

It was aimed at them. All seven.

"Back room," said Aberforth. "Fire's on the wall. Goes to the visitors' end. Don't touch the goat." He turned back to his glass. "And tell my brother, if you see him, that I'm too old for this."

* * *

The fire took them one at a time.

Harry went last. He stood in the small back room that smelled of goats, with the fire green in front of him, and he read the six already gone, faint, very far away, in the south. Six silences and noises. Millicent's fear, small and fierce and held down hard. Neville's, enormous and not stopping him. Luna's calm. Ron's terror for Hermione and Hermione's for Ron. And Evelyn's silence, which he could not read at all, and which he could feel anyway, because there was a place in the south where the noise of six people had a hole in it exactly her shape.

He read further.

He read the Ministry, which was very big and very empty at seven o'clock on a summer evening. A guard at a desk. A few people working late. The Order, coming in through different doors, frightened and quick. Sirius among them. Tonks among them. Kingsley already there.

And under the Ministry, a long way down, a ring of five hungry things on stone steps, and a man in the middle of them, waiting.

The cold thing in Harry was pointing straight at him.

Harry stepped into the fire.

* * *

The Atrium was empty.

It was the long gold hall with the peacock-blue ceiling, and the fountain in the middle of it with its wizard and witch and centaur and goblin and house-elf, and the fireplaces in their rows down both walls, cold. The light was the dim gold light of a building after hours. The others were waiting by the fountain, very close together. Evelyn was at the edge of them. She had her watch out.

"Seven minutes past seven," she said.

There was nobody at the security desk. There was nobody at the golden gates. They went through them into the hall with the lifts, and Harry pressed the button, and one came, rattling, and its grille slid back, and they got in, all seven, very close.

A cool female voice said, *Department of Mysteries*, before anybody had pressed anything.

The grille slid shut.

Harry read the building go up past them as they went down. Level after level, nearly empty. Then nothing. Then, a long way down, the thing he had felt through the scar since Friday, very close now, very cold, and enormous, and patient.

It knew they were coming. He could feel it know.

He felt it turn, not its head, something much further back than a head, and look up the lift shaft towards him.

The lift went down.
