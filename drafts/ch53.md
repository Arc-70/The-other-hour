## Chapter 53: The Vision

The Defence Against the Dark Arts practical examination was held in the Great Hall at two o'clock on the second Wednesday of June, and the examiner who called Harry's name was a tiny, stooped witch so old that her face looked as though it had been folded up and put away in a drawer for a century.

"Potter," she said. "Harry Potter? Well, well."

He went up.

She was Professor Griselda Marchbanks, the head of the Wizarding Examinations Authority, and she had been giving the Defence practical, the other students said, since before Dumbledore was born. She was very deaf. She had an ear trumpet and she did not use it. She looked at Harry over the top of a pair of tiny spectacles with an expression of great and private interest.

"I've heard about you," she said. "Counter-jinx, please. Then a Banishing Charm. Then whatever you like."

He did the counter-jinx. He did the Banishing Charm, and the cushion she had put on the table went across the Hall and hit the far wall so hard that a Ravenclaw at the next table shrieked.

"Hm," said Professor Marchbanks. "Boggart."

There was one in a wardrobe at the end of the Hall. It came out at him as a dead boy in the grass with his eyes open, and Harry looked at it and thought about a jam jar, and it turned into a beetle in a jar, banging its head on the lid, and Professor Marchbanks made a small dry noise that might have been a laugh.

"Something extra," she said. "If you've got it."

Harry raised his wand.

He did not think about the Patronus. He did not reach for anything. He had been teaching it every Wednesday since January, and he had made it in a dark alley in Surrey with his mother screaming in his head, and on a lake with a hundred and fifty Dementors coming over the water. He knew where it was in him. He took hold of the thing he always took hold of now, a rock and a girl in a grey cardigan saying *I'd like that noted*, and made it larger, and let it go.

The stag came out of the end of his wand and filled the Great Hall.

It did not mean to. Harry did not mean it to. It came out larger than he had ever made it, larger than the one in the alley or the one on the lake, and it went up into the enchanted ceiling and its antlers went into the clouds, and it turned, very slowly, in the empty space over the four long tables, and every examiner in the Hall stopped what they were doing and looked up.

So did every student.

Harry read the Hall look. No crowd noise came out of it. Something quieter came instead: the feeling of three hundred people at once seeing something they had never seen and would remember.

The stag walked the length of the Hall, over their heads, and came back, and stood in front of Professor Marchbanks with its head lowered, looking at her.

Then it was gone.

Professor Marchbanks did not say anything for some time.

"I have been examining Defence," she said at last, "for a hundred and four years, Mr Potter. I have seen one Patronus like that before."

Harry waited.

"He was seventeen," said Professor Marchbanks. "He did things with a wand I'd never seen. He went on to do a great many more." She wrote something on her parchment. "Thank you, Mr Potter. That will be all."

He went back to his seat.

He did not need to ask who. He could feel the answer in her as he went, faint and old and fond, and it had a long beard even then.

* * *

Umbridge sent for him at four.

He had known she would. He had read her across the Hall during the practical, at the far end of the staff table, where she sat every day now with her clipboard and her cardigan and her little bow. He had felt her watching the stag. And he had felt what came up in her, under the cold hard thing with his name in it, at the sight of a boy who could do that, in her school, in front of the Examinations Authority, after everything.

She was afraid.

It was small. It was very small and very new, and she did not know she was feeling it, and it made the cold hard thing on top of it colder and harder still.

Filch fetched him. Filch was delighted. Filch had been delighted for two months, since the Head's office had gone to a woman who had given him back permission to use the old chains in his office, and he walked Harry up three flights of stairs to the pink office with his hand on Harry's shoulder the whole way.

She was alone.

"Shut the door, Argus," she said.

Filch shut it, from the outside.

* * *

"You will tell me," said Umbridge, "where Albus Dumbledore is."

She had her wand out. She had not had it out before, in this office, in all the times he had been here. She was holding it quite loosely, across her lap, the way a woman holds knitting.

"I don't know where he is, Headmistress."

"You don't know."

"No."

"You are his favourite. You have been his favourite for five years. You were at the top of his little list. He told you to *keep them safe*." She smiled. "I heard him."

"He didn't tell me where he was going."

Harry read her.

He did not have to go down very far. It was all at the top now. It had all come up, since the stag. There was the cold hard thing with his name in it, and the small new fear under it, and under the fear, sweet and steady and gentle, the enjoyment. It was not general any more. It had not been general since the fireworks. It was aimed at him.

And there was something else.

It was a decision. She had made it this afternoon, watching the stag go through the clouds. It was quite small and quite neat and it was folded away very tidily at the back of her, the way she folded the pink napkins round her biscuits, and Harry read what it was, and went still.

She was going to use the Cruciatus Curse.

She had thought about it, carefully, over two months. She had decided the Ministry would not mind. She had decided that the Minister did not need to know. And she had decided, this afternoon, at the sight of the stag, that it would be today.

"I'll show you," said Harry.

Umbridge blinked.

"You'll…"

"I'll show you where he left it." Harry looked at his hands. "He left something. Before he went. In case we needed it. I know where it is. I'll show you, if you promise not to… if you promise." He looked up at her, and let his voice go small, and let his face go as frightened as a fifteen-year-old's face should go, in a pink office, alone, with the door shut. "It's in the forest."

He read her take it.

It went into her like the tea, like the biscuits, like the second floor and the Ravenclaws. It went in smoothly and it settled, and the decision at the back of her, folded so neatly, did not go away. But it moved. It moved over, a little, to make room for something she wanted more.

"What kind of something?" she said.

"A weapon," said Harry.

* * *

They went down through the grounds at half past four, in the sun, the two of them. Umbridge walked a little behind him with her wand at his back. Nobody saw them go. The whole school was in the castle, in the common rooms and the library, revising for the next day's exams, and the lawns were empty and green and the lake was very still.

They went into the forest by the path past Hagrid's hut. Hagrid was not there. Hagrid had not been there since April. Harry did not know where he was.

The trees closed over them.

It was dim under the trees, and green, and warm, and there was a smell of leaf mould and something sweeter, a long way off, that might have been honeysuckle. Umbridge did not like it. Harry read her not like it. Every step they took into the forest, the small new fear in her grew a little, and the cold hard thing grew to cover it, and her wand pressed a little harder into his back.

"How much further?"

"Not far."

He knew where he was going. He had known since the pink office. He had been into the forest four times in five years, and on one of those times, in his first year, with Hagrid and a dead unicorn, he had stood at the edge of a clearing in the deep part of the forest and felt something he had never felt before or since. It had been a great many minds at once, very old, very proud, very still, watching from the trees. They had not been human. They had not been quite animal either.

He walked towards them.

He felt them before he saw anything. He felt them come, at the edge of what he could reach, a long way ahead, the same as in his first year. Proud. Watchful. Very old. And, this year, angry. There was an anger in them that had not been there five years ago, aimed outwards at the castle, at the Ministry, at every human who had ever come into this forest and told them where they might and might not go.

He walked faster.

"Potter," said Umbridge. "Potter, slow down."

He did not slow down.

He went on until the trees opened, and there was a clearing, wide and dim and quiet, with a great old oak at the far side of it, and he stopped.

He did it then.

* * *

It was not difficult. That was the thing he would remember. It was not difficult at all.

He reached for her mind the way he had reached for Tonks's, in the dining room at Grimmauld Place, to put an apple on the table. He found it at once, small and pink and certain, and very busy, and he laid something over it. He did it gently. He did not need to do it hard.

He put Dumbledore in the clearing.

He put him under the great oak at the far side, standing quite still, in his long blue robes, with his hands behind his back and his beard silver in the green light, looking at Umbridge over the top of his half-moon spectacles with an expression of mild and kindly interest. He put him exactly as Harry had last seen him, by a window in a pink office, telling the Minister for Magic about his army.

He did not put anything else in the clearing.

The clearing was full. It had been full since a moment before Harry stepped into it. There were forty of them, at least, at the edges of it, between the trees, with their bows drawn and their faces hard, and they had been watching two humans come blundering into the oldest part of their forest, and they had been deciding what to do.

Harry took them out of her eyes.

All of them. Every one. He laid the empty clearing over them, and Dumbledore under the oak, and nothing else.

Umbridge saw Dumbledore.

He read her see him. The fear went down, all at once, and something came up in its place that was very fierce and very sweet and very glad, and she pushed past Harry into the clearing with her wand out.

"*Dumbledore!*" she said.

The illusion under the oak looked at her, kindly.

"I knew it," said Umbridge. "I *knew* it. Hiding in a forest like a, like a half-breed, with the animals. I always knew. Albus Dumbledore, you are under arrest by order of the Ministry of Magic and the Headmistress of Hogwarts School, and if you so much as…"

She raised her wand.

She sent a spell at the illusion. It went through it, and through the oak, and into the trees behind, where there was a centaur. Harry heard it hit. He heard the centaur cry out, short and furious, and he heard forty bowstrings creak at once.

Umbridge did not hear them. She could not. He had taken them out of her ears as well.

"Filthy," she said, to Dumbledore, to the empty clearing, to the trees full of drawn bows. "Filthy, filthy half-breeds and beasts and Muggle-lovers, the whole forest of you, you'll all be…"

The first centaur came out of the trees.

Harry let go.

* * *

He let go of all of it at once. Dumbledore went. The empty clearing went. And Dolores Umbridge, standing in the middle of the oldest clearing in the Forbidden Forest with her wand raised and her mouth open on the word *beasts*, saw what she was standing in the middle of.

Forty centaurs.

They were all round her. They were very close. They were enormous, much larger than Harry had remembered from his first year, chestnut and black and grey and palomino, with their bows drawn and their faces like rock. One of them, a black one with a wild black beard, was holding his arm where her spell had hit him, and the arm was bleeding.

Harry read her.

He made himself. He stood at the edge of the clearing where she had left him and read her, all of it, and did not look away.

It was terror.

It came up in her so fast and so large that there was nothing else. The sweetness was gone. The certainty was gone. The cold hard thing with his name in it was gone, and the small neat decision folded at the back of her was gone, and the enjoyment, the gentle steady enjoyment of watching somebody else be made smaller, was gone as though it had never been there.

There was only terror.

It was the purest terror he had ever read in anybody. It was purer than Dudley's in the alley. It was purer than Fudge's on the night in June. It was the terror of a person who has believed her whole life that she is at the top of a ladder, and has just looked down, and found that the ladder is standing in the sea.

"Get away," said Umbridge. Her voice was very high. "Get away from me. I am a Senior Undersecretary… I am the Headmistress… you are *beasts*, you are filthy *half*…"

They took her.

Harry did not watch how. He heard her scream. He heard it go away into the trees, very fast, and get smaller, and stop.

He stood at the edge of the clearing.

He waited to feel sorry.

He waited quite a long time. He stood there in the green light with the honeysuckle smell coming and going, and he thought about her. He thought about the quill in her drawer, and Neville's hand under the Gryffindor table, and the words on the back of his own. He thought about the pink napkins, and the three sugars, and the hurt in her when she found out he had lied, which had been real.

He thought about the decision folded at the back of her.

He did not feel sorry.

He looked for it. He went down into himself, the way he went down into other people, floor by floor, and looked for it, the way you look for a coin you are sure you put in a pocket.

It was not there.

* * *

There was one centaur left in the clearing.

It was the black one, with the beard, holding his bleeding arm. He was standing under the oak, exactly where Harry had put Dumbledore, and he was looking at Harry.

Harry read him.

He was angry. It was very old and very proud and it was aimed at Harry. And under it there was something else, cold and clear and very steady, the feeling of somebody who has understood exactly what has just been done.

"You made her see something, foal," said the centaur.

"Yes."

"You walked her here. Into us. And you made her see something that was not there, and not see us, who were."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"She was going to hurt me," said Harry. "She'd decided. This afternoon."

The centaur looked at him for a long moment.

"Bane," he said. "My name is Bane." He took his hand away from his arm and looked at the blood on it. "We do not harm foals. That is our law. It is an old law, older than your castle. So you will go back to your castle, Harry Potter, and nobody here will stop you." He looked up. "But hear this. We do not like to be used. Not by her kind, who think us beasts. And not by yours."

"What's my kind?"

Bane looked at him.

He did not answer. He turned, and went into the trees, and Harry stood alone in the clearing for some time before he found the path back.

* * *

The History of Magic examination was on the Friday, at two o'clock, in the Great Hall.

Harry had not slept on Thursday night. He had lain in his four-poster and felt the thing coming in through his scar, the preparation, steady and slow and enormous, the arranging of things on a table, the moving of one of them a quarter of an inch to the left. It had been there every night for six weeks. It had been louder every night.

On Thursday night it had been very loud.

He sat in the Great Hall on Friday afternoon with the June sun coming in through the high windows and the scratching of a hundred quills all round him, and he wrote about the goblin rebellions of the eighteenth century. He wrote about the International Statute of Secrecy and the year it was signed, which was sixteen eighty-nine, and the year it came into force, which was sixteen ninety-two, which Evelyn had made him say out loud eleven times on the train in September.

The sun was warm on the back of his neck.

He put his head down on his arm for a moment.

* * *

He was in a corridor.

He knew it. He had been down it twice in his life, once in another world with a stolen watch, and once in this one, on his way to a courtroom, past a plain black door. He was not in his own body. He was walking very fast, and he was taller than himself, and the hands at his sides were long and white.

He went through the black door. He went through a round room full of doors, which turned. He went into a long high room full of shelves, and the shelves were full of small glass balls, glowing faintly, thousands and thousands of them, going away into the dark.

There was a man on the floor at the end of an aisle.

He was lying on his back with his arms out. His face was thin and bearded and frightened, and Harry knew it. Harry knew it better than he knew his own. He had known it as a dog, in a cave, and as a man in a tunnel, and in a kitchen in Grimmauld Place singing "Good King Wenceslas" with tinsel round his neck.

"Get it for me," said the high cold voice that was Harry's mouth. "Take it down. Bring it to me."

"I won't," said Sirius. "I'll never…"

"Then you will die. But first you will scream."

The long white hand raised a wand.

Sirius screamed.

* * *

Harry did not fall off his chair.

He did not shout. He did not jump up. He lifted his head off his arm very slowly, and the Great Hall was there, with the sun and the scratching quills and Professor Tofty walking slowly down the aisle between the desks with his hands behind his back.

He had seen this.

He had seen it from the outside, in another world. He had stood in the Room of Requirement with Evelyn under a borrowed face and heard the other Harry tell the others about it. He had followed them to the Ministry. He had been in that room, the room of glass balls, and seen it was empty. He had watched a man fall through a curtain in a stone archway that did not move without any wind.

It was a trap.

He knew it as plainly as he knew the date of the Statute of Secrecy. It was the same trap. The same dream, the same room, the same man on the floor. It had been made for the other Harry and it had worked, and it had been made for him, here, now, and it was not going to.

He picked up his quill.

He did not write. He sat very still, with the quill in his hand over the parchment, and the sun on the back of his neck, and he went back.

He went back along the thread.

He did not go far. He had promised himself he would never go out again, and he did not go out. He only stood at the very end of himself, where the thread began, and listened. He listened the way he listened to a house through a wall, to see what kind of day it was.

The dream had been made to be seen. He could feel the shape of it, now he was looking. It had been made carefully, the way the illusions he made for Umbridge were made, with a beginning and an end and nothing on the other side. It was a picture somebody had put in front of his eyes.

But behind the picture, there was something else.

There was the preparation.

It had been coming in on him for six weeks, slow and steady, a table being laid. Now it was neither slow nor steady. It was coming in on him so loudly that he could hardly hear the quills. And he understood, sitting in the Great Hall with the June sun on his neck, that it had not been aimed at Sirius. It had never been aimed at Sirius.

It was aimed at a place.

A round room, very deep, very still. Stone steps going down on every side, like the inside of a bowl. And in the middle, on a raised dais, an archway, old and cracked, with a black curtain hanging in it that moved, very slightly, without any wind.

And on the steps around it, laid out in a particular order, a quarter of an inch to the left, were things.

He could not see them. He could feel them, very faintly, at the far end of the thread, the way he had felt the diadem in a cathedral of hidden things. They were hungry. Every one of them. A small cold hunger, and another, and another, all round the bottom of the bowl, all waiting.

Five of them.

And at the centre, where the dais was, the cold itself, very large, standing beside the archway with its long white hands at its sides, waiting for something.

It was waiting for the sixth.

Harry knew that the way he knew his name. He did not know how he knew it. He knew it the way the compass needle in him had known the diadem, a small cold certainty at the bottom of everything, turning, pulling, all in one direction.

It was tonight.

* * *

He finished the paper.

He did not know afterwards why he had done that. He wrote two more paragraphs on the goblin rebellions and a short answer on the Statute of Secrecy, and he put down his quill when Professor Tofty said quills down, and he walked out of the Great Hall at four o'clock with everybody else, and nobody looked at him twice.

Evelyn was waiting at the bottom of the marble staircase.

She was not taking any exams. She was in sixth year. She had no reason to be there. She was standing with her bag over her shoulder and her hand in her right-hand cardigan pocket, where the watch was, and she looked at his face and said, "What?"

"It's tonight," said Harry.

She did not ask what. She looked at him for a moment more. Then she turned round and started up the stairs.

"Where are you going?"

"Her office," said Evelyn over her shoulder. "It's the only fireplace in the castle the Ministry isn't watching. And she's not using it."

* * *

The pink office was empty.

Nobody had been into it since Wednesday. The kittens on the plates had got bored and gone to sleep. There was a cup of cold tea on the desk with three sugars in it, and a biscuit on a saucer beside it, and the fire was out.

Evelyn locked the door behind them with a word that was not *Colloportus*, and stood with her back to it.

Harry knelt in front of the fire. He lit it. He took a pinch of Floo powder from the pot on the mantelpiece, a pink pot, and threw it in, and the flames went green and high.

"Twelve Grimmauld Place," he said, and put his head in.

* * *

The kitchen at Grimmauld Place came spinning into view, the long table and the iron range and the ceiling, and somebody was sitting at the table with a newspaper.

"Harry?"

It was Sirius.

He was there. He was sitting at the kitchen table in a dressing gown with a cup of tea and the *Evening Prophet* and a plate of toast, and he was alive, and he was not on the floor of anything, and he was not screaming. He put down the toast and stared at Harry's head in the fire.

"Harry. What's happened? What are you…"

"Are you all right?"

"Am I… yes. Yes, I'm fine. What's…"

"You haven't been out. You haven't been to the Ministry."

"I haven't been *anywhere*. I never go anywhere. Harry, what's…"

Harry read him.

He could not read him well, through the fire. It was like reading somebody through a thick window. But he could feel enough. He could feel the alarm come up, and the love under it, loud as ever, and under that, nothing. No pain. No fear for himself. He had been sitting at the table eating toast.

Harry let out a long breath.

"Listen to me," he said. "I need you to listen to me and not argue. Is anyone else there?"

"Kreacher. Upstairs. Somewhere. And Remus is coming at six."

"Get Remus now. Get Moody and Kingsley and Tonks. Get everybody you can. And get a message to Dumbledore. Wherever he is. However you do it."

"Harry…"

"Voldemort's at the Ministry," said Harry. "Tonight. He's in the Department of Mysteries. In the room with the archway. He showed me you, being tortured there, so I'd come. It's a trap. I know it's a trap. I've seen it before." He heard his own voice and did not stop. "But there's something else. He's not just waiting for me. He's got things with him. Five of them. He's laid them out. He's going to do something. Tonight. I can feel it."

Sirius looked at him through the green fire.

Harry read him go very still.

"What sort of something?"

"I'll tell you when they're there," said Harry. "Get them."

* * *

It took twenty-five minutes.

He knelt on the floor of the pink office with his head in the fire and his knees going numb, and Evelyn stood with her back against the locked door and counted, out loud, every five minutes, so that he would know. In the kitchen at Grimmauld Place, people came. Lupin first, through the front door, with his face grey. Then Tonks, with her hair mousy brown and flat, which Harry had never seen, which meant she had come straight from somewhere she had needed not to be noticed. Then Moody. Then Kingsley, last, very calm, from the Ministry, where he had been on duty.

Harry told them.

He told them all of it. The preparation, six weeks long. The round room, the archway, the steps. The hungry things on the steps, five of them, in an order. The ring, the locket, the cup, the diadem, the snake. He did not have to explain what they were. Kingsley and Moody already knew. Harry read them know, and read that Dumbledore had told them, and that Dumbledore had not told the others.

"It's tonight," he said. "He's going to put himself back together. He's waiting for the sixth piece."

"And where's the sixth?" said Moody.

Harry did not answer.

He did not know the answer. He knew that he did not know it. He could feel the compass needle at the bottom of him, small and cold, turning, and he did not know what it was turning towards, and he did not say anything about it, and nobody in the kitchen noticed that he had not.

"Right," said Moody. "We go. Now. Kingsley, Tonks, Lupin, me. Black stays here."

"Black does *not* stay here," said Sirius.

"Black is a convicted murderer with a face on every wall in the Ministry…"

"Black is *going*."

"And I'm going," said Harry.

The kitchen went quiet.

* * *

"No," said Lupin.

"You can't stop me."

"I can stop you very easily, Harry. I can lock this fireplace from this end and you'll be standing in a pink office with soot on your face."

"Then I'll find another way," said Harry. "I'll fly. I'll walk. I'll get Hagrid's Thestrals." He looked at them, one after another, through the green flames. "You don't understand. I'm the only one who can feel it. When he starts. When the ritual begins. I'll know. I'm the only one who'll know. You'll be standing in a room full of doors with no idea which one, and I'll know."

"He's right," said Kingsley, quietly.

"He is *fifteen*," said Lupin.

"He's right anyway."

"Remus," said Tonks. "Remus. He's been in there. In the other… he knows the way. He knows the rooms."

Lupin looked at her. Harry read him. He was frightened, very frightened, for Harry, and under the fright, the old guilt, aimed at Harry, which had been there since a classroom with a Boggart in the wardrobe and a stag in the moonlight. He was thinking about James. He was thinking about a night fifteen years ago in Godric's Hollow when nobody had been there in time.

"Moody," said Lupin. "Tell him."

"Tell him what?" said Moody. "That he can't come? The boy's got a direct line into You-Know-Who's skull. I'd be a fool to leave him in Scotland." His magical eye swivelled round to look at Harry through the fire. "But not alone. And not first."

"And if anybody," said Sirius, "tries to leave him behind in that school, I'll go and get him myself. On a broom. In broad daylight. With my face on every wall in the Ministry." He looked at Lupin. "I mean it, Moony. If he goes, I go with him. If he doesn't go, I go and fetch him."

There was a long silence.

Then Moody said, "Fine," in a voice like a door slamming.

* * *

He took his head out of the fire.

Evelyn was standing against the door with her watch in her hand. It was twenty to six.

"Well?" she said.

"Tonight. Eleven o'clock. The visitors' entrance. The Order's coming through the Atrium at a quarter past, so we don't all trip the alarms at once." He got up off his knees. They were numb. "They want people with me. Not too many. People who can fight."

"I'm coming," said Evelyn.

It was not a question. He had not asked. He looked at her.

"Of course you are," he said. "Somebody's got to count."

* * *

He chose them that evening, in the empty fourth-floor classroom, one at a time.

Millicent said "Yes," before he had finished, and then, "Where?" and then, "Good," and went to get her wand.

Neville went white. Then he went red. Then he said, "My parents. The woman who… is she going to be there?" and Harry said, "I think so," and Neville said, "Then I'm coming," and did not say anything else.

Luna said, "Oh, the Department of Mysteries. Daddy says they keep a brain in a tank there. I'd like to see that." Then she looked at Harry for rather longer than usual and said, "You're very frightened, aren't you? It's all right. So am I. I'll come."

Ron and Hermione came together.

They had been told by Neville, who had been told by Harry, and they came up the stairs to the fourth floor at half past seven, and Ron had his wand out already, and Hermione had a bag on her shoulder with books in it, because she always did.

"We're coming," said Ron.

"It's dangerous."

"We know."

"It's going to be worse than anything you've…"

"Harry," said Hermione. "We *know*. You don't have to do the speech." She looked at him. "You asked us to trust you. In August. On the stairs. You said you'd have to ask, one day." She shifted the bag on her shoulder. "This is the day, isn't it?"

"Yes."

"Well, then," said Hermione.

* * *

Theo did not come.

Harry went to find him last, in the dormitory, at eight o'clock. Theo was sitting on his bed with a chess problem on his knees and his back against the headboard, and he looked up when Harry came in, and Harry did not need to say anything.

"Tonight," said Theo.

"Yes."

"The Ministry."

"Yes."

Theo put the chess problem down.

He had come back from Easter very quiet. He had told Harry what his father said, on the first night back, in the gap between their beds, in a flat calm voice. His father had said that when Theo came of age, in a year and a half, he would be expected to choose. His father had said that he hoped Theo would choose well. His father had said that the Dark Lord was a patient master, and a generous one, to those who chose well.

"He'll be there," said Theo. "Won't he."

"I think so."

"Then I can't." Theo looked at his hands. "It's not that I'm frightened. I am frightened. That's not why." He was quiet for a moment. "If I'm in that room, and he's in that room, one of us is going to have to do something. And I don't know which of us it'll be. And I don't want to find out tonight."

Harry read him.

The calm sadness. The edge, very close. And under it, so quiet that Harry had to go a long way down to find it, a boy who had sat on the end of his mother's bed when he was seven, waiting for her to move, and who did not want to sit at the end of anybody else's.

"All right," said Harry.

"You're not going to argue?"

"No."

Theo looked at him for a long time.

"Come back," he said. "That's all. You lot. Come back."

* * *

They went through the Hog's Head.

It was Moody's idea. The Hog's Head had a fireplace that was not on any Ministry list, because its owner had taken it off the list himself thirty years ago and nobody had ever noticed. They went down to Hogsmeade at ten, in the dark, seven of them, by a passage behind a statue of a one-eyed witch on the third floor that Harry had found on a map in his third year.

The bar was closed. The door was not locked.

Aberforth was behind the bar, drying a glass with a cloth that made it dirtier. He looked at the seven of them coming in out of the dark, one after another, with their wands out, and Harry read him.

He was not surprised.

He had been told. Somebody had sent word, and Aberforth had been sitting here behind his bar in the dark waiting for seven children to come in from a passage in his cellar that he had pretended for thirty years he did not know about. He was angry. He was angry the way he was always angry, in a worn channel, at a brother. And under the anger, kept very carefully out of sight, there was the kindness, and it was aimed at them, at all seven of them, and it was very frightened.

"Fireplace is through the back," he said. "Floo's set for the Leaky. Tom knows you're coming. Don't touch anything on the way."

They went through the back.

Harry went last. He stopped at the bar.

Aberforth looked at him. The blue eyes were very sharp behind the dirty glasses.

"You," he said. "You're the one paid for twenty-six butterbeers in October and didn't leave a tip."

"Yes," said Harry.

"Nobody doesn't leave a tip." Aberforth put down the glass. "People leave tips. They think it buys them something. You didn't." He looked at Harry for a long moment, the way you look at somebody you think you might have met before and cannot place. "Why not?"

Harry thought about a room upstairs that smelled of goats, and soup, and a bed, and *my brother will use you, boy*.

"Somebody told me once you didn't like them," he said.

Aberforth went on looking at him.

"Nobody knows that," he said.

"No," agreed Harry. "Thank you for the fireplace."

He went through the back.

* * *

It was eight minutes to eleven when they came up out of the Underground into the shabby street of offices, and it was very quiet. A taxi went past at the far end. There was a pub on the corner with its lights off. There was a skip full of broken plasterboard outside one of the buildings, and a car with a flat tyre, and the red telephone box with most of its panes missing.

Seven of them would not fit.

They did it in two goes. Harry went in the first with Evelyn and Neville and Luna, squashed up against the glass, Neville's elbow in his ribs, Luna humming. He dialled. Six-two-four-four-two.

"Welcome to the Ministry of Magic," said the cool woman's voice. "Please state your name and business."

"Harry Potter," said Harry. "Neville Longbottom. Luna Lovegood. Evelyn Selwyn." He thought about it. "We're here to save somebody."

There was a short pause.

"Thank you," said the cool voice. "Visitors, please take the badges and attach them to the front of your robes."

Four badges slid out of the coin return. Harry picked his up and looked at it.

HARRY POTTER, it said. RESCUE MISSION.

Beside him, Evelyn made a small sound, very quiet, through her nose.

"Don't," said Harry.

"I didn't say anything."

"You were going to."

"I was going to say," said Evelyn, "that it's spelled correctly."

The floor of the telephone box shuddered.

They began to go down.

Harry stood with his back against the glass and his wand in his hand and Evelyn's shoulder against his arm, and the pavement rose up past the windows, and then the darkness, and he reached down through the floor of the box, through the Ministry, through all the floors of it and the lifts and the corridors and the plain black door, a long way down, to a round deep room with a curtain in it.

The cold was there.

It was very large. It was very still. It was standing at the bottom of the bowl with the five hungry things round it on the steps, and it was waiting, and it was not waiting for Sirius.

It knew he was coming.

He felt it know. He felt it turn, very slowly, at the bottom of the Ministry, the way it had turned once at the end of a thread in December. It turned towards the telephone box going down through the dark, and it was pleased.

The telephone box went on going down.
