## Chapter 73: Hogsmeade

The Hog's Head had not changed, because in this world nobody had ever given it a reason to.

Harry came in out of the sleet at half past six on the first Tuesday in February, in the face of a tired middle-aged wizard with a sack over his shoulder, and stood just inside the door and let the room come up round him. It was dark and low and filthy. The windows were so grimy that the light from the high street came through them brown. There was sawdust on the floor and a smell of goats, and four customers at four different tables, all hooded, none of them looking at any of the others. Behind the bar a tall old man with a long grey beard was wiping a glass with a cloth that was making it dirtier.

Harry knew him.

He had stood in this room at thirteen, in another world, with a girl beside him he could not read and a gold watch that had stopped, and a tall old man with a long grey beard had looked at the two of them over this bar and said *you'd better come upstairs, the pair of you, before somebody else notices you*, and had taken them up, and kept them for eight weeks. He had fed them. He had shouted at them. He had taught Harry to milk a goat called Mabel, and had looked at the watch in Evelyn's hand and gone quite white and refused to say why, and on the last night, in the room above the bar, he had said *my brother will use you, boy. He uses everyone he loves.*

This was not that man.

This was the same man, in the same bar, wiping the same glass, and he had never seen Harry in his life.

Harry crossed the sawdust to the bar and put the sack down on it.

"Potatoes," he said. "From Leeds. For the passage."

Aberforth Dumbledore looked at the sack. Then he looked at Harry, at the tired middle-aged wizard with the sack, and Harry read him.

Suspicion, on top. It was old and dry and permanent, like the dirt on the windows. Under it, much quieter, there was a weariness that went all the way down, the weariness of a man who has watched a great many people come into his bar and go out of it to die. And under that, at the very bottom, so faint that Harry nearly missed it, a kind of stubborn, grudging tenderness, aimed at nothing in particular, at the whole of the dark room, at the hooded people at the four tables and the goats in the yard and the children at the end of the passage behind the back room, who were not his, and whom he had decided, without telling anybody, to keep alive.

It was the same. It was exactly the same as the other one.

"Back room," said Aberforth.

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me. Take it through."

Harry picked up the sack. He did not move.

Aberforth's eyes narrowed. They were very blue. They were his brother's eyes, in a different face, a face that had been angry for a hundred years and had stopped noticing.

"What," he said.

"Nothing."

"You're looking at me."

"Sorry."

"You're looking at me," said Aberforth slowly, "like you know me. People don't look at me like that. They look at me like I'm the bloke behind the bar." He put the glass down. "Who are you?"

Harry very nearly said his name.

Not *Harry*. The other one. The name he had nearly said in a pub in another world at thirteen and had bitten back, and nearly said here at fifteen, ordering a butterbeer before the first meeting of a defence group, and bitten back again. He felt it come up now, quite easily, the way it always had, as though some part of him had been waiting since he was thirteen to say it to this face and be answered.

He did not say it.

"I met somebody very like you once," he said instead. "A long time ago. He kept goats."

Aberforth looked at him for a long moment.

"Lot of people keep goats," he said.

"Yes."

"Was he any good? The bloke you met?"

Harry thought about a room above a bar, and two children in it who did not belong in that world, and a man who had not had to take them in, and had.

"He was the best man I've ever met," said Harry.

Aberforth said nothing.

He picked the glass up again and went on wiping it with the dirty cloth, and Harry read something go through him, very quick, under the suspicion and the weariness. Not belief. Something more like embarrassment, aimed at himself, the embarrassment of an old man who has been told a kind thing by a stranger and does not know where to put it.

"Back room," said Aberforth gruffly, to the glass. "Go on."

* * *

Lockhart was in the back room, unpacking a crate of tinned peaches by candlelight and singing.

He was not in disguise. He was meant to be. He had told Harry so, on the radio, in January: *alive and well and in disguise, my dear fellows, somewhere with a very good view.* The disguise turned out to be a pair of spectacles with plain glass in them and a false moustache that he had not stuck on properly. Under it he was entirely Gilderoy Lockhart, in his sugared-apricot cloak with the gold at the cuffs and the hem, kneeling on the stone floor of Aberforth Dumbledore's back room with a tin of peaches in each hand.

"*Harry!*" he said. "My dear fellow. You've brought potatoes. Splendid. We're very short of potatoes. The young are eating nothing but peaches, which is delightful for them and very bad for their teeth." He stood up, beaming, and the moustache slipped. "Is that you under there? It is, isn't it? You've done something very clever with your nose."

"You're meant to be in disguise," said Harry.

"I *am* in disguise." Lockhart touched the moustache. "Nobody's recognised me for a week."

"Everybody in Hogsmeade's recognised you."

"Well, yes. But they're very discreet. They're on our side, you see. Rosmerta winks at me." He took the sack of potatoes from Harry and looked into it with great satisfaction. "Marvellous. Come through. Come and see."

There was a door at the far side of the back room, low and narrow, and behind it a passage went down into the dark. Harry knew where it went. It went under the lane and under the fields and up, a long way up, into a room on the seventh floor of the castle that was whatever you needed it to be. Neville Longbottom was living in that room, with thirty-one other students who had decided they would rather not go to lessons with the Carrows, and every week since Christmas the village had been feeding them through this door.

Lockhart had organised it.

He told Harry so, for twenty minutes, by candlelight, with a great many flourishes. He had a list of every family in Hogsmeade who had given anything, in purple ink on a roll of parchment three feet long. He had a rota. He had a system of signals involving the curtains at Madam Puddifoot's. He had made friends with the Carrows' house-elf, which nobody else had managed, by signing a photograph for her. He had, on two occasions, led a Death Eater patrol in a circle round the back of the village for an hour by walking ahead of them in the dark in the character of a confused old man looking for his cat.

"I was magnificent," said Lockhart. "I wish you'd seen it. I had a limp. A very small one. Subtle. Here." He walked three steps across the back room with a very small, subtle limp. "You see? The cat was called Mr Whiskers. I said it with great feeling. *Mr Whiskers*. One of them very nearly helped me look."

Harry laughed.

It came out of him short and surprised, and it was the first time in some weeks, and he noticed it as it went.

Lockhart noticed too. He stopped limping. He looked at Harry by the candlelight, with the moustache crooked and the plain glass of his spectacles catching the flame, and Harry read him, and found under the vanity and the warmth and the great bright pleasure in his own magnificence something quite different, steady and plain and aimed at Harry.

It was worry.

"You're very thin," said Lockhart.

"I'm fine."

"You're not, you know. You're very thin, and you've got a look about you." He tilted his head. "I've seen it before. On other people. In the Himalayas, once. A man I knew. He walked a Path too, you know. Not yours. A different one. He got that look at the end."

"What happened to him?"

Lockhart looked at him a moment longer.

Then he smiled, brilliantly, the old smile, and turned back to the crate of peaches.

"Oh, he was fine," he said. "Perfectly fine. Lived to a great age. Took up beekeeping." He held out a tin. "Have a peach, my dear fellow. Have two. You're far too thin."

Harry did not read whether that was a lie. He found that he did not want to.

He took the peaches.

"Gilderoy," he said, at the door.

"My dear fellow?"

"If they come. For the village. Because of this." He nodded at the passage, the crates, the purple list. "What will you do?"

Lockhart considered it. He took the false moustache off, finally, and looked at it on his fingertip, and put it in his pocket.

"Something memorable," he said. "Obviously."

"I mean it."

"So do I." Lockhart smiled at him. It was a smaller smile than usual, one that Harry had seen perhaps twice. "I've had a very good run, Harry. Better than most. I've been everywhere and met everybody and written it all down, slightly improved. If they come, I shall go and stand somewhere inconvenient and be extremely annoying for as long as I possibly can." He put a tin of peaches in Harry's other hand. "And you will hear all about it, because I shall make sure somebody tells you."

"That's not a plan."

"It's the only plan I've ever had," said Lockhart. "It's served me beautifully. Mind the step."


* * *

The silver badger came through the wall of the Majestic at thirteen minutes to three on the morning of Thursday the twenty-sixth of February.

Harry was at the screen. He had been at the screen since eleven. He was making Diagon Alley in the rain, with two hundred people in it, every one different, and he was holding it without effort, the way he held everything now, and he was thinking about nothing at all.

The badger walked through the left-hand wall by the side boxes and down the aisle to the orchestra pit, as the doe had in October, and stopped, and looked up at him.

It spoke in Neville Longbottom's voice.

It was not Neville's ordinary voice. Harry had heard that for six years, round and anxious and kind. This was the voice of somebody who had been shouting for some time and had stopped because there was no longer any point.

"They've come for the village," said the badger. "For feeding us. Forty of them. More. They're burning it. Lockhart's at the bridge. He's holding them so the families can get to the passage. He sent me to send this. He said tell Harry. Please. Harry. *Please*."

Then it was gone.

Harry let Diagon Alley go.

He stood below the screen in the dark for perhaps three seconds. He did not think. He reached up behind him, through the ceiling, to the projection room, and found Ron asleep and Hermione asleep, the slow warm untidy feelings of sleep, and in the far corner by the projectors, where Evelyn's camp bed was, the place in the building where there was nothing.

He did not wake them.

He would think about that, later. He would think about the three seconds below the screen, and the three people asleep upstairs, and how easy it had been not to. He did not think about it then. He went up the side aisle and through the foyer and out of the fire door into the alley, where the Apparition ward let you out, if not in, and turned on the spot.

* * *

He came out on the lane above Hogsmeade at twenty-four minutes past three, between two hedges that had been cut back hard in the autumn, and stood still.

It was beginning to snow.

The first flakes were coming down out of a sky he could not see, slowly, in no hurry, in air so still that they fell quite straight. One landed on the back of his hand. It did not melt at once.

Below him, the village was burning.

Not all of it. Most of the fire was out. But halfway along the high street the Three Broomsticks was still alight, a dull orange glow inside its broken windows with smoke going up from the roof in a thick grey column, straight up, not bending at all, because there was no wind. Further down there were other glows, smaller, going out. There was a smell of wet ash.

There was no noise.

He reached.

He reached down the lane and into the village, the whole of it, the way he had learned to read a building in the Ministry, and he found it.

At the top end, behind the Hog's Head, very far down, underground, there were people. A great many. Frightened, and grieving, and in pain, and alive. Forty, perhaps. More. They were in the passage. They had got there. They were going up it, slowly, in the dark, carrying each other.

In the yards behind the shops there were a few more. Hiding. Hurt.

In the high street, there were fourteen.

They were pleased.

He found that first, before anything else, standing on the lane in the falling snow. Fourteen people in the high street, and every one of them was pleased. Nothing like the cold pleasure of the churchyard or the lawn. Hotter and louder and much more stupid. It was the pleasure of men who had done a thing they had been told to do and enjoyed doing it more than they had expected, and were now standing about in the wreck of it, drinking somebody else's mead out of the bottle, and laughing, and kicking things.

And between them and the passage, at the bottom of the high street, on the bridge, there was nothing at all.

The place where a person had been.

He went down the lane.

* * *

He went down past the houses. He did not count the windows. He would learn, later, that there were eleven broken on the left and four on the right, because somebody told him. He did not see them. He did not see the open door of Gladrags, or the broom on the pavement outside the apothecary, or the black cat on the Honeydukes sill. He went down the lane to the bottom of the high street, to the bridge, in the falling snow.

Lockhart was lying on it.

He was on his back. His wand was a yard from his right hand, in the gutter of the bridge, against the parapet. His right hand was lying palm up on the stones beside him, open, the fingers a little curled, as though he were waiting to be handed something. The spectacles with the plain glass were gone. The false moustache was gone. His face was quite calm. His eyes were open and looking up at the snow coming down out of the dark, and a flake landed on one of them, and it did not blink.

He was wearing the cloak.

Of course he was. Sugared apricot, with gold at the cuffs and the hem. Madam Malkin had made it for him specially. It was a colour that nobody would ever have chosen to wear to a war. It was spread out round him on the stones of the bridge like a flag, the most absurd colour Harry had ever seen anybody die in, and the snow was beginning to settle on it.

Harry stood on the bridge and looked at the road on the village side.

The scorch marks went across it in a wide half circle. Dozens of them, hundreds, black stars and long black streaks, burnt down into the cobbles. Every spell that had been thrown down the high street at the bridge for however long it had been, an hour, two hours, and every one of them had stopped at the same place. At the near end of the bridge. In a line as straight as the edge of a ruler.

Nothing had got past it.

Not one spell had crossed that line, while the man in the sugared-apricot cloak stood behind it with his wand up, holding a bridge alone, and forty people went past behind him, up the stream and through the yards, carrying their children, into the passage, into the dark.

Harry looked at the line.

He thought about a crate of tinned peaches.

He thought about a limp, very small, very subtle. *Mr Whiskers*. He thought about a tunnel under the school, a long time ago, when he was twelve, collapsing, and a man in a ridiculous cloak holding it up with a spell so that a boy could go on, and staying there, holding it, until his back broke.

He thought about *you're very thin*.

He did not feel anything.

He noticed that. He noticed it standing on the bridge with the snow coming down, quite clearly, as a fact. He had stood by Cedric in a churchyard and by Dumbledore on a lawn and he had felt them go, the place where a person had been, and something had happened in him each time, something large, something he had not been able to turn down. He had not felt Sirius go. He had been running. He had felt the laughter stop and kept running and had not stopped since.

He did not feel anything now.

He looked inside himself for it, carefully, as he had on a bench at Shell Cottage, and it was not there.

And he thought, quite calmly, standing on the bridge, that that was right.

It was a very simple thought. It did not come with any feeling of its own. It came the way a sum comes out, when you have done all the working. There was a war. In a war people died, and the ones who were left had to do things. Feeling things was what you did afterwards, if there was an afterwards. It was a luxury. It was for people who had time. It was for people who were not at war. And he was at war, and he had been at war since an orchard in August, and he had been wasting a great deal of time.

He stepped round Lockhart's open hand without touching it, and went up into the high street.

* * *
They saw him at once.

He did not hide. He walked up the middle of the high street in his own face, in the snow, with his hands in his pockets, and the fourteen men in black stood about in the road and on the pavements in the light of the burning inn and watched him come.

He read them as he walked.

He read them one at a time, slowly, the way he read the people in Diagon Alley on the screen, to get them right.

The big one nearest the bridge, with the beard, was the most pleased. He had a bottle of Rosmerta's oak-matured mead in one hand and his mask pushed up on his forehead, and he was pleased because he had been the one to set the Three Broomsticks alight, and he had enjoyed watching the woman inside try to put it out with her hands. The one beside him, thin, with a long neck, was pleased in a fussier way, pleased that it was done and that it had been done properly and that somebody would tell the Dark Lord so. The two who had been kicking a handcart over by the kerb were pleased the way boys are pleased after a fight. The one in the doorway of the bookshop was pleased and bored, and had been thinking about going in to see if there was anything worth taking.

The youngest was standing in the gutter outside Scrivenshaft's. He was about Evelyn's age. He was pleased too, but it was thinner than the others' and newer, and under it, a little way down, he was frightened of how pleased he was. It was the first time. He had killed a man on the stream bank an hour ago, an old man with a lantern, and it had been easy, and he had not expected it to be easy, and he could not stop thinking about how easy it had been.

Harry knew that feeling.

He had read it in a cousin in a play park, two and a half years ago, from a gate. He had read it in himself, more recently, in the front row of a dead cinema. He put it with the rest.

"Well, look at *this*," said the big one with the beard.

He said it with great delight. He lifted his bottle. He was the first to see the scar, and Harry felt the recognition go through him, and then through the others, one after another, like a match going along a row of candles, and every one of them got more pleased.

"Potter," said the thin one with the long neck. "That's Potter. That's *Harry Potter*."

"Come to see the bonfire, have you, Potter?"

"Bit late, son. Party's over."

"Your mate on the bridge put up a good show, though. Didn't he? We'll give him that. Funny little man. Wouldn't shut up. Kept *talking*." The big one laughed. "Kept saying *come on, gentlemen, is that the best you've got*, right to the end. In that bloody cloak."

Several of them laughed.

Harry stopped in the middle of the high street, about level with the post office, and stood in the snow and looked at them.

They had their wands out now. Most of them. Not hurrying. There were fourteen of them and one of him, and he was seventeen and very thin and had his hands in his pockets, and they were pleased, and they were in no hurry at all.

"Don't think the Dark Lord'll mind if we have a bit of fun first," said the big one. "Do you, Rowle?"

"He said alive," said the thin one.

"Alive's a broad church."

Harry read them all at once.

He did not need to reach. They were very close and very loud. He stood in the snow and let the fourteen of them come up into him, all fourteen pleasures, all fourteen sets of everything under the pleasures, and he went down through each one, quickly, the way you go down through the drawers in a desk when you are looking for one particular thing.

He was looking for the worst night.

Everybody had one. He had known that since he was six. Everybody he had ever read had one night somewhere in them that was worse than all the others, that they did not think about, that they had built everything else on top of so that they would never have to look at it again. It was always there. It was always at the bottom. It was always the same shape, in everybody, a door with something behind it that they had shut and walked away from and would give anything never to open.

He found it in all fourteen.

The big one with the beard had a night when he was nine, in a kitchen, with his father and a belt, and a younger brother under the table, and a choice. The thin one with the long neck had a night in a hospital, by a bed, with his mother in it, and a thing he had promised to do for her and had not done, and the door closing. The one in the bookshop doorway had a cellar. One of the boys by the handcart had a girl, and a lake, and ice. The youngest, in the gutter outside Scrivenshaft's, had an hour ago, and an old man with a lantern on the stream bank, and how easy it had been. It had already gone to the bottom of him. It had gone straight there, in an hour. He would carry it there for the rest of his life.

Harry thought about that.

He thought about it for perhaps a second, standing in the snow. He thought that it would be a long life, and that it would be a long time to carry it.

Then he opened the doors.

* * *

He did not do it the way he had done the illusions on the screen. He did not build anything. There was no need to build anything. It was all already there, in each of them, perfect, every detail, every word, every smell and sound, built by themselves, over years, with great care, in the dark at the bottom of their own minds.

He just let them in.

He put each of the fourteen men in the high street inside his own worst night, all of it, and made it real, and made it now.

And he did not put an end on it.

That was the only thing he built. It was very small. It was the smallest illusion he had ever made, and it was the one that mattered. A night has an end. Every worst night anybody has ever had has an end, a morning, a moment where it stops and you are on the other side of it and it is behind you. He took the end away. He took it out of all fourteen of them, quietly, the way you take the last page out of a book, so that when they got to where it should have been, they were back at the beginning.

It took less than a second.

The big man with the beard dropped his bottle.

It hit the cobbles and did not break, and rolled, and the mead went out of it across the snow in a dark stain. The big man did not look at it. He was looking at a place in the air about a yard in front of him and a little to the left, at the height of a small boy hiding under a kitchen table, and his face had gone quite still, and then it began, steadily, without any noise at all, to cry.

The thin one with the long neck turned his head, very politely, to one side, as though somebody had spoken to him.

"No," he said. "No, I've told you. After supper. I said I'd do it after supper."

The one in the bookshop doorway went half round and stopped, as though he had been about to go in and had remembered something.

One of the boys by the handcart went down on his knees and put his hands over his ears. The other went down on his knees beside the cart and held his hands out in front of him, palms up and open, the way you would hold them out to somebody under the ice.

The youngest, in the gutter, took hold of his own left wrist, the wrist of his wand hand, very tightly, in his right. And after a moment he began to laugh. Two short breaths and a pause. Two short breaths. Neither happy nor unhappy. The sound of somebody who has understood a joke a long time after it was made.

They were all quite still.

None of them moved his feet. None of them looked at Harry. Their wands hung from their fingers or lay in the snow, and their eyes were open, and each pair of eyes was looking at something different, and none of the things they were looking at was in the high street.

Harry stood among them and read them.

He read them living it.

He read the big one under the table, and the thin one in the hospital, and the youngest on the stream bank with the old man and the lantern, all of it, at once, coming to the end that was not there and going back to the beginning, and coming to it again. It was very loud. It was the loudest thing in the village.

He did not turn it down.

He looked for something in himself about it.

There was nothing there. He had expected that. He had decided it on the bridge.

He held it, and it held itself. That was the thing about it that he would think about later, and that he did not think about at all now. He did not have to do anything. He had built one small thing, the missing end, and they did the rest themselves, all fourteen of them, over and over, with their own minds, and it did not cost him anything to keep it going. It was the cheapest illusion he had ever made.

He looked round the high street, at the fourteen men standing in the snow, and decided that it would do.

Then he went and sat down.

* * *

The post office had three steps up to its door.

He sat on the second. He did not choose it. It was the one his feet found. He sat down with his elbows on his knees and the fourteen in front of him, scattered up and down the high street between the post office and the bridge, the way people stand about in a room before a party has properly started.

The snow was coming down more thickly now.

It came down quite straight, in the still air, out of a sky he could not see, and it settled on the fourteen men. It settled on their shoulders and the folds of their hoods and the tops of their pushed-up masks. It settled on the big man's hair. It settled in the crook of the youngest one's elbow, where he held his own wrist. It settled on the handcart and the bottle and the dark stain of mead and the open door of Gladrags and the broom outside the apothecary.

It settled on Harry.

He did not brush it off.

He put his hand in the pocket of his coat, for no reason, and found an apple.

He looked at it. It was a green one. He had bought it at Kirkgate Market on Tuesday from a woman with a stall at the end of the fruit row who called everybody *love*, and put it in his pocket, and forgotten it. It was cold right through.

He took his right glove off to eat it.

He put the glove down on the step beside him, palm up, and took a bite of the apple, and it was very cold and very sour, and he chewed it slowly and watched the high street.

He did not feel the cold.

He would have, once. He would have felt it in his hand, and noticed it, and put the glove back on. He did not notice it. He sat on the post office step with the apple in his bare hand and the snow coming down on him and on everything, and watched the fourteen, and was perfectly calm.

It was the calmest he had been in his life.

He watched them the way some people watch a fire. He was not waiting for them to do anything. He watched them because they were there, and they were doing what they did, and he had nowhere in particular to be.

At some point in the night he wanted there to be flowers.

He did not know why. He did not think about it. He was looking at the snow round the big man's feet, at the half-filled print of his boot, and he thought that it would be nice if there were flowers in it, so there were. They came up through the snow round the feet of all fourteen of them, slowly, as he watched. White ones first, too big for snowdrops. Then blue ones. Then others. They came up out of the white round the black boots and opened, in the dark, in February, in the snow, and there was no reason for them at all, and he did not need one.

He looked at them for a while.

They were very pretty.

He took another bite of the apple.

* * *

It got light at about seven.

It came up grey and then white behind the hills on the far side of the village, and the snow stopped some time after that, and the smoke from the Three Broomsticks went on going straight up into a white sky, and the fourteen stood in the high street with the snow on their shoulders, living it.

People came.

Not up the high street. Nobody came up the high street. They came at the top end, round the back of the Hog's Head and down through the yards behind the shops, along the frozen stream, over the wall behind the bookshop. They were carrying things. They were looking for people. There was a door that kept banging somewhere and somebody who kept going back to shut it. There was a child asking where its shoes were.

He read them as they came.

They were frightened, and grieving, and very tired. And when they looked down the high street, at the fourteen men standing in the snow, and at the boy sitting on the post office step at the far end of it, eating an apple, they were frightened of something else as well.

He read that too. He did not mind it.

A witch with a box of bandages came out of the alley by the bookshop at some point and stopped dead and looked at him down the length of the street. Hestia Jones. He knew her a little, from the Order. He read her. She was horrified, and sorry, and something under both that was like the feeling you have when you look over the edge of a cliff and a part of you wants to step off. She did not come any closer.

Nobody came any closer.

He did not want them to.

He sat on the step and watched the fourteen, and the clock over the post office door, which had a crack across its face, went on going above his head. He did not look at it. He heard it. It was a very small, very ordinary tick, quite steady, and it was the only thing in the high street apart from the youngest one's laughter that made any noise.

He finished the apple, except for the core.

He found another in his other pocket, a red one, and started on that.

* * *

At about twenty to ten somebody came down the lane.

He heard them before he read them. He heard feet in the snow, coming down from the top of the lane where it ran out between the hedges, slowly, stopping and starting. The feet came down into the high street and went past the open door of Gladrags and the broom on the pavement and the cat on the Honeydukes sill. They went to the bridge.

They stopped at the bridge for a long time.

Then they came up into the high street, slowly, and went in among the fourteen. They stopped by the big one. They stopped by the thin one with the long neck, long enough to listen to him. They went to the youngest, in the gutter outside Scrivenshaft's, and stopped there, very close.

Harry did not look up.

He reached for whoever it was, idly, the way he reached for everybody, without deciding to.

There was nothing there.

He noticed it the way you notice a step that is not where you expected it, and then forget it. There was a person walking about among the fourteen men in the high street, quite slowly, stopping, looking, and when he reached for the person there was nothing. No fear. No grief. No horror. Not even the cliff-edge thing he had read in the witch with the bandages. Not anything at all. A gap in the morning shaped like somebody.

He did not find it interesting.

He went back to the street.

The feet came on, after a while. They went to the alley by the bookshop, and stopped, and he heard voices, two of them, low. Then one set of feet went away up the alley. Then the other set stood still in the road for a long time, and went back, once, to the gutter outside Scrivenshaft's.

Then they came towards him.

They came quite slowly, straight up the middle of the high street through the snow. Forty-one paces. He did not count them. Somebody did.

They stopped at the foot of the post office steps.

Harry looked up.

There was a girl standing at the bottom of the steps, in the snow, with her hands in the pockets of a grey cardigan. She had straight brown hair, tied back. She had an ordinary face. It was the kind of face that people did not remember. She was looking at him.

He looked at her.

He looked at her the way he had looked at the street and the handcart and the cat on the sill, and he reached for her, as he had reached for everything all night, and found nothing. Nothing in her at all. Not even silence. Just a girl at the bottom of some steps, with snow on her shoulders, in a cardigan, who had come down from the lane.

There was nothing there that needed doing.

He smiled at her.

It was a perfectly nice smile. It was the one he used on shop assistants, and train guards, and Madam Pince, and Mr Akhtar in the newsagent's. He had been using it since he was six. It cost him nothing at all.

"I wouldn't stand so close to them, if I were you," he said pleasantly. "They're busy."

He took another bite of the apple.

She did not move. He did not mind. He had gone back to the street.

He thought, for a moment, watching the youngest one laugh in the gutter, that she must be one of the villagers. Somebody from the passage, come down to look. Somebody's daughter.

He did not wonder whose.
