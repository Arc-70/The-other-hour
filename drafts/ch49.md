## Chapter 49: Snape's Worst Memory

Professor Snape's office was cold, and lined with shelves, and on the shelves there were jars, and in the jars there were slimy things suspended in coloured potion, and they had been there for as long as Harry had been coming to this room, and none of them had ever once moved.

"Sit," said Snape.

Harry sat.

Snape did not. He stood behind his desk with his wand in his hand and looked at Harry with his flat black eyes, and Harry read the edge of him, the readiness, smooth and cold.

"The Headmaster," said Snape, "has asked me to teach you to close your mind. He believes that the Dark Lord may be able to make use of the connection between you. I am inclined to believe him. I am also inclined to believe that you will be the worst pupil I have ever had in this subject, because you have spent your entire life with your mind open and listening at every keyhole in this castle, and you will not know how to stop." He paused. "Am I wrong?"

"I don't know, sir."

"You will find out. I am going to try to enter your mind. You are going to try to stop me. You may use your wand if you wish. You may use anything you like." He raised his own. "Clear your mind, Potter."

"How?"

"*Legilimens.*"

* * *

The office went.

It did not go dark. It went sideways, the way a room goes sideways when you trip on a stair, and then it was not there at all, and instead there were pictures, one after another, very fast, like the pages of a book being riffled under a thumb.

He was five and in the cupboard under the stairs, and the bulb was on, and he was frightened of the dark.

He was nine and Dudley was holding him over a toilet.

He was eleven and there was a giant in a doorway on a rock in the sea.

He was twelve and in a round room under the school with a black stone desk, and a lamp that lit only for him.

That one he felt go. He felt Snape take hold of it, the way you take hold of a book on a shelf because the spine has caught your eye, and begin to draw it out.

*No.*

Harry did not decide anything. He did what he had done in every dark place he had ever been in. He reached out to see what was there.

He reached out along the thing that was coming in.

It was like reaching along a rope in the dark. It was a cold, thin, very strong thread, and it went out of his own head and across the office into somebody else's, and at the end of it there was Snape. Not the edge. The inside. For one second Harry was through the smooth closed surface of him and in, and it was not dark in there at all.

There was a small house. A narrow street of small houses, and a mill chimney at the end of it, and a man shouting in a kitchen. A woman crying, very quietly, so that the man would not hear.

The office came back.

Harry was on the floor. He did not remember falling. His chair had gone over. Snape was standing behind his desk with his wand still raised, and he was very white, and Harry read him, because he could not stop, and for once the edge of him was not smooth at all.

He was angry. He was so angry it had gone cold. And under it, for the first time in four years, Harry read something in Severus Snape that was aimed at Harry and was nothing to do with his father.

It was fear.

"You will not," said Snape, very softly, "do that again."

Harry got up off the floor.

"You did it first," he said.

* * *

It went on like that.

He went down to the dungeons on Mondays at six and came up again at seven with a headache that lasted until morning, and the lessons were not lessons. Snape attacked. Harry did not defend. He had never in his life known how to defend. He did the only thing he had ever been any good at, which was reaching, and when Snape came in along the cold thread, Harry went out along it.

Snape got better at stopping him.

He got better very fast. By the second lesson the inside of him was shut so hard that reaching along the thread was like reaching along a rope into a wall. By the third, Snape had learned to come in and go out again so quickly that Harry had no time to follow. He would see the cupboard, or the dragon, or Cedric's face in a churchyard, and then the office would come back and Snape would be standing at his desk with his wand lowered, saying "Again," in a voice that had nothing in it at all.

Harry did not learn to close anything.

"You're not trying," said Snape, at the end of the third lesson.

"I am trying."

"You are trying to win," said Snape. "It is not the same thing. You have never in your life closed a door. You have only ever gone through them." He put his wand away. "The Dark Lord will not be stopped by a boy who goes through doors. He will be delighted by one. Get out."

* * *

"Close it?" said Evelyn. "You don't close it."

They were in the fourth-floor classroom, at nine o'clock on the Thursday, with the moon on the floor and her feet up on the desk in front, which she only ever did when nobody but Harry was there.

"Snape says close it."

"Snape says a great many things." She looked at the ceiling. "Grandmother never said close. She said *keep*. Keep your own counsel. Keep your own things. A lady's thoughts are her own."

"That's the same thing."

"It isn't." She was quiet for a moment, the way she was when she was getting a sentence exactly right. "When you close a door, you're still standing next to it. Holding it. Anybody who wants to can feel you holding it, and push. That's what Snape does. I've watched him with the seventh-years. He holds the door very hard, and it's a very good door, and you can tell it's there from the other side of the room." She took her feet down. "I don't hold anything. There isn't a door. There's just a room with nothing left on the table. Somebody comes in, they look round, there's nothing there. They go out again."

"How?"

"Every morning since I was five," said Evelyn, "I wound the watch, and while I wound it Grandmother made me put everything away. Every thought. Every feeling. Folded. In a drawer. Twelve turns of the crown. By the twelfth, the table had to be clear." She looked at him. "I still do it. I did it this morning. I've done it every morning for eleven years and I've never once left anything out."

Harry looked at her for a long time.

He read her. He did not mean to. He never meant to. There was nothing. There was the silence, steady and quiet and complete, and he had always thought of it as a wall, or a door, or a closed shutter.

It was a table with nothing on it.

"I couldn't do that," he said.

"No," agreed Evelyn. "You'd go round the room first and look in all the drawers. To see if anybody else had left anything." She put her hands in her cardigan pockets. "You're not built for it, Potter. You were never going to be. Whoever thought you'd learn to close your mind in a term had never met you."

"Dumbledore thought it."

"Then Dumbledore had never met you either," said Evelyn.

* * *

The fourth lesson was on the second Monday of December.

It was very cold. There was snow on the windows of the corridor going down to the dungeons, and Snape's office was colder than the corridor. Harry came in and sat down without being told to, and Snape looked at him across the desk with something in the edge of him that Harry had not felt there before.

It was tiredness.

Snape had been out the night before. Harry knew it the way he knew it was Monday. He had gone wherever he went, and come back very late, and stood in front of the person he went to, and done what he did there. Harry did not know what that was. But it had left Snape tired, very tired, in a way that went right through the smooth closed surface of him and showed on the outside, like water through a ceiling.

"Clear your mind," said Snape.

"Sir…"

"*Legilimens.*"

He came in fast. He came in faster than he had ever come in, as though he wanted it over, and Harry saw the cupboard and the giant and his own face in a mirror in an empty classroom with his parents standing behind him, and then a churchyard, and then a white ward in another world, very briefly, and Snape stopped at that one. Harry felt him stop. He felt Snape look at it, puzzled, a hospital ward with a face in it that he did not know.

He felt Snape go still to look.

And Harry went out along the thread.

He did not think about it. He went out the way he had gone out in the first lesson, along the cold rope, and this time there was no wall at the end of it. There was a door, and it was held, and it was a very good door. But the hand holding it was tired.

He went through.

* * *

He was in a playground.

It was a small dingy playground at the edge of a town, with a river beyond it and a mill chimney beyond that, and it was summer, and it was a long time ago. There were two little girls on the swings. One of them was thin and fair and was saying something cross. The other one was swinging very high. She had long dark red hair, and she was laughing, and at the very top of the swing's arc she let go.

She flew.

She went up into the air and came down slowly, far too slowly, as though the air were holding her, and landed on her feet on the tarmac, and laughed again.

There was a boy behind the bushes.

He was thin and sallow and he was wearing clothes that did not fit him. His coat was too big. It was a man's coat, and the sleeves came down over his hands, and his trousers were too short, and he was watching the girl with the red hair.

Harry was not watching the boy.

Harry was the boy.

He was inside him, the way he had been inside a younger Dumbledore in a stone basin in June, only nothing like that at all. No smoke. Nothing faint. He was not reading the boy from outside, a feeling at a time. He was in the place where the feeling was, and it was coming up all round him, and he did not have to reach for it. It was everywhere.

It was the girl.

The boy was looking at her, and what he felt was so large and so simple that it took Harry a moment to know what it was, because he had never felt anything like it in his life. He had read love before. He had read Mrs Weasley's, and Hagrid's, and the other Sirius's, which he had kept. He had read it in parents and in friends and in a very old man for a girl in a portrait.

This was not like any of them.

There was nothing else in it. There was no layer under it. There was no fear holding it up, or pride, or wanting. There was only the girl on the swing, and the boy behind the bushes, and the light that came off her when she laughed, and he would have stood in those bushes for the rest of his life just to see her do it once more.

It was the purest thing Harry had ever read in anybody.

And it was already hurting.

* * *

It changed.

The playground went, and there was a train, and the girl was older, and there was a castle. There were years, very fast. There was a corridor and a classroom and a common room under a lake. There were the two of them by a river, and in a library, and on a staircase, and in every one of them the thing was there, the same, enormous, and in every one of them it was a little more hurt.

Then the lake.

It was the Hogwarts lake. It was summer, and there were students on the grass by the water, and exams had just finished. Harry knew it the way you know things in dreams. The boy was older now, fifteen or sixteen, thin and lank-haired, and he was sitting by himself under a bush with an exam paper, reading it over.

There were four boys under a beech tree.

One of them had untidy black hair and glasses, and was playing with something small and golden, catching it and letting it go. One of them was handsome and bored and had his feet up. One of them was pale and tired and reading. One of them was small and watery-eyed and laughing at whatever the first one did.

Harry knew them all.

He knew the bored one by his laugh. He had heard that laugh in a hut on a rock, and in a tunnel under a tree, and in a hall in Grimmauld Place three months ago.

He did not watch what they did. He did not have to. He was inside the boy under the bush, and he felt it. He felt them come. He felt the wand go flying out of his hand. He felt himself hang upside down in the air with everybody on the grass laughing, and the old robes falling over his face, and the grey underwear, and he felt the shame of it, which was huge, which was a whole lake of shame, and under the shame a hatred so clean and so hard that it had edges like a knife.

And he felt her come.

The girl with the red hair. She came across the grass, furious, and stood in front of the boy with the glasses and told him to stop it. She was defending him. She had come across the whole of the lawn in front of everybody to defend him.

And the thing in him, the great pure thing, rose up to meet her like a wave.

And at the same moment, in the same place, the shame rose up to meet it. All the laughter on the grass, the whole lawn watching, the robes over his face. And the shame met the great pure thing in the middle of him, and the shame was larger, for one second, only one.

He said a word.

It was the word Malfoy had said to Hermione outside the Great Hall in second year. It was the worst word there was, and he said it to her, to the girl who had come across the grass, and Harry felt it go out of him, from the inside, the way you feel a bone go.

He felt her face change.

He felt the great pure thing, which had been the whole of him since a playground by a river, break. It did not stop. That was the worst of it. It broke, and it went on. It went on being exactly as large as it had always been, and exactly as pure, and it was broken all the way through, and it would be broken forever.

It was the worst thing Harry had ever felt.

* * *

He was thrown out.

He was thrown out of Snape's head so hard that he went backwards over the chair and into the shelves, and a jar came down on the stone floor beside him and burst, and something grey and slimy slid across the flags in a puddle of yellow potion and lay still.

Harry lay on the floor and did not get up.

He did not know where he was for a while. He was in a dungeon office. He was under a bush by a lake. He was in a playground watching a girl fly. He put his hand flat on the cold stone floor until he knew which one.

Snape was standing over him.

His wand was out. It was pointing at Harry's face. His own face was perfectly white and perfectly still, and his hand was shaking, very slightly, so slightly that anybody who could not read him would never have noticed.

Harry read him.

He could not stop. He would not have stopped if he could. The door was open. The tired hand had let go of it, and Snape had not got it shut again, and everything in him was at the top.

Shame. Enormous. The shame from the lake, all of it, as fresh as though it had been that afternoon.

Hatred. Aimed at Harry. Aimed at Harry's face, which was his father's face, the face of the boy under the beech tree with the golden ball.

And under both of them, coming up through them, a new thing.

It had not been there in four years of loathing. It had not been there in the first lesson, or the third. It was aimed at Harry and it was new, and it was so cold and so absolute that Harry knew, lying on the floor among the broken glass, that it would never go away.

He had been felt.

Snape had spent his whole life with a door shut over the one true thing in him. He had held it for twenty years, in a castle full of children and a house full of masks and an office with jars in it. Nobody had ever been in that room. Not the Dark Lord. Not Dumbledore. Nobody had ever stood where Harry had just stood and felt it from the inside, the whole of it, the girl on the swing and the word on the grass.

Harry had.

And he was James Potter's son.

"Get out," said Snape.

"Sir…"

"*Get out.*"

Harry got up. He got up slowly, because his head was ringing and his hand was bleeding where he had put it down on the glass, and he went to the door.

"These lessons," said Snape behind him, "are over. You may tell the Headmaster so. You may tell him that I am unable to teach a boy who cannot keep his hands out of other people's pockets." His voice did not rise at all. "And Potter. If you ever speak of what you saw. To anyone. To your little Selwyn. To your godfather. To *anyone*."

He did not finish.

He did not need to. Harry read the end of the sentence in him, quite plainly, and it was not a threat. It was simpler than a threat.

He went out, and the door shut behind him, and he heard the lock turn.

* * *

He did not tell Evelyn what he had seen.

He told her that he had got in, and that he had seen a memory, and that Snape had thrown him out and ended the lessons. He told her on the rock by the lake on the Tuesday afternoon, in the snow, with his hand bandaged.

She was quiet for a long time.

"You went in," she said.

"He went in first. Every lesson. He went through everything. The cupboard. The churchyard. Everything."

"I know."

"So I don't see why…"

"That's not the same thing," said Evelyn, "and you know it."

He looked at her.

She was looking at the lake. There was ice at the edges of it, thin and grey, and snow on the rock between them, and her face was the face it always was, the face people forgot.

"He was teaching you," she said. "He was doing what he was asked to do. Badly, probably. Unkindly, certainly. But he was asked, and he said yes, and he was doing it." She did not look at him. "You weren't asked. You went in because you could. Because there was a door and you've never once in your life left a door alone."

"That's not fair."

"No," agreed Evelyn. "It isn't. It's accurate."

Harry did not say anything.

He thought about the playground. He thought about the boy behind the bushes, in the coat that was too big, watching the girl fly, with nothing in him at all but her. He thought about the word on the grass and the thing breaking and going on.

He thought: *I would not want anybody to have seen that. Not anybody. Not ever.*

"You saw something," said Evelyn. "Something that matters."

"Yes."

"Are you going to tell me what?"

"No."

Evelyn looked at him for the first time. He could not read anything in her face. He never could. But she looked at him for a long moment, quite steadily, and then she nodded once, the way she nodded at a correct answer.

"Good," she said.

She skipped a stone. It went three times, and stopped at the ice, and lay on top of it, and did not sink.

* * *

He wrote to Dumbledore that night.

He wrote that the lessons were over and that it was his fault. He did not write why. He wrote that he was sorry. He did not write what for.

The answer came two days later, by an ordinary school owl, in a hand he knew.

*Thank you for telling me. I will speak to Severus. Please do not try to speak to him yourself. A.D.*

That was all.

He read it, and he read the shape of the place where it stopped, the way he had read the others, and he knew that Snape had already been to the Headmaster's office before Harry's letter had even arrived. He knew it as plainly as though he had been in the room. He did not know what Snape had said. He did not know what Dumbledore had said back.

He knew that neither of them was going to tell him.

* * *

The dreams had started in November.

They were not like ordinary dreams. They did not have pictures in them, at first. They had feelings, and the feelings were not his, and they came up through his scar in the dark the way they had come all summer at Privet Drive, only louder now. Closer.

Impatience, mostly.

It was the impatience of someone who has been waiting a very long time and has decided he does not have to wait any more. It came every few nights. It went on for hours. And under it there was something Harry had felt once before, in a churchyard, in a hot dream in a tower, and that was cold and pleased and had no bottom to it at all.

He did not tell anybody about the dreams.

He had been meant to learn to shut them out. He had not learned. The lessons were over. And with the lessons over, and the snow coming down every night against the dormitory window, and Theo asleep in the next bed, Harry lay awake in the green dark and thought about the cold thread in Snape's office, and how he had gone out along it.

He thought: *it's the same.*

He thought: *the scar is a thread. It goes out of my head and into someone else's. I've been lying here every night letting things come in along it. I've never once gone out.*

He knew it was a bad idea.

He knew it the way you know a staircase is about to move. He thought about what Dumbledore had written. *In both directions.* He thought about what Snape had said. *You have never in your life closed a door. You have only ever gone through them.*

He thought about Cedric.

He went out.

* * *

Going into Snape had been nothing like this.

This was no rope. It was much longer than a rope, and much thinner, and it went out of his forehead a very long way, out of the castle and over the hills and a great many miles of dark, and at the end of it there was nothing he could see.

There was only cold.

He had felt the cold in a churchyard. He had felt a sliver of it in a boy in another world, and a smaller sliver in himself, and he had felt it in a draught under Quirrell's warmth when he was eleven. He had never been inside it.

It was very large. It was much larger than Snape. It was larger than Dumbledore. It was the largest mind Harry had ever touched, and it did not feel like a person at all. It was like a house with a great many rooms, and every room was cold, and in every room there was something being kept.

He did not try to read all of it. He could not have. He went in a very little way, along the thinnest edge, the way you go into a cold sea one step at a time, and he read what was near the door.

Pleasure. Cold, slow pleasure. Aimed at a list.

He did not see the list. He felt the shape of it. Names. People. A man called Rookwood who had worked in the Department of Mysteries and could be got out of somewhere. A man called Avery who had been useful once and was not useful now. The giants, in the mountains, who were being spoken to. The Dementors, who were being spoken to, and who were listening.

And a house in Kent.

He did not get the name of it. He got the feeling of it, the way you get the smell of a room through a door, and the feeling was a Muggle-born wizard who had said something in public that the cold did not like, and a family, and a date. The date was a Thursday. The cold was looking forward to the Thursday.

Harry came out.

He came out very carefully, the way he had gone in, backwards, one step at a time. He lay in his bed in the dark and his scar was burning and he was soaked with sweat, and Theo had not woken, and nobody knew.

He wrote it down. All of it. Rookwood. Avery. The giants. The Dementors. The house in Kent, and a Thursday.

In the morning he gave the paper to Fawkes, who came to the Owlery when Harry asked him to, which Harry had not known until he tried.

* * *

There was no attack in Kent that Thursday.

There was a very small paragraph in the *Prophet* on the Saturday, on page nine, about a family of four in a village near Sevenoaks who had gone abroad unexpectedly for a long holiday, and whose house had been found the next morning with every window broken. Nobody was hurt. The house was empty. The Ministry was not investigating, because there was nothing to investigate.

Harry read it at breakfast.

He read it three times. Then he folded the *Prophet* and put it under his plate, and looked up at the staff table, and Dumbledore was not there.

He did not need him to be.

He sat at the Slytherin table with the paper under his plate and his porridge going cold and Theo beside him talking about Transfiguration, and he found that he was smiling. He did not know at first what it was. He had not felt it in a long time. Happiness was the wrong word, or too soft. This was something sharper.

It was winning.

He went out again on the Sunday night.

He went out four more times before the end of term. He went a little further each time. He did not go into the rooms. He stayed near the door and read what was there, and wrote it down, and gave it to Fawkes. He did not hear anything from Dumbledore about any of it, and he did not expect to, and three times there was a small paragraph in the *Prophet*, or there was not one where there should have been, and he knew.

He got very good at it.

That was the thing he thought about afterwards. Not that he had done it. That he had been good at it. That he had gone out along a thin cold thread into the largest, darkest mind in Britain, every few nights, for three weeks, and come back every time with something useful in his hands, and nobody had caught him.

He thought he was very quiet.

He thought the cold did not know he was there.

* * *

It was the night of the fifteenth of December. It was snowing.

He went out at a quarter past one. He went the usual way, along the thread, out over the hills and the miles of dark, and came to the door of the cold, and went in a little way, carefully, the way he always did.

It was very quiet in there.

That was the first thing. He had been in seven times, and every time there had been something near the door, something being looked at or looked forward to, a list, a plan, a face. Tonight there was nothing near the door. The rooms were very still. The cold was not doing anything at all.

He went a little further in, to see.

And the cold turned round.

He felt it. He had no other word for it. He was standing in the doorway of the largest mind in Britain, very quietly, and the whole of it, every cold room, turned, slowly, the way a man turns in a chair when he hears someone come in behind him.

It looked at him.

He felt it look. He felt its attention, which was enormous, come down the thread, the same thread, his thread, the one he had walked out along so carefully seven times. It came down it the other way, into him.

He felt it inside his head.

It did not hurry. It went along his memories the way a man goes along a shelf in a library, running one finger down the spines. It went past the cupboard without stopping. It went past the giant and the train and the Sorting and the troll. It went past Quirrell, and paused there, for one moment, with something like amusement, and went on.

He tried to stop it.

He did not know how. He had never closed a door. He tried to put things away, the way Evelyn put things away, folded, in a drawer, and there was no time, and there were too many, and the cold went along the shelf.

It found a round room.

It found it the way a finger finds a book with a spine that catches the eye. A small round room, under a school, behind the feet of a great stone statue. Shelves on every wall. A cold lamp that lit for one person only. A desk of black stone.

And on the desk, a book.

Small and black, with no title. Harry had read it a great many times, at that desk, under that lamp, with Evelyn reading the Latin over his shoulder. The cold opened it. He felt it open it, in his own memory, with his own hands, and turn the pages.

It went to the end.

It went to the last page, where a second hand had written a line underneath the first, in older, browner ink. Harry knew the line. He had asked Evelyn to read it to him again, in the study, on an evening in his third year.

The cold read it.

And the cold went very still.

* * *

Harry woke up.

He was sitting up in his four-poster with the curtains open and his hand over his scar, and he was breathing as though he had been running, and the dormitory was dark and silent, and the snow was coming down past the green window.

Theo was asleep. Crabbe was snoring. Nobody had woken.

He sat there for a long time.

It was a dream. He told himself that. It was a dream, the way the others had been dreams, only worse, because he had been tired and frightened and had not been sleeping properly for three weeks. He had dreamed about the study because he missed it. He had dreamed about the book because he thought about it more than he should. He had dreamed that the cold had turned round because he had been afraid, every single time he went out, that it would.

That was all.

He told himself that very clearly. He told it to himself the way he told things to Umbridge, calmly and reasonably and one piece at a time, watching himself rise to each piece and settle.

It very nearly worked.

He lay down again. He pulled the blankets up. He did not go out along the thread, and he did not let anything come in, and he lay in the green dark and listened to the snow and to Crabbe snoring and to the water moving against the window, very quietly, under the lake.

His scar did not hurt.

That was the strange thing. That was the thing he thought about, lying there until it was light. Every other night, when he came back, it had burned.

Tonight it did not hurt at all. It felt the way a door feels, he thought, when somebody has closed it very gently from the other side, so as not to wake the house.
