## Chapter 75: The Shrieking Shack

The doe came for the fifth time on the night of Sunday the twenty-second of March, at ten past one, and it did not come to warn him of anything.

Harry was below the screen. He had stopped making anything on it some time before. He was standing in the orchestra pit with his hands in his pockets and the grey canvas in front of him, not doing anything, which he did more and more now, between raids, for an hour at a time. He did not think about anything when he did it. He was simply there.

The doe came through the left-hand wall by the side boxes, as it always did, and walked down the side aisle, and stopped in front of the screen, and looked at him.

He read it.

He did not have to. He knew what it was made of. He read it anyway, because he always did, and it was the same as it had been every time: one love, very old, kept in a very small closed place, aimed at a girl with red hair.

It was the same.

He would think, afterwards, that he ought to have noticed it was the same. That a man who had decided to do what this man had decided to do would have had something else in him, somewhere, and that it would have got into the doe. It had not. The doe was made of the love, and the love had not changed in twenty years, and it did not change now.

The doe opened its mouth.

"Tuesday," it said, in the flat colourless voice that had been made not to be recognised. "Midnight. The Shrieking Shack. Come by the village side, through the back, the door to the old kitchen. Come alone. Bring the Cloak."

Harry did not move.

"He knows where it is," said the doe. "Not the place. The house. He has had it from a prisoner. He will have the cinema by Friday. The Cloak must not be there when he comes. I will take it somewhere he will not look. Come alone. Tell nobody. If you tell anybody, he will have it from them, as he had the cinema."

It stood a moment longer.

Then it walked into the screen, and stood on it, silver on the grey, and faded from the edges in, the way it always did.

Harry stood in the orchestra pit.

He thought about it. He thought about it clearly and calmly, the way he thought about everything now. A prisoner had talked. That was quite possible. There had been a great many prisoners. Selby had talked to the Aurors for nine hours. Somebody, somewhere, in some cellar, had talked about a cinema in Leeds. And the Dark Lord had two of three, and wanted the third more than he had wanted anything since a door in a dark round room, and the third was folded into a small square in the inside pocket of Harry's coat.

He thought about a courtyard at the foot of a tower, last June.

He thought about a man standing alone in it, looking up at a parapet, unable to close.

He thought about a wand held out across a tower roof without a word.

He trusted it.

He did not tell anybody. That was easy. He had stopped telling anybody anything three weeks ago, and nobody had noticed, except perhaps the one person he did not look at.

* * *

He went on Tuesday at a quarter past eleven.

He did not take the stairs to the projection room to say he was going. He went out through the foyer, past the ticket booth with its brass grille, past the stairs to the circle where nobody was sitting, and out of the fire door into the alley. It was raining. It was the thin cold steady rain of Leeds in March, and it had been raining for four days.

He did not look up at the small square windows of the projection room as he went.

If he had, he would not have seen anything. The projection room was dark. Ron was asleep. Hermione was asleep. And the camp bed in the far corner by the projectors had a shape on it, under a blanket, with its face to the wall, that was quite still, and would stay quite still until Ron got up at seven and found it was two pillows and a rolled-up cardigan.

Harry did not know that.

He turned on the spot in the rain.

* * *

He came out at the end of the lane that ran past the Shrieking Shack, on the far side of Hogsmeade from the high street, where the village gave up and the hill began.

No rain here. It was very cold and very clear, and there was a moon, nearly full, and the frost was white on the long grass of the hill and on the brambles and on the broken fence round the Shack's overgrown garden. Below him, to the left, the village lay dark. He could see the high street from here, a long pale line between black roofs, and the bridge at the bottom of it, and the post office at the top.

There was nobody in the high street now.

He did not look at it for long.

The Shack stood at the top of its garden, black against the frost. It was as he remembered it. He had been inside it once, at thirteen, in the dark, at the end of a tunnel, with a man who was not yet his godfather and a rat with a toe missing, and a werewolf and a Potions master and a girl he could not read. The windows were boarded. The roof sagged. The chimney leaned. It had been the most haunted house in Britain for twenty-five years because a boy had screamed in it once a month, and the boy was a man now, somewhere in the north among the packs, and sent messages on a coin signed R.L.

Harry went round to the back.

There was a door to the old kitchen, as the doe had said. It hung off one hinge. It had been forced, a long time ago, and nobody had ever mended it. He pushed it, and it went in with a long low scrape, like the fire door at the Majestic, and he went through it into the dark.

He reached ahead of him as he went.

He found the house empty. Rats in the walls, small and frightened and hungry. An owl in the roof. Nothing else. No people. No feeling anywhere in the house at all, from the cellar to the attic, except the rats and the owl.

And in the front room upstairs, the one with the four-poster bed, the one he remembered, there was nothing.

Not empty. Nothing. The particular nothing he had felt every day for seven years in the Great Hall at the staff table, at the front of a dungeon classroom, across a desk in an office lined with jars. A closed mind. Very closed. Closed the way a door is closed when somebody is leaning on it from the other side.

It ought to have warned him.

He would think that, afterwards, many times. He had read this man closed for seven years and open exactly twice. He had read him open in a courtyard with grief, and open on a tower with permission, and both times it had been because something had happened to him that was too large to hold the door against. He had trusted the man because of those two times. And now the man was in a room at the top of the stairs, waiting for him, and the door was shut, and leaned on, harder than Harry had ever felt it.

He went up the stairs.

* * *

Severus Snape was standing by the boarded window.

There was a candle on the floor. It was the only light. It stood in the middle of the dusty boards between the door and the four-poster, and it threw Snape's shadow up the wall behind him and across the ceiling, very tall and very thin. He was in black, as he always was. He had his hands folded in front of him. He did not have his wand out.

He looked older. Harry had not seen him since a courtyard in June, nine months ago, and he looked ten years older, and much thinner, and his face was the colour of the candle.

"Potter," he said.

"Professor."

"You came alone."

"Yes."

"You brought it."

Harry put his hand on the inside pocket of his coat. He did not take the Cloak out.

"Yes."

Snape looked at him.

He looked for a long moment, across the candle, from the window. Harry reached for him, out of habit, and found the door, shut, and the weight on the other side of it. Nothing came through. Nothing at all.

"Sit down," said Snape.

"I'll stand."

"As you like." Snape did not move from the window. "I am going to say some things. It will take about a minute. Then we will do what we came here to do." He paused. "I would be obliged if you did not interrupt. I have been thinking about how to say them for some days, and I would like to say them in the right order."

Harry said nothing.

"Your mother," said Snape, "was the best person I ever knew."

The candle burned. Somewhere in the walls a rat moved.

"You know that. You have known it since your fourth year, when you came into my head in a lesson that was meant to teach you to keep people out of yours, and took it. You did not see it. Anybody can see a memory. You felt it. What I felt for her. You were the first person who ever did, and I have never forgiven you for it, and I never will." He said it quite flatly, as though he were reading out a list. "I mention it so that you will understand that I know what you are. I knew before Albus did. I have watched it grow in you for seven years, in my classroom and my house, with her eyes in your face. I have known exactly what you could do since you were eleven."

He took a breath.

"Albus knew too. He was afraid of it. He was afraid of very few things, and he was afraid of you. He told me so. On the tower. In April of last year." Snape's eyes did not leave Harry's face. "He asked me to do something, if it came to it. I said no. I said no for an hour. I did not believe it would ever come to it. I thought he was a tired old man who had seen one boy go wrong and was seeing ghosts."

He looked at Harry.

"I have read the reports from Hogsmeade," said Snape. "I have read what the Healers wrote. I have read about Selby, who talks to the wall. I have read that the Dark Lord's people are calling you *the other one*, and I have thought about what they mean by it, and I think they are right."

Harry said nothing.

He did not feel anything. He noticed that. He stood in the candlelight in the room with the four-poster bed and heard the man who had held out a wand across a tower roof tell him that the Dark Lord's people were right about him, and he looked for what he felt about it, and there was nothing there.

He thought that was probably what Snape was talking about.

"You were the boy who felt sick," said Snape. "Albus told me. When he showed you Tom at eleven, in the orphanage, you felt sick. He said that was the difference. That was the whole of it. What you felt about the people in the way." He unfolded his hands. "What did you feel, Potter, on the step? At Hogsmeade? Watching them?"

Harry thought about it honestly.

"Nothing," he said.

"No," said Snape. "I thought not."

He took his wand out of his sleeve.

He did it quite slowly. He did not point it at Harry. He held it at his side, loosely, and looked at Harry across the candle with his black eyes, and for one second, less, the door in him moved, a crack, and Harry felt through the crack the thing that was on the other side of it, leaning.

It was grief.

Not for Harry. For her. It was for a girl on a swing, and it had been made, tonight, larger than Harry had ever felt it, larger than in the courtyard, larger than in the doe, and it was aimed at the green eyes in Harry's face, and it was saying goodbye.

Then the door shut again.

"I am going to take it away from you," said Severus Snape. "All of it. The reading. The illusions. Every door you have. I am going to go into your head and close them, one after another, from the inside, the way I have closed my own every day for twenty years, and I am going to lock them, and I am going to break the keys. When I have finished, you will never feel another person again. Not one. Not ever. You will be what everyone else is." He raised the wand. "And then I am going to take the Cloak, and burn it, so that he never has it. And then you can go home."

Harry understood.

He understood all of it at once, quite clearly, standing in the candlelight. The doe, the same as always, because the love in it was the same as always and always would be. The message. *He will have the cinema by Friday.* Perhaps true, perhaps not. *Come alone. Tell nobody.* The courtyard. The tower. *If he becomes what Tom became.*

He understood, and he did not feel anything about it, and he reached for his wand.

"*Legilimens*," said Snape.

* * *

It was not like the lessons.

He had had lessons, in his fifth year, in Snape's office, and they had been duels, and he had won one of them and been thrown out of the room for it. In the lessons Snape had come into his head the way a man comes into a house to look for something, quickly, opening drawers, and Harry had learned to go the other way, back down the line, into Snape's own house, and open his.

Snape was not looking for anything.

He came into Harry's head the way a man comes into a house to shut it up for the winter.

He went straight to the doors.

Harry felt him go. He felt him walk in, very cold and very quick, past everything, past the cupboard under the stairs and the Sorting and the Chamber and the graveyard and the lawn, past every memory Harry had, without stopping to look at a single one of them, the way you walk past furniture in a house you are closing, to the back of the house, where the doors were.

Harry had never known there were doors.

He knew now. He felt Snape find them. There was one for the reading, the oldest one, the one that had always been open, that he had never once in his life closed or even known could be closed. There was one beside it for the push. There was one, newer, much larger, for the illusions. There were others. Small ones. Ones he did not know the names of. They were all open. They had always been open. That was what he was.

Snape put his hand on the first one, and began to close it.

* * *

Harry fought.

He fought the way he fought everything now, without feeling, very fast, very hard. He threw a wall in front of the doors, a wall of the Majestic, red plush and gilt and the faces of women with stars in their hair, and Snape walked through it as though it were not there, because it was not there, and he knew it.

He threw a crowd. Two hundred people in Diagon Alley in the rain, every one of them different, walking between Snape and the doors. Snape walked through them, too. He did not look at them. A crowd is weak, and full of holes, and Snape went through the holes.

He threw Lily.

He did not decide to. He was losing, and he reached for the strongest thing he had, and the strongest thing he had was in the man in front of him. He took the girl on the swing, out of a memory he had stolen at fourteen, and put her between Snape and the doors, at eleven, laughing, with her red hair flying.

Snape stopped.

For one second, less, he stopped, in the back of Harry's head, and looked at her.

Then he went past her.

He went past her the way you go past a photograph of somebody you have loved on the wall of a house you are closing, without stopping, because if you stop you will never finish. Harry felt him go past. He felt what it cost. It cost more than anything Harry had ever felt anybody pay, and Snape paid it, and kept walking, and put his hand back on the door.

The first door began to close.

Harry felt it go. It did not hurt. That was the worst thing. It was quiet, and slow, and it did not hurt at all. He felt the world begin to go quiet round him. Not the room. Further out. The owl in the roof went first. Then the rats in the walls. Small things, at the edges, going out like the lights of a town seen from a train, one after another, into the dark. He reached for the owl, and it was not there. He reached for the rats, and they were not there.

He did the only thing left.

He went the other way.

He went back down the line, into Snape's head, the way he had in his fifth year, the way that had won him a lesson and lost him a teacher. He went to find the worst night. Everybody had one. It was at the bottom. It was always behind a door they had shut and walked away from. He would open it, and take the end off it, and Snape would stand in this room until he died of cold.

He found it at once.

Not at the bottom, and not behind any door. A lake shore on a bright day, and a word said in anger to a girl with red hair, and her face as she heard it. And it was standing quite open, at the very front of Snape's mind, where anybody could see it, where Snape could see it every time he turned round.

He had never shut it.

He had looked at it every day for twenty-two years. He had walked past it every morning on his way to everything else. It had no end because it had never had an end, and Snape had never needed one, because Snape had never once tried to get away from it.

There was nothing to open.

Harry could not use it. He stood in the front of Snape's mind with the worst night of Snape's life in front of him, wide open, and there was nothing he could do with it that Snape had not already done to himself, every day, on purpose, for twenty-two years.

Snape knew who he was.

That was the thing. Harry understood it standing there. Snape was weaker than he was, much weaker, in every way that could be measured. But Snape knew exactly who he was, and what he had done, and what it had cost, and he had looked at all of it every day, and he was standing on all of it. And Harry had built a cinema, and a crowd, and fourteen men in the snow, and a stranger's face at breakfast, and he had gone into every one of them so far that there was nothing left in the middle to stand on.

He had no centre.

Snape found that before Harry did.

He felt Snape find it, and turn back to the doors, and close the first one all the way.

* * *

Harry was on the floor.

He did not remember falling. He was on his knees on the dusty boards beside the candle, with his hands flat on the floor and his wand somewhere he could not see, and the room was very quiet.

The reading was going.

Not gone. Going. He could still feel Snape, a little, through the door Snape had left open in himself to come through. He could not feel the owl, or the rats, or the village below, or anything beyond the walls of the room. It was like going deaf. It was like the whole world going deaf at once. He had never in his life been in a room where he could not feel the next room.

Snape was standing over him. He could see the hem of the black robes. He could see the wand, held quite steady, pointing down.

The second door was closing.

Harry reached for anything at all.

And something went past.

It went past him in the dark at the back of his head, in the house being closed up for the winter, very quick. Not his. One of Snape's, shaken loose by the fighting, the way a picture comes off a wall when somebody slams a door. It was a memory. It went past like a lit window going past a train.

A tower. A moonless night. The door at the top of the stairs shut. An old man at the parapet with his back to the room, and his white hair, and his hands on the stone.

*If he becomes what Tom became,* said Albus Dumbledore, quietly, without turning round, *you will have to stop him.*

The window went past.

Harry was on his knees on the floor of the Shrieking Shack, and he could not feel the owl, and he could not feel the rats, and he understood that he was going to be stopped, and that Dumbledore had asked for it, and he found, looking for it, that he did not feel anything about that either.

His face was going empty.

He could feel it go. Not the face on other people's eyes. His own. From the inside. The way you feel your face go when you are falling asleep at a table, and your mouth goes slack, and your eyes stop looking at anything.

The third door began to close.

* * *

Evelyn had been in the passage under the stairs for eleven minutes.

She had followed him from the Majestic. She had followed him for three weeks now, every time he went out alone, and he had not known once. She had been on the camp bed with her face to the wall at a quarter past eleven, as she was every night, listening, and she had heard him come up from the screen, and stop in the foyer, and go out of the fire door, and she had been off the bed and down the stairs with her wand and her coat before the door had finished closing, leaving two pillows and a rolled-up cardigan under the blanket.

She had held his sleeve in the alley as he turned on the spot.

He had not felt it. She had made very sure that he would not. She had touched only the very edge of the cuff, with two fingers, from behind, in the rain, at the exact moment he turned, the way her brother had taught her to take a sweet out of somebody's pocket when she was eight. She had come with him, side by side in the dark, and landed at the end of a lane on a hill above Hogsmeade, and let go, and stepped back into the shadow of a hedge, and he had never once looked round.

She had watched him go into the Shack.

She had waited four minutes. Then she had gone in after him, through the kitchen door off its hinge, and up as far as the turn of the stairs, and stopped there, in the dark, with her back to the wall and her wand in her hand, and listened.

She had heard Snape.

She had heard every word. She had stood on the stairs in the dark of the Shrieking Shack and heard Severus Snape say *your mother was the best person I ever knew*, and *Albus was afraid of you*, and *what did you feel, Potter, on the step*, and Potter say *nothing*.

She had heard *I am going to take it away from you. All of it.*

She had not moved.

She had stood with her back to the wall and her wand in her hand and thought, very clearly and very fast, that this was what she had crossed out. That Snape was going to do what she could not bring herself to do, from the other side. That if Snape closed every door in Potter's head, Potter would never again make fourteen men stand in the snow until they died. That he would never again move Weasley's feelings round like furniture. That he would never again sit on a step and look at her and not know who she was.

She had thought that it might be the right thing.

She had thought it for eleven minutes.

Then she heard Potter fall.

* * *

She went up the last six stairs and stood in the doorway.

The candle was on the floor in the middle of the room. Snape was standing over Potter with his wand pointing down. Potter was on his knees on the boards beside the candle, with his hands flat on the floor, and his head had come up, and he was looking at nothing.

She looked at his face.

She had looked at his face for six and a half years. She had looked at it when he was eleven and lying to her about a troll, and twelve and frightened for her, and thirteen in another world, and fourteen in a locked classroom saying *you're the only person I can't read*. She had looked at it on a step in the snow, smiling at her as though she were a stranger. She knew every single thing it did.

It was not doing any of them.

In front of her eyes, it was going empty. Not the way it went when he was holding an illusion, the small careful stillness she had learned to see through. Not the way it had been on the step, which was a face with nothing behind it that was still, in its way, his. This was something else. This was a face going out like a candle when the wax is all gone. His mouth was slack. His eyes were open and looking at a place on the floorboards a foot in front of him, and they were not looking at it. They were not looking at anything. There was nobody behind them doing the looking.

It was not an illusion. She would have seen through an illusion.

It was real.

She knew one spell that would end it.

She had never cast it. She had never wanted to. She had heard about it from Potter on the floor of a classroom on the fourth floor at two in the morning, a year ago, in the dark, while she sat beside him for an hour and eleven minutes and he told her everything. A spell from the margin of a book. *For enemies.* A bathroom full of red water, and a boy going quiet. And a man kneeling in the water, singing a counter-curse three times, who had written it in the margin in the first place.

She did not think about it.

She would think, afterwards, many times, that she ought to have. That there must have been some other way, some spell, some word, some way of putting herself between them. She would lie awake and go through them all, every spell she had ever learned, in order, the way she went through everything. She would not find one that was fast enough.

She did not think about any of them then.

She raised her wand.

"*Sectumsempra*," said Evelyn.

* * *

It was very quick.

Snape went over sideways. He went over the way a tree goes over, all at once, without bending, and hit the floor beside the four-poster with a sound that shook the dust off the curtains. His wand went out of his hand and rolled away across the boards and stopped against the leg of the bed.

He did not get up.

Something had opened in him, in several places at once. Evelyn saw it and looked away from it and made herself look back. There was a great deal of it, and it was very dark in the candlelight, and it was spreading across the dusty boards round him quite quickly, in no particular shape.

She did not look at it again.

She looked at Potter.

He was still on his knees. But his head had moved. His eyes had come up off the floorboards. They had gone to the man on the floor beside the bed, and they were looking at him, and there was somebody behind them doing it.

Snape's eyes were open.

He was lying on his back, with his head turned on the boards, towards Potter. His face was the colour of the candle, and then whiter, and his mouth was moving. Something was coming out of him. Not blood. Something silver. It was coming from the corners of his eyes, and from his ears, and from his mouth, in thin bright threads, like smoke, like water, like neither, and lying on the air above the floor, and drifting.

Potter crawled to him.

He did it on his hands and knees, across the boards, through the dust, round the candle. He knelt beside Snape. Snape's hand came up off the floor, very slowly, and took hold of the front of Potter's coat, and held it.

"Look," said Snape.

It was barely a voice. Evelyn heard it from the doorway. It was the voice of a man with almost nothing left to say it with.

"Look at me."

Potter looked at him.

He looked down into Snape's face from a foot away, in the candlelight, with Snape's hand on his coat, and Snape looked up. He looked up into Potter's eyes, quite steadily, for a long moment. Evelyn could not see what he saw there. She knew whose eyes they were. She knew what colour they were. She knew who had had them first.

Snape's hand went loose on Potter's coat.

It did not fall. It stayed there, on the cloth, with the fingers still curled, but there was nothing holding it there any more. His eyes stayed open. They stayed on Potter's face. The silver went on drifting up out of them, very slowly, and hanging in the air.

He did not look away.

He would not, now.

* * *

Evelyn stood in the doorway.

She was still holding her wand. It was still raised. It was pointing at the place where Severus Snape had been standing a minute ago, by the candle, over Potter, and there was nobody standing there now, and she had not lowered it.

She looked at her hand.

It was her right hand. It was holding a wand of pear wood, eleven inches, quite rigid, that she had bought in Diagon Alley at the age of eleven, the week after a gold watch was put into her other hand by a woman she loved. She had used it every day for eight and a half years. She had used it to light lamps and to mend books and to do her Charms homework and to take a lock off a ship and to throw wands across the floor of a courtroom. She had used it once to still a cup of water in the air, and the whole of a box full of darkness.

She had used it just now to kill a man.

She looked at the hand that was holding it.

It was quite steady.

She noted that. She noted it from a very long way off, from somewhere at the far end of a corridor in her own head that she had not known was there, where she seemed to be standing, quite calmly, taking notes. *Twenty-four March nineteen ninety-eight. The Shrieking Shack. The upstairs front room. Eleven minutes past midnight. Severus Snape.* She noted that the candle was guttering. She noted that the silver was still coming. She noted that Potter had not moved.

She noted that her hand was perfectly steady.

She did not lower it.
