## Chapter 56: What Was Left

Dumbledore sent for him on the Monday, at four o'clock, and when Harry came up the moving stair the old man was sitting behind his desk with a walking stick propped against the arm of his chair.

It was a plain stick. Blackthorn. It had not been there before.

"Sit down, Harry," said Dumbledore.

Harry sat down.

He had not seen him since the Atrium. Nobody had, except Madam Pomfrey and Professor McGonagall and the Minister, who had come up by Floo on the Saturday morning and stayed for three hours and gone away again looking ill. The *Prophet* had said that Albus Dumbledore had been reinstated as Headmaster of Hogwarts and Chief Warlock of the Wizengamot and Supreme Mugwump of the International Confederation of Wizards, all three, in one sentence, on page one, under a photograph of Lord Voldemort that somebody had taken in the Atrium with a camera they should not have had.

The *Prophet* had not said anything about Dumbledore's face.

Harry looked at it now.

It was very old. It was older than it had been in the Atrium, if that was possible, because in the Atrium it had been changing, and now it had stopped, and what was left was what was left. The skin was thin and soft and folded. The eyes behind the half-moon spectacles were the same blue, but they were deeper in, as though they had been pushed back into the face a little way. The hair and the beard were white. Not silver. White, all through, like paper.

His hands were folded on the desk. They were spotted on the backs.

"I am old," said Dumbledore.

He said it quite simply, the way he might have said that it was Monday.

"I was old on Friday morning," he said. "I am a good deal older now. Madam Pomfrey has been very kind about it, and very honest, which I prefer. I find I need the stick on the stairs. I find I need to sit down rather more than I did. I find…" he paused, "…that I am tired, Harry, in a way I was not tired before. I thought you should hear it from me. You would have read it anyway."

"Yes," said Harry.

"Then I have saved us both some time."

* * *

He told him everything.

He did it the way he had done it in March, at a quarter to one in the morning, after a cathedral of hidden things. He did not talk around it. He sat behind his desk with his hands folded and his stick against his chair and he told Harry, plainly, in order, as he would have told an equal.

He had suspected the scar from the beginning.

He had suspected it on the night of the thirty-first of October, nineteen eighty-one, when Hagrid brought him a baby out of the ruins of a house in Godric's Hollow, a baby with a cut on its forehead in a shape no cut should have been. He had not known. He had suspected. He had watched for it. He had watched for it when the boy talked to a snake at a zoo, which Arabella Figg had reported to him by owl. He had watched for it in the boy's second year, when he spoke to snakes in front of the whole school and opened a door that only Slytherin's heir should have been able to open.

He had been nearly certain by the boy's third year.

He had been certain on a night in June, a year ago, after a graveyard, when the boy had told him about a cold splinter in another boy in another world, and another in himself, and called it a stain.

"I did not tell you," said Dumbledore.

"No."

"I did not tell you because I was afraid." He looked at his spotted hands. "I was afraid of what it would do to you. To know. To be fourteen, fifteen, and to know that there was a piece of the man who murdered your parents living inside you, and that it had been there since you were one year old, and that it might be the reason for some of what you are." He paused. "I was afraid you would go looking for it. I was afraid you would find it. I was afraid of what you would do, if you found it, alone, in the dark, without anybody to tell."

"You could have told me."

"Yes."

"You could have told me in June. In this office. When I told you everything. You let me tell you everything, and you didn't tell me the one thing."

"Yes," said Dumbledore.

"I'd have known what it was. In the Room. When it turned towards the diadem. I'd have known. I'd have told you that night instead of standing there like a…" Harry stopped. "I'd have known what he was calling. At the Veil. I'd have known it was me."

"Yes."

"Would it have changed anything?"

Dumbledore was quiet for a long moment.

"I do not know," he said. "I have thought about it a great deal since Friday. I have thought about very little else. I do not know whether, if you had known, you could have done anything to keep it. I do not know whether you would have wanted to." He looked up. "I do not know whether Nymphadora Tonks would be alive."

Harry did not say anything.

"I am sorry," said Dumbledore. "I am going to say it once, as I said it once before. I am sorry that I did not tell you. It was my decision and I made it badly. I have made a great many decisions about you badly, Harry, and this was the worst of them."

* * *

Harry read him.

He did not have to try. Dumbledore was open, the whole way down, and it was all there, and none of it was hidden. The thing that had been held under water with both hands in December and in March was not held any more. It was at the top. It was the apology. It was the whole shape of it, the suspicion and the watching and the fear and the decision, fourteen and a half years of it, and the guilt, which was enormous and very old and had a great many other things in it as well, things that were not about Harry at all.

And under the guilt, still, small, the other fear. The one from June. The one Harry had noted and not understood.

It was still there. It had not gone. It had not changed.

Harry did not ask about it.

He was angry.

He was angry in the plain, quiet way he got angry, the way that made him quieter and quieter until there was nothing left in his voice at all. He was angry about June, and about December, and about March, and about a man laughing on the edge of a dais and a woman with pink hair standing a little behind him and to one side.

And he understood.

He understood the fear. He had felt it himself, in the Room of Hidden Things, the moment the compass needle in him turned. He had felt what it was like to find something in yourself that you had not known was there and that was not yours. He had stood frozen in the dark for long enough to let Draco Malfoy walk away with a crown. He did not know what he would have done at fourteen, alone, if somebody had told him.

He understood. And he was angry.

He did not try to make one of them win. He sat in the chair in front of the desk and let both of them be there, at the same time, the anger and the understanding, side by side, the way the two Pinnacles had stood in the middle of the Atrium, pressing, neither of them willing to step back.

"I'm still angry," he said.

"Yes," said Dumbledore. "I should think you would be."

"I don't think it's going to stop."

"No. I don't suppose it will." Dumbledore looked at him over the half-moon spectacles. "I should not like it to stop, Harry, if I am honest. I should like you to go on being angry with me for the rest of my life. It is very good for me. Nobody else has dared for a long time."

He said *the rest of my life* quite lightly.

Harry heard it.

He did not say anything about it. Neither did Dumbledore.

* * *

He told Dumbledore the rest of the other world.

He had told him some of it, a year ago, in a rush, with Sirius on his knees. He told him the rest now. The Hog's Head and the other Aberforth. The faces he had held for eight weeks. The other Harry in the Room of Requirement with his stag. The other Evelyn, in her dormitory, with her watch, whom they had never found. The Time Room, and winding a watch forwards, and the other Sirius falling.

Dumbledore listened to all of it.

When Harry had finished, he sat for a while with his eyes closed.

"My brother," he said. "In that world. He told you that I would use you."

"Yes."

"Because I love you."

"Yes."

Dumbledore opened his eyes.

"He was quite right," he said. "He usually is. It is the most irritating thing about him." He reached for the blackthorn stick, and held it across his knees, and looked at it. "Umbridge is in St Mungo's. I went into the forest for her on Saturday. The centaurs gave her to me without a word, which I am told is unusual. She has not spoken since. She lies in a bed on the fourth floor and looks at the ceiling, and if anybody walks past her door in hard shoes, she screams."

Harry did not say anything.

He read himself, the way he read other people, floor by floor. He went down looking for it again, the thing he had looked for in a clearing in the forest. It was not there. He did not know what it said about him that it was not there.

He thought Dumbledore probably knew.

He thought Dumbledore had told him about Umbridge so that he would look.

* * *

He went to Myrtle's bathroom alone that night, at eleven, under the Cloak.

He did not tell Evelyn he was going. He did not tell anybody. He went up through the dark castle to the second floor and along the corridor to the girls' bathroom that nobody used, and went in, and took off the Cloak.

It was the same as it had always been. Cracked mirrors. Chipped sinks. A puddle on the floor under the end cubicle. Moonlight coming in through a high frosted window and lying on the tiles.

He went to the sink in the middle.

He knew it the way he knew the way to the dormitory. He had been here in his second year, frightened, at night, with a girl in a red and gold jumper missing and a man with golden hair coming down a pipe behind him. He had been here in his third year, and his fourth, a great many times, on his way down to a round room with a black stone desk and a cold lamp that lit only for him. He had stood here and spoken to a tap with a small snake scratched into the side of its copper, and the sink had sunk into the floor.

He had never once heard what he said.

He looked at the snake on the tap.

"Open," he said.

He heard it. He heard every letter of it. It was an ordinary English word, said by an ordinary English boy, in a bathroom on the second floor of a castle in Scotland, at a quarter past eleven at night.

Nothing happened.

He tried again. He tried to remember what it had been like. He tried to make his mouth do what it had always done, without his ever having to think about it, the long cold hiss he had spoken all his life. He tried to imagine the snake on the tap was alive, the way he had always done. He looked at it and imagined it moving.

"Open."

It was English.

He tried a third time. He tried it louder. He tried it very quietly. He tried it with his eyes shut. He tried to find, in his mouth, in his throat, anywhere, the place where the other thing had been, the place he had spoken from without knowing it was there.

It was not there.

The sink did not move.

* * *

Myrtle came out of the end cubicle.

She came out slowly, through the door, the way she always did, pearly and transparent, with her pigtails and her thick spectacles, and she floated over to him and looked at him with her head on one side.

Harry read her.

One thin grey note. Sadness, the way it always was with ghosts. And something on top of the sadness, faint, curious.

"You used to be able to do that," said Myrtle.

"Yes."

"You used to come in here and hiss at it, and it would open, and you'd go down, and you wouldn't come back up for hours. And you never once said hello." She sniffed. "Not once. In three years."

"Sorry."

"Can't you do it any more?"

"No," said Harry.

Myrtle looked at him for a moment longer. Then she looked at the sink.

"*He* could," she said. "The other one. The one before. A long time ago." Her voice went very quiet. "He hissed at it on the day I died. I heard him. I was in the cubicle crying, and I heard a boy hissing, in a language I didn't know, and I came out to tell him to go away." She paused. "And that was that."

Harry said nothing.

"I'm glad you can't," said Myrtle. "I think. I'm glad." She floated back towards the cubicle. Then she stopped, and looked back at him over her shoulder, and her thin grey note had something else in it now, something he had not felt in a ghost before.

"You can still come and visit," she said. "If you want. You don't have to hiss. You can just come in."

She went back into the cubicle.

Harry sat down on the floor.

He sat with his back against the cold tiled wall under the sinks, in the moonlight, with his knees up, and he looked at the snake on the tap.

He thought about a round room behind a statue's feet. Shelves on every wall. A cold lamp that lit for him and nobody else. A black stone desk. A small black book that said *the next of us may not be as long alone*. A door with I WILL scratched into it by a boy who could not get in.

He would never see it again.

Nobody would. Nobody alive could open the sink, now, or the door behind the statue. Slytherin's study was shut, with everything in it, the books and the lamp and the desk and the line in the second hand at the end of the black book, and it would stay shut, under the school, in the dark, forever.

He sat there for a long time.

He did not cry. He did not do anything. He sat on the floor of a bathroom nobody used, with his back against the wall, until the moonlight had moved all the way across the tiles from one side to the other, and then he got up and put on the Cloak and went back to bed.

* * *

They buried Nymphadora Tonks on the Thursday, at a small grey church in a village in Devon, under a sky so blue it did not look real.

It was hot. It was the hottest day of the year so far. The church was very old and very small, with a square tower and a lychgate and a churchyard that went down a hill towards a river, and there were roses on the churchyard wall, red ones, so many that they hung over into the lane. There were bees in the roses. There were sheep in the field beyond the river.

It was a beautiful day.

Harry stood at the back of the crowd round the grave and read it, and the day did not match anything that was in it.

There were a great many people. There were Aurors in their best robes, standing very straight. There were the Order, and the Weasleys, all of them, Mrs Weasley crying already. There was Moody, leaning on his staff, with his magical eye fixed on the coffin and not moving. There was Kingsley. There were people Harry did not know, a great many of them, from school and from the Auror office and from a life she had had that he had known nothing about. There was a Muggle woman with a pram who had stopped in the lane, and did not know what she was looking at, and stayed anyway, very quietly, with her hand on the handle of the pram.

There were her parents.

Her father was a big fair man with a kind face and a great deal of belly, in a black suit that did not fit him, and he was holding his wife's hand. Harry read him. It was grief, and it was so simple and so complete that there was nothing else in him at all, not even surprise. He had been a father for twenty-three years, and now he was not, and that was all he was, that day, at that grave.

His wife made Harry go still.

She was tall and dark, with heavy-lidded eyes and long dark hair. She looked like the woman on the front page of the *Prophet* in January, among ten others, laughing. She looked like the woman on the edge of a dais, laughing, with her wand raised. For one second, standing at the back of the crowd in the heat, Harry could not breathe.

Then he read her.

It was nothing like her sister. It was nothing like anything in her sister at all. It was grief, very deep and very quiet and very held, the grief of a woman who had been told she would lose everything when she married a Muggle-born at eighteen and had decided, then, that she would rather lose everything than not. And she had not lost everything. She had had twenty-three years. She had had a daughter who fell over bins and changed her hair to match her moods and was happy all the way down.

And now her own sister had stood in the room where it happened, and laughed.

Andromeda Tonks was holding her husband's hand very hard. She was looking at the coffin. She did not look at anybody else.

* * *

Lupin stood at the end of the grave.

He stood a little apart from everybody, on his own, in shabby robes, with his hands at his sides. He did not cry. He did not say anything. When the coffin went down, and the priest, who was a small round wizard with a Devon accent, said the words, Lupin did not move.

Harry read him.

It was not grief. Or it was, but it was something underneath grief, something Harry had felt only once before, in a ward on the fourth floor of St Mungo's on Christmas afternoon, in a woman in a bed with all her lights put out.

It was stillness.

Not Evelyn's silence. Not the stillness of somebody who has cleared the table. It was the stillness of a clock that has stopped. Everything in Remus Lupin had been going, ticking, moving forward, very quietly, for some time. Since Christmas, perhaps. Since a jumper knitted badly for Kingsley and a bottle of mead. Harry had not noticed. He had not been looking.

It had stopped.

Lupin stood at the end of the grave with the bees in the roses behind him and the sheep in the field and the river going by at the bottom of the hill, and his life had stopped, and he did not seem to know what to do with the rest of it.

Harry looked away.

* * *

Sirius stood beside the grave.

He stood very straight. He was wearing new black robes that Mrs Weasley had made him buy, and his hair was cut short, and he was standing at the very edge of the grave, closer than anybody except her parents, with his hands behind his back like a soldier.

He was alive.

Harry read him. He had read him all week. He had read him in the kitchen at Grimmauld Place, where the Order had taken him on Friday night, sitting at the long table for eleven hours without moving, and he had read him at breakfast on Saturday, and on Sunday, when the *Prophet* came.

It was the same every time.

There was grief. There was a great deal of it, for his cousin's daughter, whom he had last seen as a small girl with green hair before Azkaban, and had met again as a grown woman eleven months ago and loved at once, the way he loved everybody, completely and at full volume.

And under the grief, all week, there had been a question.

It was very quiet. Sirius did not ask it out loud. He did not ask it of anybody. But it was there every time Harry read him, under everything, steady, the way Theo's sadness was always there at the bottom of Theo.

*Why am I alive?*

Harry knew the shape of it exactly. He had read it in a man on his knees by an arch, looking at the veil, which was still moving, and at the empty air twelve inches to the left of where he had stood.

They walked back up through the churchyard together afterwards, the two of them, behind everybody else, slowly, between the graves.

"You did something," said Sirius. "By the arch."

He did not look at Harry when he said it. He was looking at the roses.

"Yes," said Harry.

"What?"

Harry told him.

He told him about the other world. Not all of it. The part that mattered. The Death Chamber in June nineteen ninety-six of a world two years ahead, and a man laughing on the edge of a dais, and a jet of red light. He told him about the look on the other Sirius's face as he fell. Surprise. Not fear.

He told him about the twelve inches.

Sirius stopped walking.

They were by the lychgate. The others had gone through it, into the lane, where the Weasleys were standing in a cluster round Mrs Weasley and the Muggle woman with the pram had finally gone on. There was nobody near them.

"So I'm alive," said Sirius slowly, "because there was another one of me. Who died."

"Yes."

"And you watched him."

"Yes."

"And you kept it. All this time. What she did. Where she aimed."

"Yes."

Sirius looked at him.

He looked at him for a long time, there by the lychgate in the heat with the bees in the roses, and Harry read him, and the question was still there. It had not gone. It had changed. It was not *why am I alive* any more. It had turned into something else, and it was aimed at Harry, and it was so large and so tangled that Harry could not get to the bottom of it, which had never once happened to him with Sirius.

Love. Enormous. Aimed at Harry, at full volume, the way it always was.

Grief, for Tonks.

And under both of them, something Harry had felt in Sirius once before, on the battlements of the West Tower, two years ago, when he had said *he'd have been a bit frightened of you, too*.

"Did it…" said Sirius. He stopped. "Harry. Did it… the red one. Going into the veil. Did that… Was she…"

"I don't know," said Harry.

"You don't know."

"I don't know," said Harry. "I've thought about it every night. I don't know if he was aiming at her or at nothing or at you. I don't know if it would have gone the same way if the red one hadn't gone where it went. I don't know." He looked at the roses. "Nobody does."

Sirius kept quiet for a while.

Then he put his hand on the back of Harry's neck, the way Harry had seen Mr Weasley do it to Ron, and held on.

He did not say anything else. Neither did Harry.

They stood there by the lychgate with the bees going in and out of the roses, until Mrs Weasley called them from the lane.

* * *

Evelyn was on the Astronomy Tower on the Saturday night, at the top of the stairs, with her back against the parapet and her knees up and a book she was not reading.

He came up at a quarter to eleven. He had known she would be there. She went up there on the last Saturday of every term, and had done since her second year, to look at the stars she had been made to learn by heart for Professor Sinistra and had never once found interesting.

He sat down beside her against the parapet.

Neither of them said anything for a long time.

The sky was very clear. There was no moon. The lake was black below them, and the forest blacker, and somewhere a long way off there was a light in Hagrid's hut, which had not been lit since April and was lit now.

Harry read the castle under them.

He did it without meaning to. It was what he did, the way you breathe. It was a long way down, the whole castle, every floor of it, and it was loud, as it always was, a great slow noise of a thousand people asleep and awake. Fear, still. A great deal of fear, still, since Friday. Grief, here and there, in pockets, where people had known her. Relief. Somewhere in Gryffindor Tower, Ron Weasley, asleep, with something in him that was not quite a nightmare. Somewhere in the dungeons, Theo, awake, very calm, standing on an edge. Somewhere in the hospital wing, Madam Pomfrey, tired.

He could read all of it.

He had been able to read all of it since the stairs in the Ministry, with blood in his eyes. He had noticed it then, running up the third flight, and he had not stopped noticing it since. Every time he came into a room and the room was there, loud, every floor of every person in it. Every time.

He had been afraid it would go.

He had not told anybody that. He had not even told himself, quite. But on the stairs, with the cold torn out of him and the places where it had been open, he had felt for it, the way you feel for a wallet in a crowded street, without thinking. And it had been there.

He did not say anything about it.

He sat against the parapet beside Evelyn and read the castle and did not say anything about it, and beside him the silence was steady and quiet and complete, a table with nothing on it, the way it always was, the one thing in the whole castle he could not read.

She did not say anything about it either.

He knew she knew. She had been on the stairs. She had been beside him on his left all the way up, and she had seen him put his hand out to the wall on the third flight and stop, for one second, and then go on. She had seen his face. She had worked it out, he supposed, before he had.

She did not say anything.

They sat there for a long time.

Then Evelyn turned a page of the book she was not reading and said, without looking up:

"Your History of Magic result is going to be appalling."

Harry looked at her.

"You fell asleep," said Evelyn. "In the middle of the goblin rebellions. I asked Professor Tofty's assistant. You wrote two paragraphs on the eighteenth century and one line on the Statute of Secrecy, and then you put your head down on your arm for eleven minutes and woke up and went white and wrote two more paragraphs in a hand she described as 'agitated'." She turned another page. "And then you got up and walked out of the exam and into a Ministry and through a wall."

"I got the date of the Statute right."

"You did," agreed Evelyn. "You got it right eleven times on a train in September. I made sure." She closed the book. "It won't help. You'll get a P. Possibly a D. I'd be surprised by an A."

"Thank you."

"I'm only telling you so you're not surprised."

"I appreciate it."

"Somebody has to," said Evelyn.

She put the book down on the stone beside her, and leaned her head back against the parapet, and looked up at the stars she had never once found interesting.

He sat beside her and did not read anything.

* * *

Sirius was cleared on the Tuesday.

It was in the *Prophet*. It was on the front page, under a photograph, and the photograph was the one from twelve years ago, the one from the wanted posters, the gaunt wild man with the long matted hair, shouting silently out of the frame. But over the top of it, in letters two inches high, there was one word.

INNOCENT.

The article underneath said that Peter Pettigrew, believed killed by Sirius Black in nineteen eighty-one, had been seen alive by no fewer than twenty-three Aurors and the Minister for Magic himself in the Atrium of the Ministry of Magic at the side of He-Who-Must-Not-Be-Named. It said that the Minister had ordered an immediate review of the case. It said that the review had found no record of a trial. It said that the Ministry offered Sirius Black its sincere apologies and an undisclosed sum in compensation, and that Mr Black was not available for comment.

Mr Black was available for comment. He was in the kitchen at Grimmauld Place, reading it out loud to Kreacher, who was hiding in the boiler cupboard.

Three weeks later, in July, after somebody at the Ministry let slip to a reporter that Sirius Black had been in the Department of Mysteries that night, fighting, the *Prophet* would run the same photograph again, the old one, the wild one. Somebody at the *Prophet* had apparently decided it was the only one they had. And over the top of it, in letters two inches high, it would say HERO.

Sirius would have it framed. He would hang it in the hall at Grimmauld Place, directly opposite his mother's portrait, so that she could look at it every time she opened her curtains.

* * *

Dumbledore told Harry on the Wednesday, the last full day of term, in his office, with the stick against the arm of his chair.

"You may live with your godfather," he said. "If you would like to."

Harry looked at him.

He had asked for it. He had asked for it at thirteen, on a night in June, with Sirius flying away on a hippogriff over the Forbidden Forest. He had asked for it at fourteen, without saying it, in a hospital wing with a dog on the floor beside his bed. He had been told *Privet Drive* both times. He had been told about a protection, an old one, in his blood and in his aunt's, set on the night his mother died.

"What about the protection?" he said.

Dumbledore looked at his hands.

"It matters less," he said, "than it did."

"Why?"

"Because he has your blood." Dumbledore did not look up. "He took it in a churchyard a year ago, to make himself a body. Your mother's protection lives in it too, now. In him. It will not keep him from you any more, not as it did. And the other thing it kept from you…" he paused, "…has gone back to him. On Friday. At the Veil." He looked up. "There is very little left for Privet Drive to do, Harry. I am told your aunt said, on the night of the Dementors, that you should stay. I think she may have meant it. I think it was very brave of her. But I do not think it is necessary any longer."

Harry read him.

He did not have to look hard. It was right at the top, under the tiredness, which was the whole of him now.

It was cost.

It was not like the cost in the Atrium. That had been years. This was something else, smaller and much older. It was the cost of a man who has held one thing very tightly for fifteen years, a house in Surrey and a boy in a cupboard and an old protection in his blood, and who has had to let go of it because it no longer works. It had cost him to say it. It had cost him a great deal. He had believed in that protection the way he believed in very few things.

And it had been his decision, fifteen years ago, on a doorstep, in the cold.

"Thank you," said Harry.

"Don't thank me," said Dumbledore. "Thank your aunt. And go and tell your godfather before he hears it from somebody else and is offended for a week."

* * *

Sirius was on the platform at King's Cross.

He was standing in plain sight, at the end of platform nine and three-quarters, where the train came in, among the parents. He was not hiding. He was not a dog. He was standing there in broad daylight with no disguise at all, and people were looking at him, and some of them were pointing, and some of them were whispering behind their hands, and one witch in a purple hat had gone up to him and shaken his hand and said something to him with her face very pink.

He was wearing a coat.

It was a terrible coat. Harry could see it from the train window, before the train had even stopped. It was long and leather and very old and had clearly been bought from a Muggle shop on the Portobello Road by a man who had been in Azkaban for twelve years and did not know that it was no longer nineteen seventy-eight. It had a fur collar. The fur collar was orange.

He was waving.

He was waving with both arms, over his head, at the train, at every window of it at once, as though he did not know which one Harry was in and did not care, as though if he waved at all of them one of them would be right.

Harry read him from the train.

He did not have to try. He had never had to try with Sirius. It came across the platform at him the way it had come down the stairs at Grimmauld Place eleven months ago, like a dog let off a lead, and it was joy. It was not the whole of him. Harry could feel the other things under it, the grief and the question and the thing from the battlements. They were there. They would be there for a long time.

But on the top, at full volume, filling the platform, there was joy.

Harry got off the train.

He did not remember getting off. He remembered Theo behind him, saying *write*, and Millicent hitting him on the arm, and Hermione saying something about the holidays and Ron saying something about Hermione. He remembered Neville's grandmother, on the platform, in her vulture hat, looking at him for a long moment with an expression he could not read and then nodding at him, once, very slightly.

He remembered Evelyn.

She had looked at Sirius in his coat, from the train door, for quite a long time. Then she had looked at Harry.

"That collar," she said, "is orange."

"I know."

"I want that noted."

"It's noted," said Harry, and got off the train, and Sirius was there, and the coat was there, and the orange collar, and Harry was inside it before he had finished getting down the step.

* * *

The letter came the next morning, at Grimmauld Place, at ten past seven.

It came by the large grey Selwyn owl, which landed on the kitchen table among the breakfast things and looked at Sirius's toast as though it were something it had been asked to step over. Sirius was frying eggs. Kreacher was somewhere upstairs, muttering. The kitchen was warm, and smelled of bacon, and the window over the sink was open to the yard, and somewhere in London a church clock was striking seven, ten minutes late.

Harry took the letter off the owl's leg.

It was one sheet of good cream paper, folded once. Her small square hand.

There was only one line on it.

He read it, and then he read it again, and then he folded it twice and put it in his pocket, and sat at the kitchen table with Sirius's eggs going cold in front of him and did not eat them.

*Lucan has the Mark, and Father has accepted a position.*
