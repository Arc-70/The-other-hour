## Chapter 50: Christmas at Grimmauld Place

He was a snake.

He knew it the way you know things in dreams, without being told. He was long and heavy and he was moving along a stone floor, and the floor was cold under him, and he could feel the cold all along the length of his body at once. He could not see very well. He did not need to. He could feel the warmth of things through the dark, faint and bright, and there was a warmth ahead of him, at the end of a long bare corridor with no windows, sitting slumped on a chair beside a plain black door.

It was a man.

He was asleep. His chin was on his chest and his glasses had slid down his nose, and there was a cloak over his knees that had slipped half off, and he had red hair, thinning on top.

The snake did not hurry.

Harry felt what it felt. It was very simple. It was a hunger, and it was a pleasure, and the pleasure was not the snake's. It came down into the snake from somewhere else, from far away, cold and glad and patient, and the snake moved because it was told to, and the man on the chair woke up.

He saw the snake. He fumbled for his wand.

The snake struck.

* * *

"Harry. *Harry.* Wake up."

It was Theo. He was standing by the bed in his pyjamas with his wand lit, and his face was white, and Harry was sitting up with his hands over his scar, and he did not know when he had sat up, and somebody in the dormitory was shouting, and it was him.

"Mr Weasley," said Harry.

"What?"

"It's Mr Weasley. Ron's dad. In the Ministry. It's bitten him. He's on the floor. There's blood everywhere."

He was out of the bed. He was pulling on his shoes without socks. Theo was saying something and Crabbe was awake and swearing and Draco Malfoy had pulled back his curtains and was looking at Harry with an expression that Harry did not stop to read.

He ran.

He ran up out of the dungeons and through the entrance hall and up the marble staircase in his pyjamas with his shoes untied, and he did not know the password to the Headmaster's office, and when he got to the stone gargoyle he did not stop. He said, very loudly, "It's Harry Potter, Arthur Weasley's been attacked, *open*," and the gargoyle looked at him for a moment and leapt aside.

It was ten past midnight.

* * *

Dumbledore was awake.

He was sitting behind his desk in a magnificent purple and gold dressing gown, and he was not reading, and he was not writing. He was simply sitting there, very still, as though he had been waiting for somebody.

"Harry," he said.

Harry told him.

He told him fast and in order, the corridor, the black door, the chair, the man asleep, the snake. He told him that he had been the snake, and that the snake had been told what to do by something far away that was pleased. He did not tell him about the night of the fifteenth. He did not tell him that he had been going out along the scar for three weeks, every few nights, on purpose. Dumbledore knew about the notes. He must have guessed where they came from. He had never asked.

Harry did not tell him now.

Dumbledore listened. He did not interrupt. And when Harry had finished, he turned in his chair and looked up at the portraits on the walls, and said, quite calmly, "Everard. Dilys."

Two of the portraits opened their eyes.

"Everard, raise the alarm. Make sure he is found by the right people. Dilys, St Mungo's. Tell them to expect him."

The portraits went out of their frames.

Then Dumbledore turned back to Harry.

Harry read him. He was not closed. He had not been fully closed to Harry since a night in June, not when it was only the two of them. And what was in him now, at the top, was alarm. It was very large and very controlled, the alarm of a man who has heard something he expected and hoped not to hear.

Under the alarm there was something else.

Harry could not get at it. It was held down. Held rather than shut, the way you hold something under water with both hands, and it was very large, and it was about Harry.

"Was I the snake," said Harry, "or was I watching it?"

Dumbledore looked at him for a moment over his spectacles.

"You were watching it," he said. "From inside. I think."

"From inside what?"

Dumbledore did not answer that.

He looked at Harry for a little longer. Then he stood up, and went to the window, and looked out at the snow, and Harry read the held thing push up against his hands and be pushed back down.

"I will need to send you to Grimmauld Place," said Dumbledore. "Tonight. With the Weasleys. Professor McGonagall is waking them now. There is a Portkey." He did not turn round. "Harry. I would like you to do something for me. I would like you not to go to sleep again until you are there."

"Why?"

"Because I would like to know where you are," said Dumbledore, "when you are asleep."

* * *

Mr Weasley lived.

He lived because the portrait of Everard had shouted in an empty corridor of the Ministry until somebody came, and the somebody had been the right person, and had found him on the floor outside a black door with his blood all over the stone, and had got him to St Mungo's before it was too late.

They had the news at Grimmauld Place at half past five in the morning, round the kitchen table, with the fire lit and Sirius making tea and nobody having slept.

Mrs Weasley put her face in her hands and cried.

Harry read the kitchen. Fred and George, who had been loud with fear all night, went quiet at once, the way a room goes quiet when a radio is switched off. Ginny was relieved so hard she was nearly sick. Ron had not let himself feel anything all night. He let himself feel it now, all at once, and Harry looked away from him, because it was not his to see.

Then Mrs Weasley took her face out of her hands and looked at Harry.

She did not say anything. She got up from the table and came round it and put her arms round him, and held on, very hard, for a long time, and he stood there in the kitchen with his arms at his sides and let her.

He read her.

He could not help it. He had read her love before, the great tired love with five sons in it, and a daughter, and him, somewhere, without being asked. He read it now and it was not the same. He was not somewhere in it any more. He was in the middle.

"Thank you," said Mrs Weasley into his hair. "Oh, thank you, Harry dear."

He did not know what to do with his arms.

After a while he put them round her.

* * *

It was the best Christmas Grimmauld Place had ever had.

Sirius said so. He said it on Christmas Eve, standing on a chair in the hall putting up holly, with the portrait of his mother asleep behind her curtains and a strand of tinsel round his neck like a scarf, and he said it again on Christmas morning, and he was right.

He had not had a full house since he was sixteen.

Harry read him all week. The fury was still there. It was always there, under everything, the fury at the walls and the stairs and the portrait and the door he could not walk out of. But it had gone down. It had gone so far down that it was nearly quiet, and on top of it, loud and warm and filling the whole house from the kitchen to the attic, there was joy. Sirius had people. He had people in every room. He had the Weasleys and Hermione and Lupin and Tonks, who came on Christmas Eve with a bottle of mead and a jumper she had knitted herself, very badly, for Kingsley. And he had Harry.

He sang.

He sang carols all week, loudly and badly, in every room he went into, and when he did not know the words he made them up. He sang "Good King Wenceslas" so many times on Christmas Eve that Fred and George charmed a bauble to shout "LOOK OUT" every time he got to the feast of Stephen, and he laughed so hard he had to sit down on the stairs.

Ron and Hermione thawed.

It was slow, and nobody said anything about it. It was in small things. It was Ron sitting next to Harry at breakfast on the third morning without seeming to think about it. It was Hermione bringing him a book she had finished, a Muggle one, a thick paperback about a girl in Oxford in an alternate world, and saying "You'll like the bit with the bear," and going away again before he could say anything.

It was Ron, on Christmas Eve, very late, in the bedroom with the lights out, saying into the dark, "You didn't have to go to Dumbledore. You could've just gone back to sleep. Nobody would've known."

Harry did not answer.

"I mean. You did, though," said Ron.

"Yes."

There was a long silence.

"Right," said Ron. "Well. Night."

Harry read him for a while after that, in the dark, until he was asleep. There was no wariness in him at all.

* * *

They went to St Mungo's on Christmas afternoon.

It was in a department store on a London street that had been closed for years, Purge and Dowse Ltd, with a dummy in the window in a green nylon pinafore. Tonks went up to the glass and said, "Wotcher, we're here to see Arthur Weasley," and the dummy nodded and beckoned, and they walked through the glass.

Mr Weasley was on the first floor, in a ward called Dai Llewellyn, in a bed by the window, and he was sitting up and eating turkey from a tray, and he was very pleased to see all of them. He had a great many bandages on him. He wanted to talk about stitches.

"Stitches?" said Mrs Weasley, in a voice that made the whole ward go quiet.

Harry left before it got worse.

He said he would find a cup of tea. He went out of the ward and along the corridor, and up a flight of stairs that he had not meant to go up, because there was something at the top of it he had read from the bottom, and he wanted to know what it was.

The fourth floor was the Janus Thickey Ward. Long-term spell damage. The door was locked from the outside and had a small window in it, and through the window Harry saw a long room with beds, and people in the beds, and a Healer going between them with a tray.

And Neville.

He was sitting by a bed near the end of the ward, with his grandmother beside him in a hat with a stuffed vulture on it. In the bed there was a woman. She was very thin. Her face was white and her hair was white, and it was thin and wispy, and she was looking at Neville.

Harry had not known.

He had read Neville every day for four years. He had read the fear and the kindness and the shame. He had felt, under all of it, a long way down, something very private and very steady that Neville never let anybody near, and he had never once known what it was.

He read the woman in the bed.

It was very hard. There was almost nothing there. It was like reading a house where all the lights had been put out, room after room, for years, until there was nobody left to put them on again. Most of the rooms were empty. Most of them had been empty for a very long time.

But in one room there was a light.

It was small, and it was clear, and it was entirely present. It was not confused or frightened. It did not know what day it was or where it was or who anybody else was. It did not know its own name. It only knew one thing, and it knew it completely, and the thing was the boy sitting by the bed.

She loved him.

That was all there was in her. It was all that was left. A small clear love for her son, in a mind that had nothing else in it at all.

She held out her hand to Neville. There was something in it. Neville took it, and looked at it, and put it in his pocket, and his grandmother said something sharp and kind, and Neville's ears went red.

Harry went down the stairs before anybody saw him.

* * *

He did not go back to Dai Llewellyn at once.

He went the wrong way at the bottom of the stairs and found himself in a corridor on the third floor that was much brighter than the others, with large windows and rails along both walls, and a sign that said REHABILITATION, and at the far end of it there was a man walking towards him with a stick.

He was walking very slowly. He was walking very carefully. He was wearing lilac robes and a lilac cravat, and his hair was golden and beautifully waved, and he was walking with a polished black stick with a silver top in a way that made it look less like a stick than like a thing you would carry to the opera.

He saw Harry. He stopped.

"Good heavens," said Gilderoy Lockhart. "Good *heavens*. My dear boy!"

Harry stood still.

He read him. He had read him last in a tunnel under the school, in the dark, with the roof coming down. He had read him then holding a spell over his head with both arms, with the stones grinding, and he had felt in him a bravery so plain and so complete that it had made Harry go on.

He read him now.

It was the same. Under the vanity, which was enormous and cheerful and filled the whole corridor like scent, Lockhart was exactly the same. He was pleased, very pleased, aimed at Harry. And he was brave. He was brave all the way down, the way Tonks was happy all the way down, and he had been lying on his back in a hospital for two and a half years and it had not made any difference at all.

"Professor," said Harry.

"Gilderoy, please. I'm hardly anybody's professor now." Lockhart came the last few yards and held out his hand, and Harry shook it. His grip was very strong. "Look at you. Look at you! You've *grown*. Fifteen, is it? And a champion! I read all about it. I had the *Prophet* brought in every morning. The dragon! My dear fellow. The *dragon*."

"How's your back?"

"Oh, my back." Lockhart waved the stick. "Gone entirely to pieces and put back together by a team of the most charming Healers in Britain, and I can now walk the length of this corridor in four minutes and twenty seconds, which is a hospital record, and I can sit up in a chair for three hours, and I have learned to knit." He leaned closer. "Don't tell anybody that. They'd never let me live it down."

Harry laughed.

He did not mean to. Lockhart beamed at him.

"And I have news," said Lockhart. He lowered his voice, which meant that it could now only be heard at the far end of the corridor. "I had a visitor. In November. A certain gentleman with a long white beard and a great many opinions about jam. And another gentleman, with a wooden leg, who looked at my get-well cards for twenty minutes for hidden curses and then told me I was *soft*." His eyes shone. "I have been *approached*, my dear boy. By a certain *Order*. With regard to my *future usefulness*." He tapped the side of his nose. "I couldn't possibly say any more."

"You just did."

"Did I? Oh dear." Lockhart did not look as though he minded at all. "Well. You're on our side, aren't you? Of course you are. I've always said so. I said so to Severus Snape once, in the staffroom, in front of everybody, and he looked at me as though I were something on his boot." He sighed happily. "It's going to be *thrilling*. I haven't been thrilled since the Himalayas. I'll be out by the summer, they say. Or the autumn. Certainly by the autumn."

Harry read him.

He was thrilled. He was thrilled the way a boy is thrilled, simply and completely, about a war. And under the thrill, quite deep, quite quiet, there was something else, and it was not vanity at all.

It was readiness. The same readiness Harry had felt in a tunnel, under a roof that was coming down.

"I'm glad you're better," said Harry.

"So am I, dear boy," said Lockhart. "So am I. Now go and find your friends. And Harry." He put his hand on Harry's shoulder. "People see what you show them. Remember? I told you that. Second year. Everybody thought I was talking about hair."

"I remember."

"I wasn't talking about hair," said Lockhart, and winked, and went on down the corridor with his stick, very slowly, in a way that made it look as though he were walking onto a stage.

* * *

Kreacher came back on Christmas night at a quarter to eleven.

Harry was in the kitchen. Everybody else had gone up. He had stayed to finish the book Hermione had given him, sitting at the end of the long table by the range with his feet on the fender, and he was on the last chapter when he felt the house-elf come in through the back of the house.

He had not known Kreacher was out.

Nobody had. Kreacher had been missing since Christmas Eve, since the afternoon, when Sirius had found him in the drawing room trying to hide a photograph of Bellatrix Lestrange under his rag, and had lost his temper, and shouted at him. *Get out.* Harry had heard it from the stairs. Sirius had shouted it twice. *Get out, get OUT.*

Everybody had thought Kreacher was sulking in the attic.

He came in now through the scullery door, very quietly, and stopped when he saw Harry, and looked at him with his great pale eyes, and Harry read him.

The hatred was there. It was always there. The grief was there, for the woman in the portrait and for the boy whose face Harry did not know.

But on top of both of them, tonight, there was something new.

It was glee.

It was very small and very secret, and it was the glee of a servant who has been somewhere he was not meant to go and done something nobody knows about, and has come back, and is pleased. It was bright and private and it made his ears quiver. It was aimed at nobody in the house. It was aimed outside, the same way the devotion at the bottom of him had always been aimed. It was aimed at the same place.

"Where have you been?" said Harry.

"Kreacher has been in the house," said Kreacher. "Kreacher was told to get out of the room. Kreacher got out." He bowed, very low, with his nose almost on the floor. "Kreacher has been cleaning. Kreacher is always cleaning. Nobody thanks Kreacher."

He went out through the other door, towards the stairs, muttering.

Harry sat with the book open on his knees.

* * *

He told Sirius the next morning.

"He was out," said Harry. "Last night. He came in through the scullery. He said he was told to get out, so he got out."

Sirius was frying bacon. He laughed.

"I told him to get out of the drawing room, not the *house*. He can't leave the house, Harry. I've told you. He's bound to it. Bound to the family." He turned a rasher. "He'll have been in the cellar. There's a boiler cupboard down there he sleeps in. He's got a nest. Old blankets. Pictures of my mother."

"He was pleased about something."

"He's always pleased about something. He's pleased when I stub my toe. He was pleased for a week when I got splinched on the stairs." Sirius flipped another rasher. "Honestly. Don't worry about Kreacher. He's a mad old elf in a house full of mad old things. He's not going to do anything."

Harry read him.

Certainty. Large and plain and without anything under it.

He thought about the glee, and the direction of it. He thought about *Miss Narcissa*, and the photograph of Bellatrix, and *get out, get OUT*.

Then Ron came in wanting bacon, and Tonks came in with her hair bright green for Christmas, and Mrs Weasley came in with the news that Mr Weasley would be home by the New Year, and the kitchen filled up, and was loud, and was warm.

Harry did not mention it again.

He thought, afterwards, that he should have gone down to the cellar and looked in the boiler cupboard at the nest of old blankets. He thought that he should have asked what was in it. He thought it on a great many nights.

He did not think it then.

* * *

The launderette in Ludlow was on Corve Street, two doors down from the bookshop, and Evelyn went into it at five past nine on the morning of the twenty-second of December with a bag of washing she did not need washed, and came out of the back of it four minutes later without the bag.

She had told her mother she was going into town for books.

She had told the woman at the launderette nothing. The woman at the launderette did not ask. She had a radio on the counter tuned to Radio Shropshire and a cigarette going in a saucer, and she had looked at Evelyn's bag and said "Service wash, love? Be ready at four," and Evelyn had said "Thank you," and gone out through the door marked PRIVATE into a yard full of bins.

Varga was in the yard.

He was standing by the bins in a long dark coat and a fur hat, very thin, very old, holding a child's black leather shoe with a buckle. He looked at her with the shame that was always in his face when he looked at her, and took her hand without saying anything, because she was of the blood and he was not, and touched the shoe.

The yard went.

* * *

Her grandfather knew.

She could tell before he said a word. She came up the three hundred and eighty-one steps behind Varga, counting, with the snow coming in through the slits in the wall, and the cell door opened, and he was standing.

He always stood when she came in. He was a hundred and twelve now and he weighed less than she did, and he stood, in his grey robe, with his white hair like thistledown, and he looked at her. But it was a different look. It was not the look of an old man who has been waiting since Tuesday for somebody to talk to.

It was the look of a man who has been reading the newspapers.

"It has begun," he said.

"Yes."

"The *Prophet* says nothing. The *Prophet* says Mr Fudge has every confidence." He sat down on the chair, the only chair, folding himself onto it the way he did. "The *Prophet* also says that a family in Kent has gone on holiday, and that a man called Avery has been found dead in a ditch in Wiltshire of natural causes, and that the Dementors at Azkaban have been reported, by a guard who has since been retired, to be restless." He looked at her. "Sit, child. Sit. Tell me what Mr Fudge does not."

She sat on the end of the bed. She told him.

She did not tell him all of it. She told him about Umbridge. She told him about the Ministry and Fudge and the hearing in Courtroom Ten, which Potter had described in a letter in a code that she had broken in under a minute. She told him about Mr Weasley and the snake, because it had been in the *Prophet*, as an accident.

He listened with his eyes half closed, his long white hands quite still on his knees.

"And the boy," he said, when she had finished. "What does the boy do?"

She had known he would ask. He always asked. He had asked every time, at Christmas and at Easter, and every time she had told him only what was in the newspaper.

She looked at him.

She thought about the Room on the seventh floor, and twenty-six names, and the third quarter of an hour on the third Wednesday, and *8.47 p.m. P. happy*.

She wanted to see his face.

"He teaches," she said.

Her grandfather opened his eyes.

"There's a group. Twenty-six of us. Every house. We meet on Wednesdays in a room nobody else can find, and he teaches us to fight. Properly. Disarming, Stunning, Shield Charms. He's going to do Patronuses after Christmas." She kept her voice quite flat. "He reads every one of them. All at once. He knows why each of them is getting it wrong. He tells them one sentence each and walks away. In the first quarter of an hour on the third night, eleven Disarming Charms out of fifty worked. In the third quarter, thirty-eight."

Her grandfather did not say anything.

She watched his face. She watched it very carefully, the way she would have watched a page for an error. It was an old face, all bones, and it did not move very much, and it moved now.

It moved the way a face moves when somebody you have never met has done something you would have been proud to do. It warmed. It warmed all the way through, for a moment, like a window when the sun comes out from behind a cloud, and his mouth moved, and he very nearly smiled.

Then it stopped.

She watched it stop. She watched him catch himself at it. She watched him notice that he was proud of a fifteen-year-old boy he had never met, in a country he would never see again, and she watched him be annoyed. It was quite a small annoyance, and it was aimed entirely at himself, and it went across his face like a cloud going back over the sun.

"Thirty-eight," he said, rather stiffly.

"Out of fifty."

"I heard you the first time."

Evelyn did not smile.

She was very nearly certain she did not smile.

* * *

"Now," said her grandfather. "Stillness."

He had a candle.

Varga had brought it. It was an ordinary white household candle, the sort the Muggles in Ludlow bought in packets of six for power cuts, and he had set it on the floor in the middle of the cell, and lit it, and it burned very straight in the cold still air.

"You have done this once by accident," he said. "In this room, a year ago. A teacup that would not become a tortoise. Everything around it stopped, for a moment. The snow. The lamp. The tick of your watch." He looked at the candle. "You did not do it. That is the important thing. You did not *do* anything. You stopped. And the world, very briefly, stopped with you."

"I don't know how I did it."

"No. You will. Look at the flame."

She looked at the flame.

"Do not try to put it out. Do not try to move it. Do not try to do anything to it at all. That is the mistake everyone makes, the first hundred times. They think it is a spell. It is not a spell. A spell is a thing you do. This is a thing you are." He leaned back. "You have been doing it every morning of your life since you were five years old. Your grandmother taught you. She did not know she was teaching you this. She thought she was teaching you to keep your thoughts. But a person who can clear a table every morning of every single thing on it, so completely that nobody alive can find a crumb, is a person who knows how to make something stop."

Evelyn looked at the flame.

She thought about the watch. Twelve turns of the crown. Every thought, every feeling, folded, put in a drawer. The table clear.

She cleared it.

She did it the way she did it every morning, without thinking, one turn at a time. Her mother. Her father across the dinner table. Lucan's door, shut. The launderette. Potter in the middle of the Room with his sleeves pushed up. One at a time, folded, in the drawer. Twelve. The table clear.

She looked at the flame with nothing on the table at all.

It stopped.

It did not go out. It did not flicker. It stopped. It stood up from the wick as straight and still as a thing made of glass, and the light from it did not waver on the walls, and the little shimmer of heat above it, that had been there a moment ago, was not there any more.

She held it for four seconds.

Then something came back onto the table, she did not know what, and the flame moved, and the heat shimmered, and the light went back to wavering on the walls.

"Four seconds," said her grandfather.

"Yes."

"I did it for one, the first time. I was fifty." He looked at her with an expression she could not read. "Again."

* * *

They did it all afternoon.

The flame, for six seconds, then nine, then fourteen. Then he took the china cup that Varga had brought up with a flask of tea, and held it out at arm's length over the stone floor, and let go.

"Stop it," he said.

She did not stop it. It fell and broke. Varga, without being asked, mended it.

"Again."

It broke four times. On the fifth, she cleared the table as it left his fingers, and it stopped in the air a foot from the floor, and hung there, quite still, the way the flame had hung, with the tea that had been in it hanging in a small brown curve beside it like something in a photograph.

She held it for three seconds. Then it fell the last foot and broke.

"Good," said her grandfather.

He said it quietly. He did not look pleased. He looked at the broken cup on the floor and the tea running between the stones, and something in his face was very old and very tired and very frightened, and she did not know why.

"Now," he said. "A spell. Elias."

Varga, by the door, went pale.

"A small one," said her grandfather. "A Stinging Hex. At her. Not hard."

"Gellert…"

"Elias. She will need this. You know what is coming as well as I do. Better. You have been reading the same newspapers." He did not take his eyes off Evelyn. "A Stinging Hex. When you are ready."

Varga raised his wand. His hand was shaking.

Evelyn cleared the table.

It was harder. There was more on it. There was Varga's shaking hand, and the shame in his face, and her grandfather's eyes, and the knowledge, quite clear and quite cold, that in a moment something was going to hurt her. She put that away last. She folded it twice, the way she folded notes into her cardigan pocket, and put it in the drawer, and shut it.

"*Everte*…"

The spell came out of the end of his wand. It was a small sharp spark of red light, and it came across the cell towards her, and it was three feet away.

It stopped.

It hung in the air between them, a little red point, quite still, fizzing very faintly. She could see it. She could see the shape of it, the way it was put together, the way it wanted to go on and could not.

She held it for one second.

Then it went out.

It did not hit her. It did not fall. It went out, like a match pinched between two fingers, and there was nothing in the air between her and Varga but cold.

Nobody said anything.

Varga lowered his wand. He was looking at her the way people looked at things in museums that had once been used to kill people.

Her grandfather was looking at her too.

"That," he said, very softly, "is the first time in two hundred years."

She did not ask what he meant. She thought she knew.

* * *

She asked him about it at the door, when Varga had gone down ahead to unlock the gate and she was putting on her coat.

"You looked frightened," she said. "When the cup broke."

He was sitting on the chair again, with the candle burnt down to a stub beside him.

"Did I?"

"Yes."

"I am a hundred and twelve years old," said her grandfather. "And I have seen a great many things. Some of them have happened. Some of them have not happened yet." He looked at the candle. "Some of them, I can see quite clearly, are going to happen in rooms with people in them whom you will not be able to keep out of the way of what you do."

"I don't understand."

"No. I would rather you did not, yet." He looked up at her. "Go home, child. Wind your watch. Come at Easter, if you can."

"Will you tell me then?"

"No," said her grandfather. "But you will come anyway, I think. You always do."

She went down the three hundred and eighty-one steps. She counted them. She did not think about the cup.

She was almost entirely successful.

* * *

Lucan rolled up his sleeve at the scullery sink on Boxing Day at twenty past three in the afternoon.

He did not mean to. That was what she thought about afterwards. He had come in from the yew walk with mud on his hands, and the elves were all at the other end of the house laying the table for dinner, and the scullery tap was the nearest, and he went to it the way he had gone to it since he was eight, without thinking, and turned it on, and pushed his left sleeve up to the elbow to wash.

She was sitting at the kitchen table with a cup of tea and a book. She had been sitting there for an hour, because it was the one room in Selwyn Hall where nobody else ever sat.

She looked up.

It was on the inside of his left forearm. It was black, and it was raised, and it was new. The skin round it was still pink, the way skin is pink round a burn that has only lately stopped being a burn. A skull, with a snake coming out of its mouth.

Lucan turned off the tap.

He looked round for a towel, and saw her at the table, and saw where she was looking.

He did not pull his sleeve down.

He stood at the sink with his wet hands and his sleeve up and the Mark on his arm, and he looked at her, and she looked at him. She was not sure how long it lasted. She did not count, which she noticed later, because she counted everything.

Then he smiled.

It was their father's smile. She had watched him learn it. She had been seven and he had been twelve, and she had seen him practising it in the long mirror on the first-floor landing when he thought nobody was there, over and over, until it was the same from every side. It was very white, and very even, and very pleasant, and there was nothing behind it at all.

He dried his hands on the tea towel by the sink. He rolled his sleeve down, quite slowly, and buttoned the cuff.

"Dinner's at seven," he said. "Father's got the Averys coming. Wear something with sleeves."

He went out.

Evelyn sat at the table and looked at her book.

She did not turn the page. She read the same paragraph four times. It was about a man in a hospital bed who had solved a crime five hundred years old by reading what everybody else had already read, and noticing what they had left out.

* * *

Her father talked about realism at dinner.

He did not talk about it loudly. He never talked about anything loudly. He sat at the head of the long table in the dining room with the candles lit and the Averys on his right, a thin grey couple who had lost a cousin in a ditch in Wiltshire in November and had not mentioned it once, and he talked, in his quiet even voice, about the importance of being realistic.

"One must deal with the world as it is," he said. "Not as one would like it to be. That is the great failing of men like Dumbledore. They are romantics. They have an idea of how things ought to be, and they cannot bear to see that things are otherwise." He turned his wine glass a quarter turn on the cloth. "A realist sees which way the wind is blowing. And he does not stand in front of it with his arms out, shouting."

"Hear, hear," said Mr Avery.

"Quite right," said Mrs Avery.

Her mother, at the far end of the table, drank her wine.

Lucan, across from Evelyn, ate his soup. He was wearing a dark grey jacket with long sleeves. He did not look at her once.

"Evelyn," said her father. "You're very quiet."

"I'm always quiet, Father."

"So you are." He looked at her down the length of the table, the way he looked at things he could not quite place. "So you are. Pass the salt, would you."

She passed the salt.

* * *

She wound the watch the next morning at seven, as she had every morning since she was eleven, sitting on the edge of her bed in the room at the top of the west stairs.

Twelve turns of the crown.

She cleared the table as she wound it. Her father and the salt. Lucan at the sink. The Averys. Her mother's glass. The candle on the floor of a cell in Austria, and the cup hanging a foot from the stones with its tea in a curve beside it. The red spark, going out.

One at a time. Folded. In the drawer.

The watch ticked in her hand. The fine second hand, the one most people never noticed, did not move. It never moved. The ordinary hands went round.

She looked out of the window at the yew walk, white with frost, and at the long grey house of Shropshire stone around her, which she had lived in for sixteen years and whose stairs she had counted since she was four, and she thought, quite clearly, without any feeling at all, that it was not her house.

She did not know when it had stopped being.

She put the watch in her cardigan pocket, and buttoned the pocket, and went down to breakfast.
