## Chapter 47: Grimmauld Place

The hall of number twelve was long and dark and smelled of damp, and the first thing anybody said to Harry inside it was "Shh."

It was Mrs Weasley. She came out of a door at the far end with her finger to her lips and her face lit from below by a candle, and she hugged him with one arm, very hard, and whispered, "Not a sound in the hall, dear. Not a sound. Mind the umbrella stand."

The umbrella stand was a troll's leg.

Harry minded it.

He read the house as he came down the hall, because it was what he did, and the house was full. There were a great many people in it. Most of them were downstairs, below his feet, and they were tired and worried and some of them were arguing. Somebody upstairs was asleep. Somebody else upstairs was not asleep and was trying very hard to look as though they were.

And somebody was coming down the stairs.

Harry felt him before he heard him. It was impossible not to. It came down the stairwell ahead of him like a dog let off a lead, and it was joy, enormous and uncomplicated, aimed straight at Harry, and it filled the dark hall from end to end.

"Harry!"

"*Sirius*," hissed Mrs Weasley. "The portrait!"

It was too late. Sirius came off the bottom three stairs in one jump and caught Harry round the shoulders and lifted him off his feet, and somewhere to Harry's left a pair of moth-eaten velvet curtains flew apart, and an old woman in a black cap began to scream.

She screamed about filth. She screamed about blood traitors and half-breeds and mudbloods in the house of her fathers. She screamed in a voice that went through the walls and up the stairs and down into the kitchen, and doors began to open all over the house.

Sirius did not let go of Harry.

He held on with one arm and turned round and shouted at the portrait, "SHUT UP, YOU HORRIBLE OLD HAG," and the old woman shrieked "*YOU!*" and Sirius and Lupin, between them, got the curtains closed again, and the screaming stopped, and the hall was quiet.

"My mother," said Sirius. He let go of Harry at last and stood back and looked at him. "Sorry. She's stuck to the wall. Permanent Sticking Charm. We've tried everything short of taking the wall down, and Moody says the wall's holding up the stairs."

Harry read him.

He was glad. He was so glad it was difficult to see past. But Harry went down through it anyway, because it was Sirius and he wanted to know, and under the gladness there was something just as loud.

It was fury.

None of it was for Harry. It was for the hall and the stairs and the portrait and the damp and the doors and the troll's leg. It was aimed at the whole house. It was the fury of a man who had spent twelve years in a cell and two years in a cave and had come out to find himself in a third place he could not leave, and it was the third that was killing him, because it was his own.

Both of them were at full volume. Neither of them gave an inch to the other.

"You've grown," said Sirius.

"You've had a bath."

Sirius laughed, his loud bark of a laugh, and the curtains twitched, and they both froze, and the curtains stayed shut.

"Kitchen," said Sirius. "Quick. Before she wakes up again."

* * *

The kitchen was in the basement, and it was the only warm room in the house.

There was a long wooden table, with the remains of a meeting all over it, rolls of parchment and empty goblets and somebody's pipe. There was a great iron range at one end with a fire in it. There were Mr Weasley and Bill, getting up from the table, and Mundungus Fletcher, who did not get up, and who Harry read before he had finished being introduced: guilt, aimed at Harry, and under it, very large, the hope that nobody would mention cauldrons.

Nobody mentioned cauldrons. Mrs Weasley looked at Mundungus in a way that made it unnecessary.

She fed Harry stew. She stood over him while he ate it. Sirius sat across the table from him and talked, and talked, and Harry let him, because the gladness in Sirius was so large that it needed somewhere to go and talking was where it went.

At a quarter to twelve Harry put down his spoon.

"What's happening?" he said. "With Voldemort."

Everybody at the table flinched at the name except Sirius and Lupin. Harry read them flinch. He read, under the flinching, a thing they all had in common, which was that they had agreed on something before he arrived and they did not all agree with it.

"Harry, dear, you're too young," said Mrs Weasley.

"I'm not too young to have been in the graveyard."

"That's not…"

"He's got a right to know, Molly," said Sirius.

"He's got a right to be a *child*, Sirius. He's fifteen. He isn't James."

The kitchen went quiet.

Sirius's fury, which had been all over the house, came down to a point. Harry felt it come. It was aimed at Mrs Weasley now, and it was very large, and under it, quite suddenly, there was something Harry had not expected and did not like, which was shame.

"I know who he is," said Sirius.

"Do you?"

"Some of it," said Lupin, quietly. "Molly. Some of it. Dumbledore said he could be told some of it."

Mrs Weasley looked at Lupin for a long moment. Then she looked at Harry, and Harry read her: a great tired love that had five sons in it, and a daughter, and him, somewhere, without being asked, and under the love a terror that had nothing to do with him at all and was about a boy called Percy.

"Some of it," she said, and sat down.

They told him some of it.

Voldemort was recruiting. He was keeping quiet. He did not want the Ministry to know he was back, and the Ministry did not want to know, which suited him very well. The Order was watching the people it knew were his. The Order was trying to recruit people of its own. And the Order was guarding something.

"What kind of something?" said Harry.

"That's enough," said Mrs Weasley.

Harry did not push it.

He had read the table when Sirius said *guarding*, and three of them had thought, at the same moment, of the same place, and Harry had felt the place go through them like the cold through a house when a door opens downstairs. It was a long way below London. It was dark and round and full of doors.

He had been in it.

He said goodnight, and Mrs Weasley showed him up the stairs, and he did not tell anybody.

* * *

Ron and Hermione were in the bedroom at the top of the second flight, both of them sitting on one of the beds, both of them wide awake.

"Hello," said Hermione.

"Hello," said Harry.

"Hi," said Ron.

Nobody said anything else for a while.

Harry put his trunk at the end of the empty bed. He read them, because they were there and he could not stop, and because he wanted to know.

They were polite. They were curious. They were very, very wary. Hermione's wariness was the careful kind, like somebody who has been told a dog bites and is waiting to see which end of it you meet first. Ron's was simpler. Ron did not know what Harry was doing here. Ron did not know what a Slytherin was doing in a house full of the Order of the Phoenix, sleeping in the other bed in his room, and he did not like not knowing.

Harry had seen them before.

He had seen them in another world, in a corridor outside the library, on either side of a boy in a red and gold scarf. He had seen how they walked with him, half a step in front and half a step behind, without ever having to think about where he was. He had felt, in that boy, how much he was loved, and how little he noticed it.

He had not thought about it for months. He thought about it now, sitting on the bed with his trunk at his feet.

"Did you really see Dementors?" said Hermione. "In Surrey?"

"Two."

"And you made a Patronus? A full one? A corporeal one?"

"Yes."

"That's NEWT level," said Hermione. "That's well beyond NEWT level, actually, some wizards never…"

"Hermione," said Ron.

"I'm only *saying*."

"Lupin taught me," said Harry. "In third year."

"I know. Everybody knows. They say it was a stag."

"It was."

Hermione looked at him for a moment as though she wanted to ask something else. She did not ask it. Harry read the question go back down, unasked, and settle at the bottom of her with a great many others.

That was how the first night went.

* * *

The second morning went worse.

They were in the drawing room on the first floor, all of them, the Weasleys and Hermione and Harry, in a long dim room with olive-green walls and the Black family tree embroidered across the whole of one wall, cleaning. Mrs Weasley had a spray bottle of Doxycide and a set of instructions and the look of a general. The curtains were infested. There were doxies in them, small and black and furious, with four arms and a great many teeth, and every few minutes one of them shot out and somebody yelled.

At a quarter to eleven, owls came.

There were two of them. They came in through the window that Fred had just opened to let the Doxycide out, and one of them landed on Ron's shoulder and the other one dropped a letter in Hermione's lap, and Harry looked at the two thick Hogwarts envelopes, and at Ron's face, and at the slight bulge in the bottom corner of each, and he knew what they were.

He had seen one in another world. He had seen it on the other Ron's chest, a red and gold badge with a P on it, in the Great Hall in April, and he had seen it on Hermione.

"Congratulations," he said.

He said it before he had thought. He said it to both of them, quite warmly, because it was true and because he was pleased for them, and because for one second he had forgotten which world he was in.

Ron stopped with his thumb under the seal.

"For what?" he said.

The room went quiet. Even the doxies seemed to go quiet.

Harry read it. He read Ron's wariness come up, sharp, and turn into something else, and he read Hermione's careful question come up from the bottom of her where it had settled, all at once. He read the twins, who were delighted. He read Ginny, who was frightened in a way that was very old and had something to do with a diary and a voice and a Slytherin who knew too much.

"Your letters," he said. "There's a badge in the corner of both of them. You can see the shape through the paper." He pointed. "There. Prefect, I should think. I'd have thought that was obvious."

It was a good lie. It was fast and it was reasonable and it was nearly true. Ron looked down at the envelope and the shape in the corner, and the wariness in him went down a little, and he opened the letter and the badge fell out into his hand.

"Blimey," said Ron.

"*Ron!*" said Mrs Weasley, and dropped the Doxycide, and the moment went past in a great noise of hugging and shrieking, and Fred and George groaning, and Ginny saying *a prefect, you?*, and nobody looked at Harry again for a quarter of an hour.

Except Hermione.

Hermione had her own badge in her hand, and her face was pink with pleasure, and she was looking at him over the top of Mrs Weasley's shoulder. She was not pleased with him. She did not believe him. He read it plainly. She had seen the envelope in her own lap before he spoke, and there had been no shape in the corner of it that anyone could have seen from across the room.

She did not say anything.

She put the question back at the bottom of her with the others. There were a great many of them by now.

* * *

It went less badly in the evening.

It was Ron who made it go less badly, and he did it, as far as Harry could tell, entirely by accident.

They were in the bedroom again. Hermione had gone to write to her parents about the badge. Ron was sitting on his bed polishing it with the corner of his sheet, and Harry was lying on his own bed reading a three-day-old *Prophet*, and neither of them had said anything for twenty minutes.

"Fred and George told me," said Ron.

Harry looked up.

"About the money." Ron did not look at him. He was looking at the badge. "The thousand Galleons. They told me in the summer. They said not to tell Mum, so I haven't. They've been buying stuff. Ingredients and that. They've got a room at the Burrow they won't let anyone in."

"Good," said Harry.

"Why'd you do it?"

Harry thought about the answer.

He read Ron while he thought, and Ron was angry. That was the top of him, as it often was. But the anger was not aimed at Harry. It was aimed at something much older and much larger, and it took Harry a moment to find the shape of it. It was a robe with frayed cuffs. It was a second-hand wand. It was a family of nine at a table with not quite enough on it, and a brother, Percy, who had looked at that table one day and decided he was ashamed of it.

And under the anger, very quiet, Ron was grateful. It was gratitude on behalf of his brothers, and it made him furious, because he would have given anything not to need it.

"Because I didn't want it," said Harry. "And they made me laugh. When nobody else did. And I owed them."

"For what?"

"Something in third year. They gave me something. I'd tell you, but it's theirs to tell."

Ron looked at him.

"Percy's gone," he said abruptly. "Did Mum tell you? He's taken a job with Fudge. Junior Assistant to the Minister. And he had a row with Dad about it, about Dumbledore and that, and about You-Know-Who, and he said things. And he's moved out. To London. Mum cries about it when she thinks we can't hear."

"I know," said Harry. "I mean. I heard."

"Yeah, well." Ron rubbed the badge with the sheet. "He's a prat."

Harry read him.

The anger went down. Under it, a long way under, there was a boy who missed his brother. Quite badly. And who would have died before he said so.

"He'll come back," said Harry.

"How would you know?"

"I don't," said Harry. "I just think he will."

He did not know. That was true. He had not seen Percy in the other world, not once, not at the other Ron's side or anywhere else. He did not know if that Percy had come back either. But he read Ron take the words, and turn them over, and put them away somewhere safe, and the gratitude in him, the furious kind, changed into something else that did not make him furious at all.

"Don't tell Fred and George I said thanks," said Ron.

"You didn't say thanks."

"No," said Ron. "Well. Don't tell them anyway."

* * *

The moment with Hermione came on the fourth day, on the second-floor landing, and it was Harry who started it.

She was coming up with an armful of books. He was coming down. They stopped, the way people do on stairs, each waiting for the other to go first.

"In second year," said Harry. "In October. Outside the Great Hall."

Hermione went still.

He read her. He had read her that day too, and it was exactly the same, three years later and on a landing in London. There was no anger. The anger had gone a long time ago. What was left was small and quiet and deep, and it had been held down so long that it had nearly grown into the floor of her.

"Malfoy called you that word," said Harry. "In front of the whole team. And I stood there. I didn't say anything."

"I remember," said Hermione.

"I was working out what it would cost me if I did. With the house. With the team." He looked at the books in her arms. "I worked it out. And I didn't say anything. I'm sorry."

Hermione looked at him for a long time.

"Why are you saying this now?" she said.

It was the right question. It was the exact right question, and it was the one he had hoped she would not ask, and he thought about lying to her. He thought about it quite clearly, the way he had thought about Dudley in the alley.

"Because I'm going to have to ask you to trust me," he said. "At some point. I don't know when. And I don't think you will unless I say it first."

Hermione went on looking at him.

He read the hurt in her move. It did not go away. It moved, a little, the way a stone moves when the ground under it has been dug, and something came up beside it that was cautious and clever and very slightly amused.

"That's quite honest," she said. "For a Slytherin."

"I'm trying it out."

"How's it going?"

"Badly," said Harry. "I don't know how you lot do it all the time."

Hermione laughed. It was short and surprised, and she looked as though she had not meant to, and she went on up the stairs with her books without saying anything else. But at the top she stopped, and looked back down at him over the banister.

"There was no shape in my envelope," she said. "Was there?"

"No," said Harry.

"I thought not," said Hermione, and went in.

* * *

Tonks taught him disguise in the dining room, which nobody used, on the afternoons when she was not on duty.

"Right," she said, on the first afternoon. She was sitting on the long table with her boots on a chair. Her hair was purple today. "Show me what you do."

Harry looked at her.

He had not done it in front of anybody but Evelyn, who could not see it, and Dumbledore, who had seen the graveyard. He thought about saying no.

He put an apple on the table in front of her.

There was no apple. There was a bare stretch of dark polished wood, with a ring on it where somebody had once put down a wet glass. But Harry pushed the apple out of himself and laid it over the wood, red and green, with a leaf still on the stalk, and Tonks looked at it and reached for it and her hand closed on nothing.

"*Oh*," she said. "Oh, that's horrible. I love that. Do it again."

He did it again. He did a mug of tea. He did a sleeping cat on the chair at the end of the table, which made her get up and go over and try to stroke it. And then, because she was laughing and he wanted her to keep laughing, he did his own face on the empty chair beside her, sitting with its hands folded, looking at her.

Tonks stopped laughing.

She looked at the face for a long while. Then she looked at Harry.

"It's not right," she said.

"What's not?"

"That. You. It's your face. Fine. It's very good. But it's sitting wrong." She got up and walked all the way round the chair, looking at the illusion the way Mr Ollivander looked at a wand. "You've done yourself the way you think you look. Sat up straight. Hands nice. Polite. Like you're at a prize-giving." She pointed. "You don't sit like that. You sit like you're about to leave. Always. You sit on the front half of every chair like there's a fire somewhere and you're not sure where."

Harry looked at his own illusion.

He let it go.

"That's the thing nobody gets," said Tonks. She sat back down on the table. "I can change my face. Nose, hair, eyes, chin. Takes a second. And it's the least of it. It's the last tenth. People don't look at faces, not really. They look at how you walk into a room. How you stand. Whether you look like you're meant to be there. You walk into the Ministry like you work there, with a cup of tea and a bit of parchment, and nobody looks at your face at all." She grinned. "I once got all the way to the Minister's outer office as a fifty-year-old wizard with a moustache, and the whole time I had pink hair. Forgot to change it. Nobody noticed. I was holding a clipboard."

"That's not true."

"Read me."

He read her. It was true.

"The face is the least of it," said Tonks. "It's posture. It's what you expect them to see. If you expect them to see a cleaner, they see a cleaner. If you walk in expecting them to see Tonks with pink hair, they see that, and then they look twice." She leaned forward. "Go out. Come back in. Don't do anything to your face. Don't do any of your magic. Just come in like you're nobody."

"How?"

"Like you've been here forty years and nobody's ever once thanked you."

Harry went out. He stood in the hall for a while. Then he went down to the kitchen, where Mrs Weasley was making a pie and Sirius was reading the *Prophet* with his feet up and Fred and George were trying to get an Extendable Ear under the door to the larder, and he walked in with his shoulders slightly forward and his eyes on the floor and a dishcloth over his arm that he had picked up in the hall, and went to the sink, and began to dry a saucepan.

Nobody looked up.

He dried the saucepan. He dried a ladle. He put them away. He picked up the kettle and filled it.

Four minutes and twenty seconds later, Mrs Weasley turned round with the pie in her hands and screamed.

* * *

He paid her back the next afternoon.

"People look away," he said. "All the time. Everybody. In every conversation. They look at you, and then at some point they look at something else, for a second. A window. Their own hands. And when they look back, they don't check that everything's where it was." He set an apple on the table again. "That's when you move. Not when they're watching you. Not when they're not. When they're coming back."

"You can feel them look away?"

"I can feel them stop paying attention. It's like a light going down."

Tonks looked at the apple.

"Mad-Eye would kill for that," she said. "Mad-Eye would actually murder somebody." She grinned. "Don't tell him. He'll want to put you in a cupboard."

He liked her a great deal.

He did not stop to wonder why. He had known since the broom over London. She was happy all the way down, and in a house where everybody else was frightened and angry and grieving and waiting, sitting in a room with her was like sitting by an open window.

* * *

The house-elf's name was Kreacher, and he hated everybody.

Harry read him the first time he saw him, on the third morning, shuffling along the first-floor landing with a filthy rag knotted round him like a loincloth and a great deal of white hair growing out of his ears, muttering. He was very old. He was the oldest house-elf Harry had ever seen. He was muttering about mudbloods and blood traitors and thieves, and about his poor mistress, and he did not look at Harry once.

He was full of hatred.

It was enormous and it was everywhere and it was aimed at everybody in the house, but most of all at Sirius. Harry had expected that. It was the sort of hatred Harry had felt in a great many people, worn into a groove, so old that it was nearly comfortable.

Under the hatred there was grief.

That, Harry had not expected. It was deep and very old and it was for two people, and Harry felt the shape of them through it. A woman, tall and proud and harsh, whose portrait screamed in the hall. And a young man, a boy really, dark and thin, whose face Harry did not know. Kreacher grieved for the boy so much that it was nearly the whole of him.

And under the grief, at the very bottom, there was something else.

It was devotion. It was small and bright and steady and it was the last good thing in him. Nobody in the house had any of it. Neither did the woman in the portrait, or the boy who was gone. It went somewhere else. It went outside.

"Miss Narcissa," Kreacher muttered, as he passed. "Miss Narcissa would know. Miss Narcissa would not let them touch Mistress's things. Miss Narcissa was always good to Kreacher."

He went down the stairs, still muttering.

Harry stood on the landing and read him all the way down to the kitchen.

* * *

"Cissy?" said Sirius, that evening. "Narcissa Malfoy? He's on about Cissy?"

"He loves her," said Harry. "It's the only thing in him that isn't hatred. Or grief. It's at the very bottom. It's aimed outside the house."

Sirius laughed.

It was a real laugh, the bark, and it was not unkind. He was sitting at the kitchen table with a bottle of firewhisky and the newspaper, and he had been drinking a little, and the fury in him had gone down for the evening to a low steady burn, the way it went down when the house was full and there were people to talk to.

"Harry," he said. "He's *mad*. He's been on his own in this house for ten years, talking to my mother's portrait. He loves everybody in my family who isn't me. He'd love Bellatrix if she came to the door, and she'd pull his ears off." He shook his head. "Cissy hasn't set foot in this house since she married Lucius. Twenty years. If that. He's not going to see her again."

"He doesn't have to see her to love her."

"No. That's true." Sirius poured himself another inch. "But he'd have to leave the house to do anything about it, and he's bound to it. He's my elf. Worse luck. He can't go anywhere I tell him not to." He raised the glass. "And I've told him not to go anywhere. So."

Harry read him.

There was nothing under it. That was what made Harry let it go. Sirius was not worried. He was certain. And Harry, who could feel certainty the way other people could feel rain, took it for knowledge, because it was so large and so plain.

He let it go.

He would think about that conversation, later, more than once.

* * *

The hearing was at eight o'clock in the morning on the twelfth of August, and Mr Weasley took him in on the Underground.

They came up out of a station into a shabby street of offices, and went into a red telephone box with most of its panes missing, and Mr Weasley dialled six-two-four-four-two with his tongue between his teeth, and a cool woman's voice asked their names and business, and a badge came out of the coin return that said HARRY POTTER, DISCIPLINARY HEARING.

The telephone box went down like a lift.

The Atrium was enormous. It was long and high and floored with dark polished wood, and the ceiling was peacock blue with gold symbols moving on it, and down both sides there were fireplaces, dozens of them, with witches and wizards coming out of the green flames in a steady stream. In the middle of the hall there was a fountain. It was a group of golden statues larger than life: a noble wizard with his wand in the air, a beautiful witch, and round them a centaur, a goblin and a house-elf, all looking up at the two humans with expressions of adoration. Water came out of the wands and the arrow-tip and the goblin's hat and the elf's ears, and fell tinkling into the pool.

Harry read the Atrium.

It was very loud. It was hundreds of people on their way to work, bored, worried, late, hungry, sweet on somebody in Accounts, and he turned it down to a roar without slowing down, the way he did with the Great Hall.

He did not like the fountain. He did not know why. He looked at the house-elf looking up at the wizard, and he thought of Kreacher on the landing, and something in him did not like it.

They went down in a lift that rattled. They went past the Department of Magical Law Enforcement and the Improper Use of Magic Office, and Mr Weasley talked the whole time, very fast, about a Muggle-baiting case on his own floor that involved a regurgitating public toilet. Then a note came fluttering into the lift, an interdepartmental memo shaped like a paper aeroplane, and Mr Weasley opened it and went white.

"They've changed the time," he said. "It's now. They've moved it to now. Eight o'clock. And it's in Courtroom Ten."

"Where's Courtroom Ten?"

"Down," said Mr Weasley. "The old ones. Come on."

* * *

The lift went down to the bottom of the Ministry.

The doors opened onto a bare corridor with black stone walls and torches in brackets, and there was no window and no portrait and no noise, and at the far end of it there was a plain black door.

Harry stopped.

He knew this corridor. He had walked down it at night, in another world, with Evelyn beside him and a stolen watch in her pocket. He had walked through that black door into a round room full of doors. He had walked into a room full of clocks and a room full of glass balls, and a room with an archway in it, and a curtain that moved without any wind.

He had watched a man fall.

"Harry?" said Mr Weasley. "This way. Left. Down the stairs."

Harry looked at the black door.

He read it, out of habit. There was nothing. It was a door. There was nobody behind it this morning, nobody awake, nothing loud. It was very quiet, the way a church is quiet on a weekday.

He turned left and went down the stairs.

* * *

He had seen Courtroom Ten before, as well.

He had seen it from inside a stone basin in Dumbledore's office in June, through the eyes of a younger Dumbledore, in the year before he was born. He had sat on these benches and watched a boy with straw-coloured hair scream at his father. He had read, faintly, like smoke, the shame build itself round a father's love and close over it.

It was the same room. Dark stone walls, lit dimly. Benches rising steeply on every side. A chair in the middle with chains on its arms.

The benches were full.

There were about fifty of them, in plum-coloured robes with an elaborately worked silver W on the left breast, and they were all looking down at him, and Harry read them all at once, in one breath, the way he had read the stands at the first task.

Most of them were bored. Some of them were curious. Some of them were hostile, a hard flat dislike that had been settled before he came in, and he marked where they were sitting. Some of them were embarrassed to be there at all, at eight in the morning, for a boy's charm in a Muggle alley.

And some of them were frightened.

He marked those too.

He sat down in the chair. The chains clinked and did not move.

In the middle of the front row sat Cornelius Fudge. On his right was a broad, square-jawed witch with very short grey hair and a monocle, whose fear was very small and whose attention was very large and entirely on the facts. On his left, there was a woman.

She was short and squat, with a broad flabby face and a wide mouth and a little black velvet bow in her short curly hair. She looked like a large pale toad. She was smiling at Harry very sweetly.

Harry read her.

He had read her before. He had read her in another world, through a window in the Great Hall, and he had felt the same thing then that he felt now, and it had not changed at all between the worlds. That was the first thing he noticed. It was exactly the same.

She was pleased.

She was quietly, sincerely, sweetly pleased, and it was aimed at him in the chair with the chains, and under the pleasure, holding it up, there was a certainty so complete that it had no cracks in it at all. And under that there was the thing he had found the first time, in the other Great Hall, and had never forgotten. It was a kind of enjoyment. It was the enjoyment of a person watching someone else be made smaller, and it was quite gentle, and it was perfectly real.

Dolores Umbridge saw him looking at her and smiled a little more.

Harry smiled back.

* * *

There was a scribe at the end of the bench with a long roll of parchment and a quill, and the scribe was Percy Weasley, and he did not look at Harry once.

"Disciplinary hearing of the twelfth of August," said Fudge, in a loud clear voice, "into offences committed under the Decree for the Reasonable Restriction of Underage Sorcery and the International Statute of Secrecy by Harry James Potter, resident at number four, Privet Drive, Little Whinging, Surrey. Interrogators: Cornelius Oswald Fudge, Minister for Magic. Amelia Susan Bones, Head of the Department of Magical Law Enforcement. Dolores Jane Umbridge, Senior Undersecretary to the Minister. Court Scribe, Percy Ignatius Weasley…"

"Witness for the defence," said a quiet voice from the back of the room. "Albus Percival Wulfric Brian Dumbledore."

Harry did not turn round.

He had felt Dumbledore come in, at the back, at the top of the steps, a moment before he spoke. He was closed. He was closed the way he had been closed for four years before a night in June, smooth and complete, and Harry did not try to get in.

But he felt the room.

He felt fifty people turn at once to look at the old man in long midnight-blue robes coming down the steps, and he felt what went through them as he came. There were a great many things. Relief. Irritation. Respect. Fear. And Fudge's, very loud, in the middle of the front row, which was the hardest of all, and which had already turned, on a night in June, into *no*.

"Ah," said Fudge. "Dumbledore. Yes. You got our… er… message that the time and… er… place of the hearing had been changed, then?"

"Did you send one?" said Dumbledore pleasantly. "How kind. I am afraid it went astray. Fortunately I am an old man who sleeps very little, and I was here at five."

A chair appeared beside Harry. Dumbledore did not sit in it. He conjured another for himself, a little behind Harry and to one side, a squashy chintz armchair, and sat down in it, and put his fingertips together, and looked at Fudge over them.

"I have brought a witness," he said. "Mrs Arabella Figg, of Wisteria Walk, who was present on the evening in question. She is waiting outside. But I think," he said, "that before we hear from her, the court would benefit from hearing from Mr Potter. He is, after all, the person who was there."

And he sat back.

Harry felt it.

Not the room. Dumbledore. He felt the old man sit back in the chintz armchair a little behind him and to one side, and fold his hands, and close his eyes very slightly, the way a man does in a concert when the part he has been waiting for begins.

He was not going to speak.

He had come all the way down here, at eight in the morning, past the black door, to sit behind Harry and not speak.

* * *

Harry spoke.

He did not do it the way he would have done it once. He did not tell them the story. He told them the story in the end, but first he did something else, which took about four seconds and which nobody in the room could see.

He read them.

He went along the benches row by row, the way you run a finger down a list. He had marked them already. Now he went down into them, one at a time, the waverers, the ones in the middle, the ones who had not made up their minds before he came in and who would decide it.

There were eleven of them.

A very old witch in the third row, near the end, with a hearing trumpet. She was frightened. She was frightened of Dementors. Not of him, not of the Ministry, not of Dumbledore. Of the Dementors. She had lost somebody to them once, long ago, and she was thinking about it now.

A thin, fussy wizard with a pointed beard, on the second row, who disliked Fudge. Not because of Harry. Because of procedure. He had been made to come down here at eight in the morning with no notice, and the time had been changed by memo, and the room had been changed to Courtroom Ten, which was for the gravest cases, and he did not like it. He did not like to be hurried.

Two witches together, who had read Rita Skeeter in the *Prophet* all summer and who expected, when they looked at him, to see a disturbed boy. And who were, he read, faintly disappointed not to.

A big heavy wizard with a red face who had played Quidditch, and who had been at the third task, in the stands, and who had seen Harry come back out of nowhere with a dead boy's hand in his.

Amelia Bones.

And on the far side, on the fourth row, very still, a lean grey man who did not move his hands.

Harry had read Corvin Selwyn once before, in a Quidditch box above a stadium in the dark. He read him now. He was very quiet. He was quiet the way a man is quiet when he is counting. He was not for Harry and he was not against him. He was watching the room the way he had watched the Quidditch, without any feeling at all for the players, only for the score.

Harry knew what each of them wanted.

He gave it to them.

* * *

He gave Amelia Bones the facts.

He gave them plainly, and in order, with the times. Twenty past nine. The alley, which he named, and the street lamps, which went out. Two Dementors from the Wisteria Walk end. His cousin on the ground. The charm at nine twenty-three, which he knew because the Ministry's own letter had said so, and which he quoted back at them, word for word, *with best wishes*.

Somebody on the second row laughed. It was the fussy wizard with the pointed beard. He laughed and then looked startled at himself, and Harry read his dislike of Fudge go up a notch, quite pleased.

He gave the old witch with the hearing trumpet the Dementors.

He did not describe them. He said, quite simply, that they had come down the alley, and that it had gone cold, and that his cousin, who was a Muggle and who had never seen anything magical in his life except Harry's Hogwarts trunk, had fallen down and been sick. He said that Dementors were meant to be guarding Azkaban. He said that he would like very much to know who had sent two of them to Little Whinging, and why, and whether anybody at the Ministry knew where they were now.

The old witch put down her hearing trumpet.

"Dementors," said Umbridge sweetly, "are under the control of the Ministry of Magic, Mr Potter. They do not wander about Surrey at random. If you are suggesting…"

"I'm not suggesting anything," said Harry. "I'm asking where they are."

He gave the two witches who had read Rita Skeeter a boy who was not disturbed. He did not change anything about his face. He did not need magic for it. He sat as Tonks had said he sat, on the front half of the chair, and then he sat back in it, the way a boy sits back who has nothing to hide and is a little tired of being asked, and the two witches looked at each other.

He gave the big red-faced wizard the maze.

He did not say Cedric's name. He did not have to. He said, at the end of the facts, that he had been to a funeral in June and he would rather not go to another one. That was all. He said it to his own hands. And on the fourth bench the big wizard's face went redder, and the thing in him that had been at the third task, in the stands, came up, and stayed up.

And he gave Corvin Selwyn the score.

He did it last, and he did it without looking at him. He turned his head, very slightly, while Fudge was talking, and let his eyes go along the benches, slowly, the way you look at a room when you are counting the people in it. He let them see him count. He let them see him finish, and look satisfied. And he read, on the fourth row, a lean grey man who did not move his hands watch a boy count the room, and come to a number, and go very still.

Corvin Selwyn went on counting for a moment after Harry had stopped.

Then he looked at Fudge, the way a man looks at a ledger that does not add up.

* * *

Mrs Figg was called. She came down the steps in a moth-eaten shawl and her carpet slippers and described the Dementors in a great deal of detail, most of it accurate, and was asked by Fudge what they looked like, and said they were big and hooded and they glided, and that if the Minister wanted it in more detail he could go and stand in that alley himself, and that the street lamps had gone out, and the cats had all run under the hedge.

"Very well," said Fudge, when she had gone. "Very well. I think we have heard enough."

"Those in favour of clearing the accused of all charges?" said Madam Bones.

Hands went up.

Harry did not count them. He had counted already. He read them go up instead, one after another, the old witch's and the fussy wizard's and the two Rita Skeeter witches' and the big red-faced wizard's, and Madam Bones's own, a firm square hand with a monocle catching the light over it, and a great many more after them, the ones who had only been waiting to see where everybody else went.

And on the fourth row, a little after the others, very slowly, without any feeling at all, Corvin Selwyn's.

"Those in favour of conviction?"

Fudge's hand went up. Umbridge's. Five or six more.

"Cleared of all charges," said Madam Bones.

Harry read Umbridge.

She was smiling still. She had not stopped smiling. But the pleasure in her had gone somewhere else, and in its place, just for a moment, there was something very cold and very interested. It was aimed at him. It was the interest of a woman looking at something she had expected to be easy and finding it was not.

Then it went back down, and the smile went on.

* * *

Dumbledore stood up.

He stood up out of the chintz armchair, and it vanished. He did not say anything to Fudge, or to Madam Bones, or to the room. He did not say anything to Harry.

He did not look at him.

He went up the steps, between the benches, in his long blue robes, towards the door, and he did not look back once, and the whole of the Wizengamot watched him go.

Harry watched him too.

A year ago he would have read it as a door shut in his face. He would have put it in the ledger. *He would not look at me.* He would have sat in the chair with the chains on it and felt, under the winning, the old cold thing.

He did not feel it.

He did not have to read Dumbledore to know. Dumbledore was closed, the way he had been closed all morning. But Harry had felt him in the chintz chair for forty minutes, a little behind and to one side, not speaking, with his hands folded, while a fifteen-year-old boy took the room apart.

He had not looked at Harry because there was nothing he needed to check.

Harry sat in the chair in the middle of Courtroom Ten, with the chains not moving, and watched the old man go up the steps without him. Behind him, on the bench, Percy Weasley was writing *cleared of all charges* in a very small, very neat hand.

At the top of the steps the door opened and closed.
