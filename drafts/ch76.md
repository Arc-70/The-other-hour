## Chapter 76: Open

Evelyn began to shake at fourteen minutes past twelve.

She noted the time. She would always note the time. She noted it from the far end of the corridor in her own head, where she seemed to be standing, and she noted that she was standing in the doorway of the upstairs front room of the Shrieking Shack with her wand still raised, and that her hand, which had been perfectly steady for three minutes, was not steady any more.

It began in the hand.

It was a small movement at first, a fine quick trembling at the tip of the wand, so that the point of it drew tiny circles on the air above the place where Severus Snape had been standing. Then it went up her arm. Then it was in her shoulders, and her knees, and her teeth.

She lowered the wand.

It did not help. She stood in the doorway with the wand at her side and shook. Her whole body shook, in long hard shudders that came up from her feet, and she could not stop them, and she did not know what they were.

She had never shaken in her life.

She had not shaken when she was five years old and her grandmother sat her down in the east wing at Selwyn Hall and taught her to close a door that she would never open again. She had not shaken in the Chamber, or in the other world, or in a lay-by on the A49 at four o'clock in the afternoon holding an old man's hand, or on a staircase in Nurmengard. She had not shaken in Courtroom Ten with her father looking at her. She had not shaken in a walled courtyard at the foot of a tower, holding a room still for four minutes while a boy died inside it. She had cried, afterwards, for twenty-two minutes. She had not shaken.

She was shaking now.

She noted it. She noted that it was very strange, and that it did not seem to be anything to do with her, and that she could not make it stop by noting it.

She noted that she had killed a man.

* * *

Harry got up.

He did it slowly, the way you get up when you have been kneeling for a long time and your knees have gone stiff. Snape's hand slid off the front of his coat as he rose. It did not fall. It slid, and turned over, and lay on the dusty boards palm up, with the fingers a little curled.

Harry stood and looked down at him.

The room was very quiet. It was quieter than any room Harry had ever been in. He noticed that, vaguely, the way you notice that a clock has stopped without knowing which clock. He could not feel the owl in the roof. He could not feel the rats in the walls. He could not feel anything beyond the walls of the room at all, and inside the walls there was Snape, who was nothing now, the place where a person had been, and there was the doorway, where there had always been nothing, and there was Harry.

He did not reach for anything. He did not think to.

He looked at Snape's face.

It was turned up to the ceiling. The eyes were open. They were black, and they were looking at the place where Harry's face had been a moment ago, when he was kneeling, and they had not moved when he stood, so they were looking at nothing now. The silver was still coming out of them, more slowly. It hung in the air above the body in long thin threads, the way smoke hangs in a room with no draught.

Snape had been going to close every door in his head.

Snape had been going to make him what everybody else was.

Harry looked down at the man who had held out a wand across a tower roof without a word, and who had sent a silver doe to a cinema in Leeds five times, and who had lured him here with it, and who had been afraid of him, and had been right.

"Good," said Harry.

* * *

He took a phial out of the inside pocket of his coat.

It was the small one he carried for nosebleeds, with a stopper and an inch of dittany in it, which Hermione had given him in October. He took the stopper out and tipped the dittany out onto the floor, where it ran into the dust and the dark, and he knelt down again beside Snape and held the phial up to the silver threads.

They went in.

They went in slowly, curling, the way milk goes into tea. He did not know how he knew to do it. He had seen Dumbledore do it, in a round office, with a wand to his own temple, years ago. He held the phial up to the threads as they came out of the dead man's eyes, and they came, and the phial filled, very bright, so that it lit his fingers from inside.

He put the stopper back in.

He did not think about what was in it. He thought that it might be useful. That was all. A man who had been in the Dark Lord's inner circle for seventeen years, and in Dumbledore's confidence for the same seventeen years, had just given up every memory he had in a silver stream on the floor, and some of them would be about where things were kept, and who could be trusted, and what the Dark Lord meant to do next. It would be useful.

He put the phial in his inside pocket, next to the Cloak.

He did not look at the doorway.

He did not decide not to. He simply did not. There was nothing in the doorway that he could read, and there never had been, and for a month now he had not looked at things he could not read. He had not looked at them the way you do not look at the sun. He knelt on the boards beside Snape with the phial of memories in his pocket and the candle guttering beside him, and stood up, and looked at the boarded window, and the four-poster, and the floor, and at Snape's wand against the leg of the bed, and not once at the doorway.

He heard it close.

It was the door of the room. It closed quite quietly, with a click of the latch. Somebody had stepped inside and shut it behind them, and was standing now with their back against it, inside the room, with him.

He did not look round.

"Potter," said Evelyn.

* * *

He turned round then.

She was standing with her back against the door, and her wand was in her right hand, hanging at her side, and she was shaking.

He saw that. He saw it the way he saw the candle and the bed and the body on the floor. He could not read her, and so he looked at her the way he looked at any of those things, and he saw that she was shaking, all of her, in long hard shudders, and that her face was quite white, and that her mouth was shut very tight, as though she were holding something in it.

He did not know what it meant. He did not, for a moment, wonder.

"Selwyn," he said. "We should go."

She did not move.

"We should go," said Harry again. "Somebody'll have felt that. The wards on the village. Or him." He looked at Snape. "He'll have told somebody where he was going. Or he won't, and they'll come looking anyway. We've got about ten minutes."

"I have just killed a man," said Evelyn.

Her voice was quite steady. It was the only thing about her that was.

"I have just killed a man for you," she said. "At eleven minutes past twelve. In the Shrieking Shack. With a spell I read about in a book, in the margin, that you told me about on the floor of a classroom at two o'clock in the morning, a year ago. A spell he wrote. He was our Head of House for six and a half years. He taught me Potions for five. He gave me an Outstanding. He was the only teacher in the castle who ever spelled my name right on a report without being told." She stopped. Her teeth were going. She shut her mouth on them, hard, and opened it again. "I have never killed anything in my life, Potter. I have never killed a spider. I put them out of the window in a glass. And I have just killed a man so that you could go on being whatever it is you are, and I would like you to look at me while I tell you, because I do not think you have looked at me for nineteen days."

Harry looked at her.

He looked at her because she had asked, and it was reasonable, and there was no reason not to. He looked at the white face against the door, and the shaking, and the wand. He could not read any of it. He never could. There was nothing there.

"Thank you," said Harry.

He meant it. He said it the way he would have said it to Ron for passing the salt.

"It was the right call," he said. "He'd have finished in another minute. Maybe less. He'd closed the first one already." He frowned, slightly, and put his hand to his temple, and took it away. "I can't feel the village. I'll have to see if that comes back. But you did the right thing, Selwyn. You did exactly the right thing, and you did it fast, and nobody else could have. You're the only one he couldn't have stopped. I'd have done the same, if it had been the other way round." He looked at the window. "Now we should go."

Evelyn looked at him.

She looked at him for a long moment, from the door, shaking. He waited. He did not mind waiting. He had nowhere in particular to be.

Then she said, in a voice he had never heard her use, very low, not to him, "*Oh*," and something in her face gave way.

Not much. He saw it. He would not have seen it in anybody else. He saw it in her because he had looked at her face for six and a half years and knew everything it did and had never seen it do this. It was as though somebody had been holding the face up from behind, and had let go.

"All right," said Evelyn.

She put her wand in her pocket.

"All right," she said again, and he did not know who she was saying it to.

And she opened.

* * *

He had been reading people for as long as he could remember.

He had read his aunt in a kitchen when he was four and his uncle in a car when he was five and Dudley in a play park and a hundred thousand people at a Quidditch World Cup. He had read Hagrid on a rock in the sea on the night of his eleventh birthday, the first warm loud thing he had ever felt aimed at himself, and it had been the loudest thing in the world, and he had thought, at eleven, that nothing would ever be as loud as that again.

He had read Evelyn Selwyn every day for seven years and found nothing.

Nothing. Silence. The one quiet place in a world full of noise. He had found it unsettling, and then fascinating, and then, over years, the place he went to rest. He had stood beside it at the World Cup with a hundred thousand people screaming and leaned on it like a wall. He had rested on it in a marquee and an Atrium and a cellar. He had known it better than he knew any feeling in anyone, because it was no feeling at all, only the absence of one, perfect, constant, the same on every day of every year since a stool in Madam Malkin's, and he had stopped, at some point, even noticing it was there. The way you stop noticing the floor.

The floor went.

It went all at once. There was no warning. There was nothing, the way there had always been nothing, and then there was not nothing, and it came through the closed door in the back of his head that Snape had shut a quarter of an hour ago as though the door were made of paper, and it was the loudest thing he had ever felt in his life.

Louder than Hagrid on the rock.

Louder than a hundred thousand people. Louder than the stands at the end of a maze, or a marquee going from joy to terror in one second, or a Great Hall grieving as one thing. Louder than all of them together. It was one person. It was one person standing against a door in an empty house, and it was everything she had ever felt, all at once, every feeling of nineteen years that had been kept behind a door since she was five, and the door was open, and it all came out.

He could not stand up in it.

He found that he was not standing up. He was on the floor. He did not know how. He was on his hands and knees on the dusty boards of the Shack with his head down and his eyes shut, and it was coming into him, and he could not turn it down. He reached for the place inside him where he turned things down, the place he had used since he was six, and it was not there, or it was there and it was nothing against this, a hand put up against the sea.

He fought it.

He did not decide to. It was the thing in him that had sat on a step in the snow for six hours and felt nothing, and it did not want this. It fought the way you fight waking. The way you fight when somebody pulls the covers off in a cold room and you curl up and reach for them and pull them back over your head, and they are pulled off again. He fought it hard. He put up walls. They went. He put up the Majestic, red plush and gilt and the women with stars in their hair, and it went like wet paper. He put up the snow in Hogsmeade, and the calm, and the step, and the apple, and they went.

It came anyway.

And it was not anything he could build an illusion against, because it was not looking at him. It did not have eyes to fool. It was simply there, all of it, in him, and it would not stop being there.

It was the killing.

That was first. That was on the top. It was a minute old, and it was enormous, and he felt it the way she felt it, from inside. A wand raised in a doorway and a word said, and a man going over sideways like a tree, and something opening in him in several places at once, and the dark spreading across the boards. And the knowing. The plain, flat, total knowing, arriving all at once and never going to go away again, that she had done it, she, Evelyn Selwyn, who put spiders out of windows in a glass.

It was terror.

Under the killing, filling every space round it, so large that the killing floated on it. She was terrified. She had been terrified for nineteen days. She had been terrified for a month. She had been terrified on a staircase at Hogsmeade, going down towards a step, knowing before she got there what she would find. And she was terrified now, of this, of what she was doing, of the door standing open behind her for the first time in her life and everything going out of it and anyone in the world able to walk in.

She was doing it anyway.

It was fury.

He had felt fury before. He had felt it in Uncle Vernon and in Sirius and in Dumbledore, once, coming through a door that had been blasted in. This was different. It was cold and very clear and it had a list. It was aimed at him. It was aimed at a boy who had looked at her on a step and smiled at her like a shop assistant, and who had moved Weasley's feelings round like furniture, and who had made fourteen men stand in the snow until they died, and who had sat on a bench at Shell Cottage with Draco Malfoy's wand in his pocket and watched her dig a grave with her hands and not got up. It was nineteen days of it. It was every one of the nineteen, counted.

It was grief.

It was for her grandfather, in a tower, and the watch on the projection-room table that did not tick. It was for Dobby, under a white stone. It was for Justin, on a stripped bed by a window. It was for Sirius, who had danced with her once and stood on her feet four times and been trying very hard. It was for Lockhart. It was for Snape, on the floor, whom she had not liked and had killed. And it was for something else, larger than all of them, so large that it took him a long time to see its edges.

It was for him.

She was grieving for him.

He understood that, on his hands and knees on the floor. She was grieving for him as though he had died. She had been grieving for him for a month. She had been standing at the edge of a grave for a month, the way she had stood at the edge of Dobby's on the cliff, and he had been in it, and he had not known.

It was him.

He saw himself. He saw himself the way she saw him. Not a reading. A picture, very clear and very exact, laid down in her over a month, piece by piece, every piece noted. A thin young man with hair that needed cutting, sitting on a step in the snow with a blue hand, eating an apple, perfectly calm. A thin young man with a stranger's face at the breakfast table, drinking tea. A thin young man below a screen in a dark cinema making rain, very well, for hours, with nobody watching. A thin young man who did not look at her.

He did not recognise him.

He looked at the young man in her, on the step, in the snow, and he did not know who it was. And he understood, with the part of him that was still able to understand anything, that this was what she had seen at the foot of the post office steps. A stranger, smiling.

And under all of it, at the very bottom, under the killing and the terror and the fury and the grief, holding all of them up, there was one more thing.

It was the loudest.

He did not have a word for it. He had a great many words. He had been finding words for what people felt since before he could spell them. He had words for love, many kinds, more than he could count. He had felt a mother's love in a mirror and in a churchyard, and Hagrid's on a rock, and Sirius's perfect happiness at the head of a table, and the other Sirius's love for another Harry in a room above a bar. He had felt Snape's love for a girl on a swing, pressed in a book for twenty years.

This was not like any of them.

It was very plain. It was very large. It did not want anything. It had never asked for anything, not once, in seven years. It was aimed at him, and it had been aimed at him, he understood, for a very long time, behind a door, where he could not feel it and she could not show it. It had been there on a stool in Madam Malkin's, small, without either of them knowing. It had been there at the edge of a maze, watching. It had been there in a cellar, saying *Potter*, twice. It had been there on a staircase in the dark of this house for eleven minutes while she decided, and it had decided.

He mattered to her.

That was all. That was the whole of it. It was the simplest thing he had ever felt in anyone. He mattered to her more than she had words for, more than anything, more than the rule her grandmother had given her every morning from the age of five, more than the door. He mattered to her so much that she had opened it.

It was louder than Hagrid on the rock.

It was louder than anything he had ever felt in his life.

* * *

*She will open the door for you once.*

It came to him from a long way off, very clearly, in an old man's voice, elegant and amused and not amused at all. A cell at the top of a tower. A bed. Snow on the slot of a window. An old man propped up on pillows, looking at Harry as he came back alone for a girl's forgotten scarf, and saying, without any preamble, as though he had been waiting all afternoon to say it:

*She will open the door for you once. Do not make her do it twice.*

He had not told her. He had never told her. He had said *he said thank you for the rain*, and put it away, and not thought about it again.

He thought about it now.

He was on his hands and knees on the floor of the Shrieking Shack with everything she had ever felt going through him like the sea through a broken wall, and he understood that it was happening, now, the once. That she had done it. That she was standing against the door with it open behind her and every part of her that had been closed for nineteen years going out into the room, where anybody could reach it, where he could reach it, and she had done it knowing exactly what it cost and not knowing whether it would work.

He stopped fighting.

He did not decide to. He found that he had stopped. The way you find, when you have been holding something heavy for a long time, that your hands have opened.

He let it in.

* * *

He was sitting on the floor.

He did not remember getting there. He was sitting on the dusty boards of the Shack with his back against the wall under the boarded window, and his knees drawn up, and the candle was nearly out, and he was himself.

He knew it the way you know you are awake. Not by any one thing. By everything at once. The cold of the floor coming up through his trousers. The ache in his knees. The smell of dust and old damp and something else, under it, that he did not let himself name. The weight of the phial in his inside pocket. Snape on the floor four feet away, on his back, with his eyes open.

He looked at Snape.

He felt it then. All of it. It came up in him the way water comes up in a well that has been dry, slowly, from the bottom, and then faster, and then over the top.

Snape. A man who had held out a wand without a word. Who had been afraid of him. Who had been right.

Fourteen men in the snow.

He had made fourteen men stand in the snow until they died. He had sat on a step and eaten an apple and watched them. He had made flowers.

A man in Leeds called Selby, talking to a wall.

Ron, standing up red at a folding table, and sitting down calm, and not knowing why.

Hermione on a roof in Sheffield with her hands not shaking.

A plain girl at the foot of some steps, whom he had smiled at, and not known.

A white stone on a cliff, and a girl with bleeding hands, and a bench, and himself on it, not getting up.

It came up and up. He did not turn it down. He could not have, and he did not try.

And then, under all of it, from the very bottom, slowly, the last thing came, the oldest, the one he had not let himself have since an orchard in August.

A kitchen door.

The Burrow, with every window lit. A man in a mustard-and-plum coat, with his back to the door and his wand up and the light behind him, laughing. Very good. Very fast. The happiest Harry had ever felt anyone. Holding the door so that the Weasleys could get through it to the fire, and laughing, and then not laughing, and the laughter stopping, all at once, while Harry turned away across the garden with a child in his arms and ran and did not stop and did not look back.

Sirius.

Harry put his face in his hands.

He cried.

He had not cried since he was very small. He had not cried at the Burrow, or in the back row of a cinema with three people beside him, or at a funeral he had not gone to, or on a bridge, or on a step. He had not cried for Dumbledore on a lawn or for Cedric in a churchyard. He cried now, on the floor of the Shrieking Shack, with his face in his hands and his back against the wall, and it was not quiet and it was not dignified and it did not stop. It went on for a very long time. It went on until the candle went out, and after, in the dark, with the moon coming through the cracks in the boards across the window in thin white lines.

She sat down beside him.

He felt her do it. He did not need to look. She came across the room in the dark, past the body on the floor, and sat down on the boards beside him with her back against the same wall, close, so that her shoulder was against his.

She did not say anything.

She did not touch him except with her shoulder. She did not put her arm round him or take his hand. She sat beside him in the dark with her shoulder against his, and her mind was still open, and he could feel her.

He could feel her.

She was still frightened. He could feel that. It was very loud, and it had not gone anywhere, and it was aimed at him and at the dark and at the door standing open in her. She was still grieving. She was still furious, a little, under everything, a cold clear list that had not been crossed out. She was shaking less. And she was there. All of her was there, beside him, in the dark, and every part of her that he could feel was staying.

He cried until there was nothing left to do it with.

Then he sat with his head back against the wall and his eyes shut, and breathed.

* * *

He did not say any of it.

He would never say it, to her or to anybody. He sat on the floor with his back against the wall and her shoulder against his, and understood it, the way you understand where you are when you wake in a strange room.

He had been living in an illusion for a month.

Not one he had made on anybody else. One he had made on himself. He had made a young man who did not feel things, because feeling things was a luxury, and he had stepped inside him on a bridge in the snow and pulled the door shut behind him, the way he had taken the end off fourteen men's worst nights, so that it would go on. And it had gone on. It had been the best illusion he had ever made. He had believed it completely. He had sat inside it, on a step, at a breakfast table, below a screen, and never once known he was in it, and nobody in the world could have seen it from outside except one person, whom it could not touch.

Oswin, the apprentice in Slytherin's journal, had walked this Path all the way up. And at the top of it, the journal said, he could not find the door back to himself.

Harry knew where the door was now.

He knew where it was the way you know where a door is in a room you have been led out of by the hand. He could find it again. He could go back in, if he had to. He could go a long way in. He could go further than he had gone, and come out.

But he could not find it from the inside. Not alone. Nobody could. That was the thing Oswin had not had. You needed somebody on the other side of it. Somebody it could not touch. Somebody standing outside the door, in the snow, at the foot of the steps, who knew who you were when you did not, and would not go away.

He opened his eyes.

The moon was coming through the boards in white lines across the floor. One of them lay across Snape's open hand. One lay across his own knees. One lay across hers.

* * *

"I can't close it," said Evelyn.

She said it into the dark, quite flatly, after a long time. Her shoulder was still against his.

"I've been trying," she said. "Since you stopped. I've been trying for twenty minutes. I've closed it every morning since I was five, Potter. I've never not been able to. I don't even think about it. It's like breathing." She paused. "I can't find the door."

Harry did not say anything.

He could feel her trying. He could feel it, very clearly, through her shoulder and the dark: a girl standing in the middle of a room where every wall had been taken down, turning round and round, looking for the place where the door had been, and not finding it.

"It's partly there," she said. "I can get some of it. Some of it's closing. It's like a window that's been painted shut. I can get it down most of the way, and then it sticks." She was quiet for a moment. "Anyone could read me. Now. Anyone at all. Not just you. Anybody with a wand and the word."

"I know."

"I've never been read in my life."

"I know."

"My grandmother would be…" She stopped. He felt what was under the stop. He did not say anything about it.

They sat in the dark.

He could feel her. He could feel all of her, still, not as loud now, but there, like the warmth of a fire in another room. She was tired, very, all the way down. She was frightened, still, about the window that would not close. She was sore, in her knees, from the floor. She was cold. And she was something else, small, new, very near the top, that he had never felt in her because he had never felt anything in her, and he looked at it for a while in the dark before he understood what it was.

"You're hungry," said Harry.

There was a pause.

"I haven't eaten since lunch," said Evelyn. "It isn't an emotion."

"It is from here."

"Stop that."

"I can't. You're very loud."

"I'm aware," said Evelyn, "that I'm very loud." She shifted her shoulder against his. "I've been aware of it for twenty minutes. It's like going out in the street with no clothes on. You could at least have the decency not to *comment*."

He felt it go through her as she said it. Embarrassment, quite sharp. Under it, something that was very nearly a laugh, and that she did not let out.

He felt himself smile in the dark.

Not the polite smile, the one for shop assistants and train guards. His own. He had not felt it on his face for a month, and it felt strange there, stiff, like a door that has not been opened in a long time.

"Selwyn," said Harry.

"What."

"You've been following me."

"For twenty-two days."

"I didn't know."

"No," said Evelyn. "That was rather the point." She was quiet for a moment. "You're very easy to follow, Potter. You never look behind you. You never have. You assume that if anybody's there you'll feel them." He felt her turn her head, in the dark, towards him. "I've been there for seven years."

He did not answer that. He could not.

He sat on the floor of the Shrieking Shack with his back to the wall and her shoulder against his and the moon in white lines across the boards, and a dead man four feet away, and a window in her that would not close, and he could feel her.

"Light something," said Evelyn, after a while. "Please. I'd like to see where I'm putting my feet before I have to walk anywhere."

He found his wand in the dark. He lit it.

She was looking at him. Her face was white, and very tired, and streaked with dust, and quite plain, and exactly as it always was. Her hands were in her lap. They had nearly stopped shaking.

"There's dust on your face," said Harry.

"There's dust on yours. And a great deal of something else." She looked at his knees, and then away. "We'll go to the Hog's Head. The back way. Aberforth won't ask questions. He doesn't like the answers." She paused. "Weasley will have found the pillows by now. Granger will be making lists."

"Yes."

"They'll be frightened."

"I know," said Harry. "I can feel you thinking it."

"Stop *doing* that."

"I'm not doing anything. You're doing it at me."

Evelyn looked at him for a moment in the wandlight, with her mouth shut tight. He felt the nearly-laugh again, quite close to the top, and felt her hold it down, and felt her decide not to bother.

"I'd like it noted," she said, "that I have still not had anything to eat."

"It's noted," said Harry.
