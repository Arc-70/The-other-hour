## Chapter 52: The Flight

Draco Malfoy was frightened at dinner on the last Wednesday of March, and Harry read it from three seats along without even looking up from his shepherd's pie.

It was the errand again.

He knew it at once. He had read it every day for a week in February, the fear and the pride and the importance, one on top of the other, and he had followed it up seven flights of stairs and into a cathedral of hidden things and watched it walk away with a crown. He knew the shape of it the way he knew the shape of Theo's handwriting.

It was back. It was the same.

Draco did not eat much. He touched the inside pocket of his robes twice. At a quarter past seven he got up and left the table without a word to Crabbe or Goyle, and Harry read the fear go out of the Hall with him, large and bright, and turn left at the door, towards the marble staircase.

Up.

"Harry," said Theo.

"Mm."

"You're going to be late."

"I know." He put down his fork. "Go on up. All of you. Start without me. Tell Hermione to do Shield Charms. I'll be there by eight."

Theo looked at him.

Harry read the look. Theo was not worried. Theo was mildly curious, and a little tired, and he had decided long ago that if Harry did not say where he was going it was because Harry had a reason, and that the reason was usually a good one.

"All right," said Theo.

Harry went to find the Cloak.

* * *

He did not take Evelyn.

He thought about it afterwards, a great deal. He thought about the eleven seconds he had stood in the dormitory with the Cloak over his arm and thought about going to find her. She was in the library. She would have come. She would have walked beside him under the Cloak with her shoulder against his arm and seen whatever there was to see, and not felt anything, because she never did.

He did not go and find her.

He told himself there was no time. That was true. He told himself it was only Malfoy, and he had followed Malfoy before. That was true too.

He went up alone.

He found Draco on the fifth floor, by the statue of Boris the Bewildered. He did not need to see him. He followed the fear up the stairs the way you follow a light down a corridor, and it was there, on the fifth floor, standing still, and Harry came up behind it under the Cloak and stopped a little way off, by a window, and read.

Fear. Pride. Importance.

The same. Exactly the same.

Draco was standing by the statue looking at nothing. He was waiting. Harry read that too, the small impatience of somebody who has been told to be somewhere at a time. He was waiting for somebody, or for a time, and when it came he would go on, up, to the seventh floor, to a black door with iron hinges and the cabinet he had opened an inch and looked inside.

Harry waited with him.

He did not know what for. He did not know whether Draco was meeting somebody, or fetching something, or simply standing there because he had been told to stand there until eight. He stood under the Cloak by the window and read Draco's fear go round and round in the same shape, steady, like a stone at the bottom of a stream, and he waited for it to change.

It did not change.

At eight minutes to eight Harry looked at his watch.

At five minutes to eight he looked at it again.

At three minutes to eight he thought: *the others are all upstairs. They've all gone in. Hermione's doing Shield Charms. They'll be wondering where I am.*

He thought: *Malfoy's not going anywhere. He's just standing there.*

He thought: *I can come back.*

He went up the last two flights to the seventh floor, quietly, under the Cloak, and along the corridor to the blank stretch of wall opposite Barnabas the Barmy, and walked past it three times, and the small plain door appeared, and he took the Cloak off and went in.

The fear on the fifth floor did not move.

He felt it not move, all the way up. He felt it stay where it was, by the statue, round and round, steady as a stone.

He felt it right up until he closed the door.

* * *

The Room was full.

Twenty-five of them. Hermione was at the front with her wand up, showing the Shield Charm to the Hufflepuffs, and Ron was arguing with Fred about something, and Luna was sitting on a cushion reading *The Quibbler* upside down. Hermione's parchment was pinned on the wall by the door, where she pinned it every week, with all twenty-six names on it and the name at the top in her neat round hand.

*Dumbledore's Army.*

Millicent had thought it was a stupid name. Hermione had said that was the point. If the Ministry was frightened of Dumbledore raising an army, then they might as well have one.

"You're late," said Hermione.

"I know. Sorry."

"Where were you?"

"Watching Malfoy."

"Again?" said Ron. "You're obsessed."

"Probably," said Harry.

He was. He knew it. He went to the front of the room and took his jumper off and pushed his sleeves up, and he could still feel it, a long way below him, three floors down: fear, pride, importance, by a statue, waiting.

They did Shield Charms. They did them for twenty minutes. Neville's held against Millicent's Stunner for the first time, and Millicent was so pleased for him that she hit him on the arm and knocked him over. Ginny's held against Fred and George together. Zacharias Smith complained that his partner was too fast.

Harry walked round the room and read them, and said one sentence to each pair, and walked on.

And at twenty past eight, the fear on the fifth floor changed.

* * *

It did not change the way fear changes. Fear goes up, or down. It turns into something else, anger or relief or shame. It goes quiet when the thing you are afraid of goes away and loud when it comes closer.

This did not do any of that.

It stopped.

It stopped the way a sound stops when somebody switches off a radio. It had been there, steady, round and round, for an hour and five minutes, the same shape every second, and then it was not there at all. There was no fear on the fifth floor. There was no pride. There was no importance.

There was something else.

Nothing on the fifth floor now. The new thing was on the seventh, outside the door. It had been there, Harry understood, standing very still, for some time.

It was triumph.

He knew it before he had finished reading it. He knew it the way he had known in a churchyard, a very long time ago, that the voice from the bundle in Wormtail's arms was pleased. It was cold and high and bright and it was aimed at the wall, at the door, at him.

And under the triumph, very clearly, was Draco Malfoy.

He had never been on the fifth floor at all.

* * *

"Out," said Harry.

Nobody heard him the first time. He said it again, louder, and the room stopped.

"Out. Now. Everybody. Malfoy's outside. Get out and go anywhere, go different ways, don't run together, get back to your common rooms…"

They went.

They went the way a room goes when someone shouts fire, all at once and badly, cushions kicked over, wands dropped and picked up, Fred and George pulling the door open and going through it at a run with Lee Jordan behind them. Harry stood by the door and pushed people through it, one after another, and read the corridor outside.

It was full.

There was Draco, standing in the middle of it, by the hanging of Barnabas the Barmy, with his wand out and the triumph pouring off him. There was Pansy Parkinson, and Crabbe, and Goyle, and Warrington, and Montague, all with small silver I's on their robes, at both ends of the corridor. There was Filch, wheezing up the stairs from the sixth floor with his lamp.

And there was Umbridge, coming round the corner at the far end, very fast for a short woman, with her wand held up in front of her like a candle.

Some of them got away. Ron and Hermione went left, towards the Gryffindor stairs, and Goyle went for them and missed. Luna simply walked past Crabbe as though he were not there, and Crabbe, astonished, let her. Theo went through a hanging that Harry had not known was a passage. Evelyn was not there. She had not been there all evening. She had been in the library, because Harry had not gone to find her.

Harry did not run.

He thought about it. He thought about the Cloak in his pocket, and how easy it would be. He thought about it for about a second.

Then he looked at Draco.

Draco was looking back at him. He was smiling. It was not their fathers' smile, Lucius's or Corvin's, polished in a mirror. It was his own. It was thin and white and very, very pleased.

"I was frightened all the way up the stairs, Potter," said Draco softly. "Did you feel it?"

Harry did not say anything.

"I practised," said Draco. "Since January. Since the landing outside her office. I sat in the common room every night and thought about being frightened. I thought about the errand. I thought about it until I could do it whenever I wanted." His smile widened. "And then I stood by Boris for an hour and did it at you."

Harry read him.

He went down into him, the whole way, past the triumph, looking for the lie. Looking for the place where Draco had been doing it, the place where the fear had been made and not felt.

He could not find it.

That was the thing he thought about, afterwards. Not that Draco had done it. That he had done it so well that even now, standing in front of him, Harry could not find the seam.

"Five years," said Draco. "Five years you've been looking at me like that. Like you're listening. Five years you've always known." He leaned a little closer. "Not tonight."

* * *

Umbridge's office was very crowded.

She had sent for the Minister. He came by Floo at a quarter past nine, in a lime-green bowler hat, with Kingsley Shacklebolt and a tough-looking wizard with very short wiry hair called Dawlish behind him, and Percy Weasley behind them with a quill and a roll of parchment and an expression of suppressed delight.

Dumbledore was there.

He had been sent for too. He was standing by the window with his hands behind his back, and he was closed, entirely, the way he had been in Courtroom Ten, and he did not look at Harry.

Harry stood in the middle of the office, between the fifty-six kittens, and Hermione's parchment was on the desk in front of Umbridge, and on it were twenty-six names, and his own was the first.

"Well, Potter," said Fudge. "I expect you know why you're here."

"Yes, Minister."

"Do you? Do you?" Fudge rubbed his hands together. "Then perhaps you'd care to tell us. In your own words. What exactly is this?"

"A list, Minister."

"A list of *what*?"

"Names," said Harry.

Kingsley, by the fireplace, made a very small sound that might have been a cough.

"Don't be clever with me, boy," said Fudge. "You were found coming out of a secret room on the seventh floor with twenty-odd students at half past eight at night. In flagrant breach of Educational Decree Number Twenty-Four. And this…" he held up the parchment, "…was pinned to the wall. With your name at the top of it. Under *that*."

"It's in alphabetical order by first name," said Harry. "Except mine. Mine's first because Hermione wrote it first."

"*Why?*"

"You'd have to ask her, Minister."

Fudge went a deeper shade of purple. Percy wrote it all down.

Harry read the Minister while he did it. He was delighted, still, under the purple. He was so delighted he could barely hold the parchment still. He had wanted something for ten months, since a night in June in a hospital wing, and he had wanted it so much that he had stopped noticing he wanted it, and here it was, in his hand, with a name at the top.

Umbridge had found it on the wall of the Room. She had gone in after them, when the door was still there, and taken it down, and read the name at the top.

*Dumbledore's Army.*

She had read it out loud in the corridor. She had read it in a voice that went up and up and up, like a kettle.

She was not shouting now.

Harry read her.

He had expected anger. He had expected it to be very large, and very sweet, the anger of a woman who enjoys making people smaller and has just been handed a great many of them to make smaller at once.

Instead there was hurt.

She was hurt. She was genuinely, sincerely hurt. It was at the top of her, very large and very soft and very wounded, and it was aimed at him. Under all the pink and all the sweetness and all the certainty, there was a fifty-year-old woman who had poured a boy tea five times since September and remembered how he took it, and who had believed every word he said, and who had just found out that he had been laughing at her the whole time.

She felt betrayed.

Harry stood in the middle of her office and read it and, very nearly, laughed.

He did not. He kept his face exactly as it was. But it was there, in him, and he knew it was there, and he knew it was not kind. She had a quill in the drawer of that desk that made children cut words into their own hands. She had put it into Neville's hand on the second Thursday of term and watched him use it for two hours. And she was standing in front of him now, hurt, because a boy she liked had told her lies.

"I trusted you," said Umbridge.

"I know, Professor."

"I *trusted* you, Mr Potter. I gave you…" She stopped. She took a breath. "I gave you every opportunity."

"Yes, Professor."

"And you…"

"Dolores," said Fudge. "Dolores. Please." He was purple with something that was mostly delight. He was holding the parchment. "*Dumbledore's Army.* Well. Well, well, well. I think that tells us everything, doesn't it?" He turned to the window. "Dumbledore. I think you have some explaining to do."

Dumbledore turned round.

* * *

He took the blame.

He did it simply and pleasantly, standing by the window with his hands behind his back. He said that the name on the parchment was accurate. He said that it was, indeed, his army. He said that he had asked Harry to recruit students for it, and that the first meeting had been his idea, and that he had been planning, for some months, to use it to overthrow the Minister for Magic and take control of the Ministry himself.

"No," said Harry.

Dumbledore looked at him over his spectacles. It was a mild, kind, entirely unreadable look.

"Harry," he said. "Please be quiet."

Harry was quiet.

He read the office instead. Fudge was so delighted he could barely stand still. Percy was writing as fast as his quill would go. Dawlish was frowning, slightly, as though something did not add up but it was not his business to say what. Kingsley was perfectly calm, the large still river, and under the calm, very quietly, he was watching Dumbledore's hands.

And Dumbledore was closed.

Harry could not get anything out of him. Not a crack. Not a flicker. He stood by the window in his long blue robes and told the Minister for Magic, quite cheerfully, that he had been plotting a coup, and Harry did not know whether he was frightened or angry or sad.

"Then you leave me no choice," said Fudge. "Dawlish. Shacklebolt. Take him."

"Ah," said Dumbledore. "Now. I think we are going to hit a little snag there."

He did not raise his wand. He did not do anything that Harry could see.

He opened, very slightly.

It was only for a second. It was only a crack, the width of a door left ajar. But through it, quite suddenly, quite clearly, Harry read Albus Dumbledore.

It was delight.

It was grim, and old, and very fierce, and it was the delight of a man who has been sitting very still for a very long time, being patient, being polite, being reasonable, and who has just been given an excuse to stop. He would not have missed this for anything. He would not have missed it for the world.

There was a flash of silver light. There was a bang like a gunshot. Fawkes was there, in the middle of the ceiling, in a burst of gold and scarlet. Kingsley was on the floor. Dawlish was on the floor. Fudge was on the floor, with his bowler hat over one ear. Percy was under the desk.

Umbridge had been knocked flat against the wall of kittens, and several of the kittens were yowling.

And Dumbledore, quite unhurried, reached up and took hold of Fawkes's tail.

He looked at Harry. Just for a moment. It was the first time he had looked at Harry all evening.

"Keep them safe," he said.

There was a flash of fire.

He was gone.

* * *

Educational Decree Number Twenty-Eight was on every noticeboard in the school by breakfast.

*Dolores Jane Umbridge (High Inquisitor) has replaced Albus Dumbledore as Head of Hogwarts School of Witchcraft and Wizardry.*

She could not get into his office. The gargoyle would not move for her. Harry read the castle know it, all day, a small hard pleasure running through every house at once, aimed at a stone gargoyle on the second floor.

Fred and George left on the Thursday.

They did not tell anybody they were going. Harry read it on them at breakfast, a kind of fizzing, the feeling of two people who have been holding a match to a fuse under the table and are waiting to see it catch. He did not say anything. He did not want to spoil it.

It caught at a quarter past eleven.

It caught in the entrance hall, and on the first floor, and the second, all at once, and it was fireworks.

They were not ordinary fireworks. They were enormous. They were dragons made of green and gold sparks that flew up the marble staircase and round the banisters and roared. They were pink and silver Catherine wheels five feet across that went whizzing through the corridors like flying saucers. There were rockets with long tails of silver stars that bounced off the walls, and sparklers that wrote swear words in the air on their own, and firecrackers that went off everywhere like mines.

Umbridge tried a Stunning Spell on one of the Catherine wheels and it exploded into ten more.

She tried Vanishing one of the dragons and it multiplied by four.

Nobody helped her. McGonagall stood in the door of her classroom and watched a rocket go past her nose and said, quite mildly, "Dear me, Professor Umbridge, I'm not sure that I have the authority," and went back inside and shut the door.

And at the end, when the whole castle was full of smoke and noise and sparks, Fred and George came down the marble staircase on two broomsticks that had been chained in Umbridge's office, and they flew three times round the entrance hall with the doors open and the whole school watching.

"If anybody fancies buying a Portable Swamp," said Fred, "as demonstrated upstairs, come to number ninety-three, Diagon Alley. Weasleys' Wizarding Wheezes."

"Special discounts," said George, "to Hogwarts students who swear they're going to use our products to get rid of this old bat."

They looked at each other. Then they looked down at Harry, standing in the middle of the entrance hall with the Slytherins round him, and George, or Fred, winked.

They flew out of the doors and away over the grounds into the sunshine.

And the school laughed.

* * *

Harry had read the whole of the Great Hall at once a great many times. He had read it angry and he had read it frightened and he had read it hating him. He had read it cheering, at the first task, and he had read it grieving, on the pitch, at the end of the maze, so loudly that he could not turn it down.

He had never read it laughing.

It was everywhere. It was the whole castle. It was every house and every year and most of the staff, it was the portraits on the walls and the ghosts in the corridors and Peeves upside down above the door, it was a thousand people laughing at once, at the same thing, for the same reason, and the reason was joy.

It went through him like warm water.

He stood in the entrance hall in the smoke and the sparks and let it come. He did not turn it down. He did not want to. He stood there with his face turned up towards the open doors, where two specks were going away over the lake towards the mountains, and he read the whole school laugh, and it was the best noise he had heard in months.

Beside him, Evelyn said, "That was ninety-three. Diagon Alley. They've had the lease since February."

"How do you know?"

"I read the notices in the *Prophet*. Property transfers. Page fourteen." She watched the specks. "Nobody reads page fourteen."

Harry laughed.

He was still laughing when Umbridge came down the marble staircase with her hair full of sparks.

She looked at him. She looked at him across the entrance hall for a long moment, with the whole school round her still laughing, and he read her, and the hurt was gone.

It had gone hard.

He had seen that happen to fear once, in Cornelius Fudge, on a night in June. He had never seen it happen to hurt. It had gone hard and cold and small and it was aimed straight at him, and under it there was the sweet, gentle enjoyment of watching somebody be made smaller, and it was not general any more.

It had a name in it.

* * *

"He lied to you," said Evelyn.

They were in the fourth-floor classroom. It was Saturday. It was raining, hard, against the long windows, and she had her feet on the desk in front and her book on her knees, and she had not opened it.

"With a feeling," said Harry.

"That's still lying."

"I know it is. I didn't know you could. Not with a feeling. Not so I couldn't tell." He was sitting on the desk by the window. He had been sitting there for an hour. "I've been reading people since before I could talk. Everybody lies. Everybody says things they don't mean. But the feeling's always underneath, and the feeling's always true. That's the whole point. That's the only thing I've ever been sure of."

"Was it true?"

"What?"

"His fear. On the fifth floor. For an hour." Evelyn looked at him. "Was it real fear? Or was it pretend?"

Harry thought about it.

"It was real," he said slowly. "That's the thing. It was real. He wasn't pretending to be frightened. He made himself frightened. He sat there and thought about the errand until he was. And then he stood by Boris and was frightened at me for an hour and five minutes, and it was real the whole time, and it was a lie."

"So he didn't lie with a feeling," said Evelyn. "He lied with a true one."

Harry did not say anything.

"Grandmother used to say the best lies are made of true things," said Evelyn. "Put in the wrong order. She said that's how my grandfather did it." She turned a page of the book without looking at it. "I don't suppose Malfoy's read the right books. I expect he worked it out on his own." She paused. "He's had five years to think about it, Potter. Five years of you looking at him like a newspaper."

"You think it's my fault."

"I think it's very clever," said Evelyn. "And I think you should have come and got me."

He looked at her.

"I'd have seen him," she said. "I wouldn't have felt anything. I'd have seen a boy standing by a statue for an hour on the fifth floor with nothing in his hands, not going anywhere, and I'd have said, *Potter, he's not going anywhere*, and you'd have said *he's frightened*, and I'd have said *so what*." She looked back at her book. "That's what I'm for."

"I know."

"Then next time," said Evelyn, "come and get me."

* * *

Theo's letter came on the Monday.

It came by an ordinary school owl, not the Nott family's, which was the first thing Harry noticed. The second was that Theo read it at breakfast and folded it in half and put it in his pocket and went on eating his porridge, and did not say anything, and did not stop eating.

Harry read him.

He had read Theo every day for nearly five years. The calm sadness at the bottom like water in a well. The bright care. The curiosity. The new thing since June, the boy at the edge of a drop, who had been standing there all year and had got used to the view.

The drop was closer.

That was all. Nothing else had changed. Theo was as calm as he always was. But the edge had moved, in the night, a long way towards him, and he was standing on it now, quite still, with his toes over.

"Your father," said Harry, that evening, in the gap between their beds.

"Malfoy wrote to his. His wrote to mine." Theo was lying on his back with his hands behind his head. "Mine wants to know why my name was on a list with yours. Under Dumbledore's."

"What are you going to tell him?"

"The truth," said Theo. "That I wanted to learn to do a Shield Charm properly. And that you're the best teacher I've ever had." He was quiet for a while. "He won't believe the second part. He thinks you're a jumped-up half-blood with a scar. He said so in the churchyard, apparently. Malfoy told everybody."

"I didn't hear that bit."

"No. You were busy." Theo turned his head on the pillow. "He wants me home at Easter. He says we need to have a talk about my future."

Harry read the edge, and the drop, and Theo standing very still on it.

"Theo…"

"It's all right," said Theo. "I've known since June that we were going to have to have it. I just thought I'd have longer." He turned back to the canopy. "I'll tell you what he says."

"You don't have to."

"I know," said Theo. "I'll tell you anyway."

* * *

The detentions started on the Friday.

He had expected them. He had expected to be sent for, at five o'clock, to the pink office, and he went, and she was sitting behind the desk with a long thin black quill on the blotter in front of her, and a sheet of parchment, and she smiled at him very sweetly.

"Lines, I think, Mr Potter," she said. "Sit down."

He sat down.

He did not need to be told what the quill was. He had read it on Neville's hand in October. He had read it on the other Harry's hand, an old faint soreness, in another world, before he understood what it was.

"You'll be writing *I must not tell lies*," said Umbridge. "As many times as it takes to sink in."

Harry looked at her.

He thought about the Wizengamot, and the second floor, and the Ravenclaws in the Arithmancy room. He thought about *I was in a maze, Professor, I may have been mistaken*. He thought about five cups of tea with no sugar, and a biscuit in a pink napkin thrown in the lake, and the hurt, which had been real.

He thought about how many lies he had told in this office.

He did not laugh. He was quite careful not to. But it came up in him, very quietly, and he let it sit there while he picked up the quill, and it made the next two hours easier.

*I must not tell lies.*

It wrote in red. It wrote in his own blood, which came out of the nib without any ink at all, and as it wrote, the same words cut themselves into the back of his right hand, and healed over, and cut themselves again.

*I must not tell lies.*

He wrote it four hundred and eleven times.

He counted. Evelyn would want to know.

* * *

The detentions went on for nine days.

On the ninth day the words did not heal over any more. They stayed, faint and white, across the back of his right hand, and they would stay, Madam Pomfrey said, when Hermione dragged him to the hospital wing, for the rest of his life. She soaked his hand in a bowl of yellow liquid that smelled of murtlap and stopped it stinging, and was very angry with everybody, and said a great many things about the new Headmistress that she would certainly not have said in front of a student if she had been thinking.

Harry looked at the back of his hand.

*I must not tell lies.*

"It's not funny," said Hermione.

"No," agreed Harry.

"Then why are you smiling?"

He did not know how to tell her. He thought about it.

"It's the wrong hand," he said, eventually.

Hermione stared at him.

"I write with my right," said Harry. "But I lie with all of me."

She went on staring at him for a while. Then something happened in her face, and she put her hand over her mouth, and her shoulders shook, and Madam Pomfrey looked round from the next bed and said "*Miss Granger*," and Hermione had to go and stand in the corridor until she had stopped.

* * *

The scar was worse without Dumbledore.

He did not know why. He had never really known what Dumbledore did, or whether he did anything at all. But when Dumbledore had been in the castle, in his office at the top of the moving stair, the scar had been something that happened at night, a few nights a week, faint, far away, the way a sound comes up through a floor.

Now it was every night.

He stopped going out along it.

He stopped the night after the Room, and he did not start again. He had gone out seven times, and the eighth time the cold had turned round and walked along his shelves and found the one book in the world that told it why it could never reach a summit. He was not going to go out a ninth time.

It did not matter.

It came in on him instead.

It came in at night, when he was asleep, or nearly asleep, or lying awake in the green dark trying not to be. It came in along the thread without being asked, the way the cold comes in under a door, and it was not faint any more, and it was not far away. He lay in his four-poster with the curtains drawn and Theo breathing in the next bed and the water moving against the window, and the cold came in through his scar and filled him up, and it was not his, and he could not make it stop.

Sometimes it was patience.

Sometimes it was anticipation, which was worse.

Sometimes, at three or four in the morning, it was something Harry did not have a word for. It was very large and very slow and very careful, and it was the feeling of somebody laying things out on a table in a particular order, and checking the order, and moving one thing a quarter of an inch to the left, and checking again.

He lay awake on the last night of April and felt it.

No glee in it, and no impatience, and none of the cold slow satisfaction of a man putting a key into a lock. It was steadier than all of those, and quieter, and it went on and on, hour after hour, and it was aimed at a place.

He did not know the place. He could feel the shape of it through the thread, faintly, the way you feel the shape of a room in the dark with your hands. It was round. It was very deep. It was very still, and there was something in the middle of it that moved without any wind.

He knew what the feeling was.

He had felt it once, in himself, on the night before the first task, sitting on his bed in the dormitory with a dragon in the morning and nothing to do but wait.

It was preparation.

It was not his.
