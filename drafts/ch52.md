## Chapter 52: The Flight

Draco Malfoy was afraid at dinner on the twenty-seventh of March, and Harry read him, and believed it.

It was a Wednesday. The group met on Wednesdays now, and sometimes Tuesdays, and once in March on a Sunday afternoon, because Harry had changed the nights every fortnight since February. He had not told anybody why. Evelyn had not asked. She had looked at him across the breakfast table on the morning he first did it, and nodded, and gone back to her egg.

Draco was at the far end of the Slytherin table with a letter beside his plate.

Harry read him over the shepherd's pie. He did it every meal now. He had done it every meal since the stairs in January, since *you do it to her too*, since Harry had noticed, too late, that Draco had started to think.

Draco was frightened.

It was a very plain fear tonight. It was on the top of him and it was small and grey and miserable, and it was aimed at the letter, and it was about his mother. Harry could feel the shape of it. It was the fear of a boy whose mother has written to say that something is wrong at home and has not said what. It went round and round in Draco, the way fear does when there is nothing to do with it.

There was nothing under it about Harry.

Harry checked. He went all the way down. He went past the fear for his mother and past the pride that was always there now, since the errand, and past the old fury from September, which had gone quiet. There was nothing at the bottom aimed at Harry at all. No thinking. No putting side by side. Draco was a boy at a dinner table worrying about his mother.

Harry let go of him.

He did not think about Draco again until twenty past eight.

* * *

The Room of Requirement was full that night. Twenty-six. They had got all the way to the Patronus Charm, which Harry had not been sure they would, and he had been teaching it since the start of March.

It was going badly and well at once.

It was going badly because it was very hard, and most of them could not do it, and there was a great deal of silver mist and a great deal of swearing. It was going well because Hermione had done an otter on the second night, and Luna a hare on the third, and Ginny a horse, and Ernie Macmillan a boar, and Ron, that night, at a quarter past eight, after three weeks of mist, a Jack Russell terrier that went round and round the room yapping silently and then jumped up at Hermione and vanished.

Ron stared at the place where it had been.

Harry read him. Astonishment, the plain kind, the kind from a prefect's badge in August. And under it, coming up very fast, a pleasure he did not seem to think he was allowed.

"I did it," said Ron.

"You did," said Harry.

"It was a *dog*."

"A terrier," said Evelyn, from the wall. She had not done a Patronus yet. She had not, as far as Harry could tell, tried very hard. "A Jack Russell. Eleven inches at the shoulder, approximately."

"Is that good?"

"It's a dog," said Evelyn. "It's not a value judgement."

Ron laughed. It was the first time Harry had heard Ron laugh at something Evelyn said. Evelyn did not look up from her book, but she turned a page that she had not finished, which Harry had learned meant something.

He was standing in the middle of the room, by Neville, who was very close, he could feel it, the mist coming thicker every time. He was not reading the door.

He had stopped reading the door in November.

* * *

It came through the wall.

There was no door in the wall. There had been no door in that part of the wall for six months. And at twenty-five past eight it was there, plain and wooden, and it opened.

Harry read who was behind it a second before it opened. Only a second. He had been reading Neville.

It was a crowd. Seven or eight of them, close together, in the corridor outside. And all of them were the same thing at once, at the top, loud, and the thing was triumph.

He turned round.

Draco Malfoy came through the door first.

He had his wand out and his prefect's badge on, and behind him were Crabbe and Goyle and Pansy Parkinson and Warrington, and a sixth-year Harry did not know, and behind all of them, very small, in a pink cardigan, with her wand held up in front of her like a candle, came Professor Umbridge.

Harry read Draco.

Triumph. On the top, enormous, the loudest thing in the room. And under it, coming up, as the door opened and Draco's eyes found Harry, there was something cold and clear and very pleased with itself.

It was knowing.

Draco had known all along. All through dinner. All through the letter. He had sat at the far end of the table with the letter beside his plate and he had been *afraid*, on purpose, for Harry to read. He had made the fear. He had made it about his mother because he knew Harry would not look under it. He had put it on the top of himself like a cloth over a cage.

And he had kept the knowing underneath, very small, very still, so that Harry would go all the way down and find nothing.

Harry had found nothing.

"*Expelliarmus*," said Draco.

* * *

It was not a fight. It might have been. There were twenty-six of them in the room and seven in the door. But Harry had spent six months teaching them to defend themselves, and it had been against the Dark Arts, and it had not been against their own Head of House's prefects and a teacher in a cardigan.

Most of them ran.

There was a second door. Harry made it. He did not know he could. He thought, very hard, *a way out*, and the Room heard him, the way it always did, and at the back, by the cabinets, the wall opened, and people went through it, Hufflepuffs first, then Ravenclaws, then everybody.

He put a wall over the gap.

He did it without his wand, which Draco had. He did it in the heads of the seven people at the front door. They looked at the back of the room and saw a wall, plain stone, with nothing in it, and twenty people running at it and going through it, which made no sense, and they hesitated.

It was the hesitation he wanted. Two seconds. Three.

Draco did not hesitate.

Draco looked at the wall, and Harry read him look at it, and there was something new in him, very fast. He did not believe it. He looked straight at the wall where twenty people were disappearing and he did not believe his own eyes, because he had learned, over five years, that when Harry Potter was in a room, what you saw was not always what was there.

"It's not real!" Draco shouted. "It's not real, there's a door, *go*…"

Crabbe went at the wall. He went through it. Pansy went after him.

They caught nine.

* * *

They caught Harry, because he had not run, because he had been holding the wall. They caught Neville, who had been next to him and had stayed next to him. They caught Ron, who had come back for Neville. They caught Hermione, who had come back for Ron. They caught Millicent, who had come back for all of them and had knocked Goyle over on the way and was sitting on him.

They caught a Hufflepuff third-year called Owen Cauldwell, who had tripped.

They caught Theo, who had walked very calmly out of the back door and then very calmly walked back in again, and stood beside Harry with his hands in his pockets.

They caught Luna, who had not run at all and had been sitting on a cushion by the bookshelves the whole time, humming.

They did not catch Evelyn.

Harry did not know how. He had last seen her by the wall with her book. He looked round once, in all of it, and she was not there, and the book was not there either. He did not see her go. He did not see anybody see her go.

He thought, much later, that nobody had looked.

* * *

Professor Umbridge's office was very full.

The Minister for Magic was there, in his lime-green bowler hat, very red, rubbing his hands. Kingsley Shacklebolt was there, tall and calm in long blue robes, by the window. A wizard Harry did not know, with very short tough-looking hair, stood by the door. Percy Weasley was there with a quill and a long roll of parchment, and he would not look at Ron. Professor McGonagall was there, very white, beside the desk.

And Dumbledore was there, behind the desk, in Umbridge's chair, which she had given him without seeming to notice she was doing it.

The nine of them stood in a line in front of it.

Harry read the room. He read Fudge, and the fear gone to *no* was still there, as it had been since June, but on top of it tonight there was something greedy and delighted. He read Kingsley, who was calm, and under the calm was a decision being made, carefully, and not by anybody else. He read the wizard by the door, Dawlish, who was simply interested, the way a man is interested in a football score. He read Percy, who was frightened and ashamed and very busy writing.

He read Professor Umbridge.

He read her last, because he did not want to.

She was standing beside Fudge with her hands folded in front of her and her face very pink, and she was smiling, the way she always smiled, and she was looking at him.

She was hurt.

He had not expected that. He went down her looking for the anger, and it was there, and he went down looking for the triumph, and that was there too. But on the top of both of them, sincere and warm and entirely genuine, she was *hurt*. She was hurt the way a woman is hurt who has given somebody tea every Friday for six months and two sugars, and has told him things, and has trusted him, and has found out.

He had lied to her. She had believed him. And she felt it, now, all the way down, as a personal betrayal by somebody she had been fond of.

It was very nearly funny.

He did not laugh. He did not move his face at all. But somewhere at the back of his head he felt something that was not quite laughter and was not quite pity, and it sat there, and did not go away.

* * *

"A secret society," said Fudge. "Meeting in the school. In direct contravention of Decree Twenty-Four. Run by a *fifth-year*." He rounded on Dumbledore. "Do you deny it?"

"I don't believe I have been asked anything yet," said Dumbledore mildly.

"Mr Malfoy," said Professor Umbridge, sweetly, "has very kindly prepared a list."

Draco stepped forward.

He held out a piece of parchment. Harry looked at it. There were names on it, in Draco's neat cramped hand, twenty-two of them, and they were the right names. Not all of them. But the right ones.

*Lists get found*, he thought. He had never made one.

Draco had.

Draco had made it by watching. By standing at the end of a corridor on the seventh floor for six weeks, on nights Harry had not checked, and writing down who went past.

Harry read him, standing there with the list, and the triumph was huge. And under the triumph, very small, so small that Draco did not seem to know it was there, there was something else, aimed at Harry, and it was a question. It had been there since the stairs. It was not *what are you doing*. It was *can you tell, now*.

Harry could.

He did not say so.

* * *

Fudge read the list. He read it out loud, slowly, with relish, and every name he read, he looked up at the nine in the line, and at Dumbledore behind the desk.

"And who," said Fudge, when he had finished, "is behind all this? Who put the boy up to it? Because I don't believe for a moment that a fifteen-year-old…"

"I did," said Dumbledore.

The room went still.

Harry read it go still. He read every person in it stop at once, the way a clock stops, and he read Fudge's delight go up so high and so fast that it nearly turned into something else.

"*You*," said Fudge.

"I asked Mr Potter to run it," said Dumbledore. "I suggested the Room. I chose the subjects. I am, I'm afraid, entirely responsible." He smiled at Fudge over his half-moon spectacles. "It was, I'm sorry to say, going rather well."

Harry opened his mouth.

Dumbledore did not look at him. He did not need to. Harry read him, and there was one thing in the old man aimed at Harry just then, and it was very plain and very gentle and it said *don't*.

Harry shut his mouth.

He read McGonagall, beside the desk, go from white to something like iron, and then to something like grief, and then back to iron, in about two seconds. He read Kingsley, by the window, finish making his decision.

"Then you'll come with us," said Fudge. His voice had gone high. "Dawlish. Shacklebolt. Albus Dumbledore, you are under arrest for conspiracy against the Ministry and sedition and… and… you will be escorted to Azkaban to await…"

"No," said Dumbledore pleasantly. "I don't think so, Cornelius."

* * *

Harry read him.

He read him stand up out of Umbridge's chair, quite slowly, and he read what was in him as he did it, and he had never read it in Dumbledore before.

It was delight.

It was grim, and nowhere near happy. It was the delight of a very old man who had been sitting still for a very long time, in an office, watching a war start through other people's letters, being careful, being watched, being patient, and who had just been handed, quite unexpectedly, on a Wednesday evening in March, a reason to stop.

He would not have missed it for anything.

"I have been to Azkaban," said Dumbledore. "Twice, on business. I did not care for the catering. I have a great deal to do this spring, and very little of it can be done from a cell."

Dawlish drew his wand.

The room went white. There was no sound at all, and then a great deal of it, all at once, like a door blown in by a gale, and the floor jumped under Harry's feet. Harry felt a hand on the back of his neck and another on his shoulder, and he was on the floor, and so were Ron and Hermione and Neville and Theo and Luna and Millicent and Owen Cauldwell, and McGonagall was crouched over them with her arms out.

When he looked up, Fudge was on the carpet, and Umbridge, and Dawlish, and Kingsley, all four of them flat and still. Percy was under the desk.

Dumbledore was standing in the middle of the room with his wand out and Fawkes on his shoulder.

"Kingsley," said Dumbledore quietly, to the man on the floor by the window. "I'm sorry. I'm afraid I had to. It would look very odd otherwise." He turned to McGonagall. "Minerva. The school is yours. Look after them."

"Albus…"

"I know." He looked down at the nine on the floor. His eyes went along the line, one by one, and stopped at the end. At Harry.

He looked at Harry for perhaps a second.

Harry read him. The delight. The grimness under it. And under that, at the very bottom, held hard with all his weight, the door with something behind it that had Harry's name on.

It was still shut.

"Do the work," said Dumbledore to him. "Keep the door closed. You know which door."

Fawkes rose.

There was a flash of fire, very bright, and a sound like a great bird beating its wings once, and the office was full of the smell of something burning that was not unpleasant, and Dumbledore was gone.

* * *

Draco was in the common room when Harry came down at midnight.

He was sitting by the fire, alone, in the high-backed chair nobody else used, with his feet on the fender. Crabbe and Goyle had gone up. Pansy had gone up. The room was empty and green and the lake moved against the windows, and Draco had been waiting.

Harry read him from the bottom of the stairs.

He did it carefully this time. He went down slowly, floor by floor, and he did not stop at the first thing he found, or the second. On the top, Draco was tired. Under it, pleased. Under that, a long way down, there was the old fear, the one that had always been there, of not being good enough. It was quieter than Harry had ever felt it.

And there was nothing about his mother anywhere.

"She's fine," said Draco, without turning round. "My mother. Since you're wondering. She wrote to say the peacocks have got mites."

Harry came across the room and stood by the fire.

"You made it up," he said.

"I made it up." Draco looked at the coals. "Not the letter. The letter's real. The mites are real. I just sat there at dinner and thought about her, and about something being wrong at home, and about my father in a churchyard, and I let it come up. I let it get big. I put it on the top." He was quiet for a moment. "And then I kept everything else very small. Underneath. Very still. Like you'd keep something in your pocket you didn't want anybody to hear jingle."

Harry did not say anything.

"I've been frightened in front of you for five years," said Draco. "Since the first night. Since the feast. You looked at me at the table and I knew you could tell. I didn't know how. I just knew. Every time I was frightened, you knew it, and you looked at me, and you didn't even have to say anything." He turned his head at last. "So I thought, if I'm going to be frightened in front of him anyway, I might as well learn to do it on purpose."

"How long?"

"Since January. Since the stairs." Draco almost smiled. "I practised on Crabbe. He's useless. He can't tell anything. So I practised on Pansy. She can tell when you're sad. That's all she can tell. I got very good at being sad."

Harry read him.

He read the pride in it. It was a different pride from the errand-pride, smaller and harder and entirely Draco's own. He read, under it, something that was very nearly the opposite of what Draco had felt at the feast in their first year, with his hand out, being refused.

It was respect. It went both ways, and Draco did not like it either.

"It won't work again," said Harry.

"Probably not," said Draco. "But it worked once." He took his feet off the fender. "And now you'll never be quite sure, will you. Every time you read me. You'll always wonder if I'm doing it on purpose."

He went up to bed.

Harry stood by the fire for a long time.

He thought about a boy at a feast with his hand out. He thought about five years of reading every flicker of fear in Draco Malfoy, and using it, and never once thinking about what it was like to be read.

He thought Draco was right. He would wonder.

* * *

Educational Decree Number Twenty-Eight was on every noticeboard in the castle by breakfast.

Dolores Jane Umbridge, High Inquisitor, had replaced Albus Dumbledore as Head of Hogwarts School of Witchcraft and Wizardry.

The gargoyle would not let her into the Head's office. Harry heard about that at lunch, from Ron, who had heard it from Fred, who had been watching from behind a suit of armour. The gargoyle had simply not moved. She had shouted at it. It had looked at her.

"It sealed itself," said Ron, with enormous satisfaction. "Against her. The *office*."

Harry read him say it. Satisfaction. And under it, steadily, aimed at Harry, something that had been gratitude since December and had turned, in Umbridge's office, watching Harry stand in the line with his mouth shut, into something that Ron had no word for and did not seem to want one.

"Ron," said Harry.

"What?"

"You came back for Neville."

Ron shrugged. He went red.

"You'd have come back for me," he said. "Wouldn't you?"

Harry thought about it. He thought about lying. He did not, with Ron, any more. He was not sure when that had started.

"Yes," he said.

"Well then."

* * *

Fred and George left on the second Friday after.

They left in the middle of the afternoon. Everybody who was there remembered it differently afterwards, and all of them were certain.

He remembered the noise first.

It came up from the entrance hall, a bang so loud that the floor of the Charms corridor shook, and then another, and then a sort of roaring, whooshing, crackling, shrieking noise, and Flitwick dropped his wand and everybody ran to the window.

There were dragons in the air.

They were made of green and gold sparks and they were enormous, and they went up the main staircase in a long glittering coil, breathing out stars. Behind them came pinwheels of purple fire that ran along the banisters like cats. Rockets went off against the ceiling of the entrance hall and came down as rain that did not wet anybody. Somebody's set of small blue bangers had got into the suits of armour and were going off inside them, so that the armour on the first floor clanked and jumped and saluted.

And there was Professor Umbridge, in the middle of the entrance hall, with a Stunning Spell going off from her wand, and a rocket the size of a man exploding at it and multiplying into ten.

Harry got to the top of the marble staircase in time to see Fred and George.

They were standing on the stairs below him with their brooms in their hands. They had summoned them from somewhere, and the brooms had come smashing through doors, one of them trailing a length of heavy chain and an iron peg, and they mounted, both together, and kicked off.

"Ninety-three, Diagon Alley," said Fred, loudly, to the whole school. "Opening Monday. Swamps, sweets, and very reasonable rates on fireworks."

"Ten per cent off," said George, "to anybody who can prove they used them on a High Inquisitor."

And then they looked up the staircase, both at once, and saw Harry.

George winked.

Fred raised one hand. Harry knew what was in it. He had put a heavy bag into it on a platform in June. He did not have to read them to know what they were thinking. He read them anyway.

It was the debt again. Going the other way. Paid.

They went out through the front doors, which were open, in a long blaze of light, and wheeled over the grounds, and were gone into the sunset.

* * *

He read the school laugh.

He had never read the whole school do anything at once except be afraid. He had read the Great Hall be afraid at a Sorting and on a morning in January and on a night in June. He had read it be angry, and be cold, and turn away from him. He had read it roar for him on the night of a dragon and he had read it go silent.

He had never read it laugh.

It went up from the entrance hall and it went up through the castle, floor by floor, the way a fire goes up a building. It was in every corridor. It was in the Hufflepuffs, who were shrieking, and the Ravenclaws, who were trying very hard to be above it and failing, and the Gryffindors, who were cheering. It was in the Slytherins. Not all of them. A good many. It was in the ghosts, faintly, a thin grey note that had gone, for once, very slightly gold.

It was in the teachers.

He read McGonagall, standing at the top of the staircase opposite with her lips pressed together very tightly, and he read her laugh. Not on her face. Under it. All the way down.

He read Flitwick laugh. He read Sprout laugh. He read Peeves, who did not exactly have feelings, saluting the twins as they went with a noise Harry had never heard a poltergeist make.

He stood at the top of the marble staircase with the dragons going over his head and the Catherine wheels whizzing past and the whole of Hogwarts laughing round him, every floor of it, and it was the best noise he had heard in months.

It was the best noise he had ever heard.

* * *

He was in detention by the Monday.

He had been out of her circle since the night of the Room. There had been no more tea. There had been no more *Mr Potter*. She had passed him in a corridor on the Thursday after and looked at him, and smiled, and the smile had nothing in it at all.

Harry read her.

The hurt was still there. It was going down. And as it went down, something else was coming up underneath it to take its place, and it was the old thing, the sweet sincere enjoyment, only it had a direction now. It was aimed at one boy. It was warm and patient and it was very, very interested in him.

He was her particular enemy now.

She gave him detention for walking on the wrong side of a corridor. Then for looking at her in a way she did not like during a Defence lesson. Then for nothing, very nearly. Every evening at five, in the pink office, with the kittens on the plates.

She gave him the quill.

"Lines, Mr Potter," she said. "*I must not tell lies.*" She smiled at him. "As many times as it takes to sink in."

He wrote it.

He felt it go into the back of his left hand. He felt the cut. It was very small and very neat and it stung, and it did not stop stinging, and when he looked down the words were there in the skin, red and raw, in his own handwriting, which was the thing Neville had said. It was his own writing.

He wrote it two hundred times on the first evening.

He was about halfway through when he found it was funny.

He did not know why. He was sitting in a pink office with blood on the back of his hand, writing *I must not tell lies* in his own writing for a woman who had believed every lie he had ever told her for six months, and had thanked him for them, and had given him two sugars. He was writing it for her. He was writing it *at* her. It was the truest thing he had ever been made to write, and the most ridiculous, and both at once.

He did not laugh. He kept his face perfectly still.

But he read her, sitting at her desk across the room, watching him with her sweet warm patient interest, and he knew, the way you know a joke has landed, that she could not tell what he was thinking. She had never been able to. She had sat across from him for six months and never once known what was going on behind his face.

He went on writing.

*I must not tell lies.*

He thought he probably would. A great many more. He thought that was probably the point.

* * *

By the end of April the back of his left hand had white letters on it that did not go away.

Evelyn looked at them in the library. She did not say anything about them. She took his hand, quite briskly, the way she would have taken a book, and turned it to the light from the window, and read it, and let go.

"Your *s* leans backwards," she said. "It always has."

"Has it?"

"It does in your letters." She went back to her Arithmancy. "It's very neat. You've never been neat. You're neat when you mean it."

He looked at the back of his hand.

"Where did you go?" he said. "That night. In the Room."

"Out," said Evelyn. "Through the back. With the Hufflepuffs."

"Nobody stopped you."

"Nobody looked." She turned a page. "Malfoy had a list of twenty-two names. I've seen it. Parkinson showed it to Bulstrode to make her cry, and Bulstrode showed it to me to make Parkinson cry, which didn't work. I'm not on it." She did not look up. "I've been in that Room every Tuesday and Friday since October. He stood at the end of the corridor for six weeks and wrote down everybody who went past. He never wrote me down."

Harry looked at her.

"He didn't see you."

"Nobody sees me," said Evelyn. "I've told you that before. You thought I was being modest." She dipped her quill. "I wasn't. It's a fact. It's quite useful."

"Evelyn."

"What?"

"He lied to me. Malfoy. At dinner. With a feeling." Harry put his hand flat on the table, white letters up. "He made himself frightened about his mother, on purpose, and put it on the top, and kept the rest small underneath, and I went all the way down and didn't find it."

Evelyn put the quill down.

"Yes," she said. "I imagine he did."

"You knew he could do that?"

"I didn't know he could. I knew he would try." She looked at him. "Potter. You've been reading people since you were six. Everybody you've ever met has been read by you and not known. And Malfoy's known for five years. What did you think he'd do? Sit there and be read until he left school?" She picked the quill up again. "He did what I'd have done. If I weren't already doing something better."

"What's better?"

"Not having anything on top at all," said Evelyn, and went back to her Arithmancy.

He sat there for a while.

He did not know whether to be insulted. He decided, eventually, that he was not, and that she had meant him not to be, and that he could not tell, and that this was, as it had always been, the point.

* * *

The scar was worse without Dumbledore.

He did not know why. Dumbledore had never touched it. Dumbledore had never done anything about it at all, except ask him, once by letter and once by note and once with his last words before the fire, to close it. But since the night of the Room, the night the office had sealed itself, it had been different, and Harry knew it was different, and he lay in his four-poster at night and felt it be different.

He did not go in.

He had kept to that since before Christmas. He did not go down the well. He did not follow the line. He lay on his back in the green dark and he did the first exercise, *be quiet in thyself*, over and over, and he did not go anywhere at all.

It came in on him instead.

It came every night now. Not words. Not pictures, mostly. Feelings, very strong, very cold, coming up through the scar the way water comes up through a floor when the ground underneath has flooded. Impatience. Satisfaction. A long, cold, careful attention to something, night after night, like a man going over and over the same small piece of work by lamplight.

And once, at the end of April, a picture.

A corridor. Stone. Very long. A plain black door at the end of it, which he had seen in a dream about a snake and in another world, at night, running.

He woke and lay still.

He was not frightened. He was very calm, the way he was always calm afterwards. He lay in his four-poster and he listened to the lake and he read the dormitory, Theo asleep, Crabbe and Goyle asleep, Draco awake, at the far end, in the dark, not moving.

And through the scar, very faint, coming and going, there was something else.

Impatience had gone, and satisfaction. This was the feeling of a man who has been gathering things for a long time, in a quiet room, one by one, and has laid them out on a table, all of them, and is looking at them in the lamplight.

It was preparation.

It was not his. He knew it was not his. He lay very still and felt it anyway, coming up through the place on his forehead where a long white finger had been laid on him in a churchyard, and under it, low down, in the place he had found in the Room of Hidden Things, the cold small thing in him stirred.

It did not point at anything. There was nothing near enough to point at.

But it stirred, the way a dog stirs in its sleep when somebody it knows walks past the window.
