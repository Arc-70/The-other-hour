## Chapter 54: The Veil

The lift doors opened on the corridor, and it was the corridor from the dream about the snake.

Long and stone and bare. No windows. Torches in brackets, burning low. A plain black door at the end.

There was nobody sitting by it.

Harry stepped out. He read the corridor, and there was nobody in it, and he read the door, and there was nothing in a door. He read further. He read the rooms beyond the door, one after another, the way he had learned to read a building, with effort, floor by floor.

They were full.

"How many?" said Evelyn, beside him.

"I don't know. A lot. Twelve. Fifteen." He did not take his eyes off the door. "They're not in the first room. They're spread out. Waiting."

"Where's the Order?"

He reached for them. He found them behind him and above, coming down, fast, in two lifts and by the stairs, frightened and quick. Sirius among them. He could have found Sirius in a crowd of a thousand.

"Coming," he said. "Two minutes. Three."

"Do we wait?"

Harry thought about it.

He thought about a ring of five hungry things on stone steps, a long way down, and the patient cold thing in the middle of them that had looked up a lift shaft at him. He thought about how long fifty years was. He thought about how little a man would mind waiting another three minutes, at the end of it.

He thought about the cold thing in himself, pointing at the black door like a dog at a gate.

"No," he said. "He knows we're here. If we wait, he chooses when. If we go, I do."

"Right," said Ron. His voice was much higher than usual. "Good. Fine. What's the plan?"

"Stay together. Don't touch anything. If I say run, run, and don't come back for anybody."

"We came back for Neville in March," said Millicent.

"I know. Don't do it tonight."

"No promises," said Millicent.

Harry read her. She meant it. He read the others, quickly, one after another, the way he had read them on cushions in a Room on the seventh floor for eight months. Neville, terrified, and holding the terror down with both hands. Luna, calm, curious, looking at the black door as though it might have something interesting written on it. Hermione, terrified for Ron. Ron, terrified for Hermione. All of them afraid of what was behind the door.

None of them afraid of him.

He did not know why he noticed that. He noticed it.

"Potter," said Evelyn. "The door."

He opened the door.

* * *

It was a round room, and everything in it was black, the floor and the ceiling and the walls, and there were twelve identical black doors set round the walls at even intervals, and candles burning blue in brackets between them.

The door shut behind them.

The room began to spin.

Neville made a sound. The walls went round, very fast, a blur of blue flame and black, and then stopped, and every door looked exactly the same as every other door, and there was no way of telling which one they had come in by.

"Right," said Ron. "Brilliant."

"I know the way," said Harry.

He did. He had come through this room in another world, on a night in June, behind a statue and then in front of it, with a watch in Evelyn's hand that had to be wound in a place where time is held. He had been through four of these doors. He had felt each room behind each one.

He felt them now.

Behind one door, a ticking, glittering, roaring thing that was not alive, and a dead quiet room full of brains in tanks. Behind another, a great cold hush, endlessly high, and rows and rows of something faintly humming, and in the rows, in the dark between the shelves, people. A great many. Waiting.

And behind a third, far off, through that room and down, a ring of hunger on stone steps.

"Evelyn," he said.

"I'm marking them." She had a stick of chalk. Harry did not know where it had come from. She went to the door they were facing and drew a small neat X on it, very precisely, and said, "That's the one we came in by. I counted the turns. The room went round four times and a quarter. Clockwise." She looked at him. "Which?"

He pointed.

* * *

The Hall of Prophecy was as high as a church and as dim as one, and it was very cold.

Rows of shelves ran away from them into the dark. On the shelves, small dusty glass globes glowed faintly blue, thousands upon thousands, and the light from them was the only light there was. Silver numbers on brackets at the end of every row. Fifty-three. Fifty-four. Fifty-five.

Harry read the rows.

They were full of people. Behind the shelves, in the dark, between the globes. He could feel them on every side, like a wood full of animals that have gone still at the sound of a step. They were all one thing at the top, and the thing was anticipation, and it was not quite pleasure.

"Stay close," he said. "Don't touch anything."

He walked down the rows. He did not have to look at the numbers. He knew where he was going. He had dreamed it that afternoon, in a hot Hall full of desks, in a body that was not his.

Ninety-seven.

He turned into it.

There was nobody at the end of it. There was no man on his knees on the stone floor, bruised, trying to stand. There never had been. There was only the row, very long and very dim, and the globes, and about halfway down, on a shelf at eye level, one globe that glowed a little brighter than the others, as though it knew.

There was a yellowed label under it, in a spidery hand.

*S.P.T. to A.P.W.B.D.*
*Dark Lord*
*and (?) Harry Potter*

He had seen this before. In another world. He had stood in this row, in that Hall, and watched a man in a mask step on the globe by accident in the dark, and the pearly figure of a woman rise out of the broken glass, and speak.

He knew what it said. Every word.

"Don't touch it," said Hermione, behind him, very quietly. "Harry. It's got your name on. It's…"

"I know what it is," said Harry.

He picked it up.

It was warm. It was the only warm thing in the Hall. And as his hand closed on it, the dark on every side of the row came alive.

* * *

"Very good, Potter."

They came out of the shelves on both sides. Out of the dark between the globes, in black robes and masks, with their wands lit, and they were all round them, front and back and on both sides, a dozen at least. Harry did not have to count. He could feel them, every one, and they were pleased now. All of them. The anticipation had tipped over.

The one in front pulled his mask off.

He had long white-blond hair and a cold pale handsome face, and he was smiling. Harry had read him at the World Cup, and in a graveyard, kneeling on the grass to be forgiven.

"Now," said Lucius Malfoy. "Hand me that, like a good boy."

Harry read him.

He was pleased, on the top. Very pleased, smooth and pleased, a man at the end of a long errand. And under the pleasure, very deep, very cold, Harry read something he had read in Lucius Malfoy only once before, in a churchyard, with his forehead nearly on the grass.

It was fear.

It was fear of failing. It was not for himself. Harry went down it, carefully, with a dozen wands round him, and it was aimed at the person he would have to go back to, downstairs, at the bottom of the building, and tell.

"No," said Harry.

"Don't be a fool," said Lucius. "There are twelve of us and seven of you. Give me the prophecy, and nobody need get hurt."

"You want to know what it says."

"I want," said Lucius, "to take it to someone who wants to know what it says."

"He can't touch it," said Harry. "That's right, isn't it? He sent Bode. It wouldn't let Bode. It only lets the people it's about. So he sent me a dream to bring me here so I'd take it down for him." He looked at the globe in his hand. "Except he doesn't need it. Not tonight. He wants the prophecy. He wants me more."

Lucius's smile went very still.

Harry read him. He read the fear at the bottom of him come up a step.

"I don't know what you're…"

"He didn't tell you," said Harry. "Did he."

He read Lucius not know. He read him not have been told, and realise it, standing in row ninety-seven with his wand up. He read the realisation go into him all the way down, and the fear under it come up another step, and find nothing to hold on to.

"I know what it says," said Harry. "I've heard it. And now nobody else will."

He opened his hand.

* * *

The globe fell.

It took a very long time to fall, or seemed to. Lucius's face changed as it went. It changed from cold and still and smiling to something else, very fast, all at once, and Harry read what was under the face change with it, and it was horror.

It was the pure, plain horror of a man watching the one thing he was sent for drop out of a boy's hand onto a stone floor.

The glass broke.

A pearly figure began to rise out of the pieces, a woman, huge-eyed, with spectacles, and her mouth opened to speak.

"*Reducto*," said Harry, and pointed his wand at the shelf behind Lucius's head.

The shelf exploded.

It went up in a shower of glass and wood, and the shelf behind it went too, and the one behind that, like dominoes, all the way down the row and into the next, and a thousand prophecies came down at once onto the stone floor and broke, and a thousand pearly figures rose out of the wreckage, all speaking, all at once, in a roar of voices that filled the whole high cold Hall from floor to ceiling.

Nobody heard the one at Harry's feet.

Nobody heard any of them.

"*RUN!*" Harry shouted.

* * *

They ran.

They ran back up row ninety-seven with the shelves coming down on both sides and glass raining off the tops of them and spells going past their heads. Harry read everything at once. Neville's terror, huge, not stopping him. Millicent's fury, which had nothing frightened in it at all. Luna, calm, running with her wand out and her hair full of glass. Ron and Hermione together, terrified for each other. Evelyn's silence, beside him, very close.

A Death Eater stepped out of the dark ahead of them with his wand up. Millicent hit him with a Stunner in the middle of the chest so hard that he went backwards into a shelf and the shelf came down on him.

"Bulstrode," said Evelyn, running. "Good."

"I *know*," said Millicent.

They came out of the end of the row. Behind them the Hall was still coming down. A curse went past Harry's ear, so close he felt the heat of it. He turned round and put a wall in the air behind them, a solid black wall from floor to ceiling, in the heads of everybody chasing them, and he read the chasing stop, and stumble, and hesitate.

Two seconds.

Lucius did not stop. Lucius came through the wall as though it were not there, and Harry read him come through, and understood, too late, that Draco had not learned it by himself.

"Potter!"

And then the door at the end of the Hall burst open and there were people coming through it, and Harry read them before he saw them, all at once, a great rush of fear and fury and purpose, and one of them, at the front, was a dog at a door.

* * *

Sirius came in first.

He came in with his wand out and his hair flying and his teeth bared, and the first spell he cast took a Death Eater off his feet ten yards away. Behind him came Lupin, very white, and Tonks, with her hair a violent fighting scarlet, and Moody, wooden leg thumping, magical eye spinning in every direction at once, and Kingsley, tall and calm in his long blue robes, already casting.

The Hall turned into a battle.

Harry had been in a fight before. He had been in a churchyard. He had been on a lawn in his third year with Dementors over the lake. He had never been in this. This was forty things at once, in the dark, in the blue light of falling prophecies, and he read all of them.

He read Moody, suspicious and decent and fighting two at once. He read Kingsley, calm all the way down, as he had decided to be. He read Lupin, frightened, and frightened for Sirius more than for himself. He read Tonks, who was enjoying it, even now, even here. He read Luna stun a man twice her size and be very slightly surprised.

He read Sirius.

Sirius was laughing. He was fighting a big Death Eater by the end of row fifty-five, and he was laughing, and the laughter was real, and it was the laughter of a man who has been shut up in a house for a year and has been let out at last, and is doing the thing he is good at.

Harry read it, and something in him went very cold, because he had read it before. In another world. By an arch.

Sirius saw him. He came down row fifty-four in three strides, knocking a Death Eater sideways without looking at him, and caught Harry by the shoulder.

"You're bleeding."

"It's glass."

"You smashed it. The prophecy. Kingsley felt the whole Hall go." Sirius's eyes were very bright. "Dumbledore'll have a fit."

"Is he coming?"

"Remus sent the phoenix. Albus is coming. He's somewhere in the north. He'll come as fast as he can, and that's very fast." Sirius gripped his shoulder harder. "Harry. Listen to me. Whatever's down there, you stay behind me. You hear?"

Harry read him.

The joy at being let out. The love, as loud as the joy, the wave coming up the beach. And under both, very steady, a decision that Sirius had made in a kitchen four hours ago and was not going to unmake for anybody.

"Yes," said Harry.

It was a lie. Sirius knew it was a lie. He grinned at it anyway, and let go, and went back into the fight laughing.

"*Harry*," said Evelyn.

He turned.

She was looking past him, towards the far end of the Hall, where there was a door he had not noticed, standing open. Beyond it there was no light at all.

"They're going through," she said. "They're pulling back. All of them. That way."

He read it. She was right. The Death Eaters were giving ground, all together, the way water goes down a drain, towards the far door. Not running. Going. As though somebody had called them.

And through the door, down, a long way down, the ring of hunger on the steps had begun to hum.

The cold thing in Harry turned towards it.

"That's where he is," said Harry.

* * *

The Death Chamber was a pit.

It was a great rectangular room, dimly lit, and the floor of it sank away in tiers of stone benches, like an amphitheatre, like the courtroom with the chained chair, down and down to a raised stone dais at the bottom. And on the dais stood the arch.

It was very old. It was cracked and crumbling, and it stood on its own with no wall round it, and from it hung a tattered black curtain, like a veil, and the veil was moving very slightly, though there was no wind at all.

Harry had seen it before. He had stood beside it.

There were steps going down to it on every side. And on the bottom step, all round the dais, set out in a ring, there were five things.

A heavy gold locket on a long chain.

A ring, ugly and black-stoned.

A small golden cup with two fine handles and a badger engraved on the side.

A tarnished tiara, the one he had watched Draco take off a bust.

And something long and heavy and green, coiled on the stone at the foot of the arch, as thick round as a man's thigh, with its head raised and swaying.

And in the middle of them, in front of the arch, standing on the dais, very tall, very thin, in black robes, with a face whiter than a skull and eyes like slits of red light, Lord Voldemort was waiting.

He saw Harry come in at the top of the tiers.

He smiled.

And he opened his arms, the way a man opens his arms at a station when somebody he has been waiting for a long time steps off the train.

* * *

"Harry," said Voldemort.

His voice came up the tiers of the amphitheatre quite softly, and every word was clear.

"You came. I hoped you would. I did not know." He tilted his head. "You knew it was not Black in the Hall. I felt you know it. And you came anyway."

Harry stood at the top of the steps.

Behind him, the Order was coming through the door, and the students, and the Death Eaters were coming down the far side of the pit, spreading out, taking the benches. He could feel them all. He could feel Sirius come through the door and stop. He could feel Evelyn, beside him, very close.

He read Voldemort.

He had read him in a graveyard. He had read him through a scar, and from inside, in a room with dust sheets. He knew the shape of him. Cold, very large, very patient, and under the cold, a long way down, the thing Harry had first felt in a diary in his second year: envy. And hunger. And, at the very bottom, so small he had almost never found it, something like a child in a dark room, afraid of the dark.

And all of it in pieces.

He had never thought of it like that before. But he read it now, standing at the top of the pit with the ring of hungry things below, and he saw it. The cold was not one thing. It had seams in it. It was like a plate that has been broken and glued, very well, by somebody very careful, and you can only see where it was broken if you hold it up to the light.

He was holding it up to the light.

"You found it in my head," said Harry. "The line."

"I found a great many things in your head," said Voldemort. "You are very untidy. A shelf. A lamp. A desk of black stone in a room under a school that would not open for me." Something moved, very deep in him, at that. It was old. It had scratched letters into a door. "A small black book that told me, in very bad Latin, why I had been climbing for fifty years and never reached the top." He smiled again. "I am grateful, Harry. I mean that. You have given me something nobody else could have given me. Not Dumbledore. Not my own long study. You."

"You're going to put yourself back together."

"I am going to be whole."

"Then you'll die. You'll be able to die. Like anybody."

"Yes," said Voldemort. "Isn't it marvellous?"

"You broke your own rule. Fifty years of it."

"I made that rule. I may unmake it. That is rather the point of rules." Voldemort looked up at him, and his head went a little on one side. "You broke the prophecy. I felt you do it. Every one of my people in that Hall felt you do it. Lucius will be very unhappy." He did not seem to mind. "It does not matter. It was a story about two people. After tonight there will only be one of them who is anything at all."

"I don't know what you mean."

"Yes, you do," said Voldemort gently. "You have always wondered. Since you were very small. Why you could feel them, all of them, when nobody else could. Where it came from." He spread his long white fingers. "I wonder too. I wonder very much, Harry, what you will be when I have taken back what is mine. Perhaps nothing. Perhaps a boy who cannot hear anybody. Perhaps that is all you ever were, without me."

Harry did not answer.

He read Voldemort say it. He read that Voldemort did not know. He read that Voldemort hoped.

Beside him, Evelyn had not moved. Her hand on his arm had not moved. She was looking down into the pit with no expression at all, the way she looked at a page of Arithmancy with an error in it.

Voldemort's eyes moved to her.

He looked at her for perhaps a second, the way he would have looked at a piece of furniture that had spoken. Then he looked away.

He had not been able to read her either. Harry felt him not be able to. He felt him not mind, very much, and not remember her.

* * *

He began.

He did not say anything. He did not raise his wand. He turned to the locket on the bottom step, and held out one long white hand towards it, palm up, and called.

Harry felt the call.

It went through the whole room. It went through the stone and the air and every person in the pit. There was no sound to it, and no spell. It was a want, very old, very cold, very large, and it was a man calling to a piece of himself across fifty years, and the locket heard it.

The locket cracked.

It cracked straight across the face, with a sound like ice on a pond, and something came out of it. It was darker than smoke. It was a kind of shadow with no shape, and it rose out of the broken gold and went across the dais through the air like a thing being pulled on a string, and went into Voldemort's open hand, and was gone.

Voldemort closed his eyes.

Harry read him. He read the shadow go in. He read it find the place it had been torn from, a long time ago. He read it fit.

One seam closed.

The ring cracked. The black stone in it split down the middle. The shadow came out of it and went across the air and in. Another seam.

The cup.

The tiara. It cracked with a high thin sound, like a wine glass, and Harry felt its hunger go out, all at once, the mouth-wanting, the thing he had felt from twenty feet in a room full of hidden things. It went across the air. It went in.

The snake.

Nagini did not crack. She was not a thing that could crack. She reared up on the stone at the foot of the arch, very high, and her mouth opened, and something came out of her open mouth, a shadow like the others, and went across the air and into her master's hand, and she sank back down onto the stone, and coiled, and was a snake.

Five.

Voldemort opened his eyes.

Harry read him, and the seams were nearly gone. The plate was nearly whole. There was one crack left in it, one fine dark line all the way across, and it ran from the place in Voldemort where something had been torn on a night in October fifteen years ago, in a nursery, in Godric's Hollow, to…

To Harry.

"And now," said Voldemort softly, "the last."

He turned and looked up the tiers.

He held out his hand.

* * *

It was torn out of him.

There was no other way to say it, afterwards. He tried, later, a great many times, to say it some other way, to Evelyn, to Dumbledore, to himself in the dark. There was no other way. It was in him, the cold small thing, low down, in the place he had not known had a floor, and it heard its name called, and it went.

It did not want to go gently. It did not know how.

It went through him the way a hook goes through a fish. It went up through the floor of him and through every room in him and out, and it took things with it as it went, the way a thing torn out of the ground brings the earth up with its roots.

His scar split.

He felt it split. He felt it open, along the old line, the lightning, and he felt warm run down his face into his eyes, and he could not see. He was on his knees on the top step of the pit. He did not remember falling.

And the snake was moving.

He could hear it. Through the roaring, through the blood in his eyes, he could hear it, and he knew the sound, a long heavy body sliding over stone. It was coming up the steps. It was coming up the tiers of the amphitheatre towards the students at the top, towards Neville, who was nearest, who had his wand up and was not moving and was too frightened to move.

Harry opened his mouth.

He did not think about it. He had done it since he was nine years old, at a zoo, through a glass. He had done it in a bathroom with a sink and a chamber under a school. He had done it in a round room to a lamp that lit for one sort of person. It was the most natural thing in him. He did not even have to decide.

"*Get back, leave him, don't you dare touch*…"

He heard it happen.

He heard it in his own mouth. He heard the hiss, the soft dry sibilant sound that was his, that had always been his, start the sentence. And halfway through the word *touch* it was not a hiss.

It was English.

It was just English. A boy shouting at a snake in English, in a dark room, with blood in his eyes.

And the snake did not stop, because the snake did not understand, because nobody had told it anything at all.

Harry stopped shouting.

He knelt on the step with his hand over his face and the blood coming through his fingers, and he understood, quite clearly, all at once, the way you understand that a step you have trodden on all your life is not there.

It had never been his.

It had been the thing in him. All of it. The zoo, and the sink, and the door under the statue's feet, and the lamp. All of it had been the cold small thing that had just been torn out of him across a room. He had been carrying it since he was a year old, and talking with its tongue, and opening its doors, and he had thought it was him.

It was gone.

The Chamber under the school was closed to him. The study behind the statue's feet. The round room, the shelf, the lamp, the desk of black stone. He would never open that door again.

He did not have time to think about it.

Somebody stunned the snake. It was Millicent. It went over backwards down the tiers like a dropped rope, and lay on the stone, and did not move, and Neville was still standing.

* * *

Harry looked up.

Voldemort was standing on the dais in front of the arch with his hand closed.

He was not moving. He had his eyes shut. He was standing quite still, very tall, very thin, with his long white hand closed on nothing, and Harry read him.

He could. That was the first thing. He read him.

He knelt on the top step with blood in his eyes and the thing torn out of him and the hiss gone from his mouth, and he reached out across the pit, the way he had reached for people since before he could remember, and Voldemort was there. Readable. The same as always.

Not the same.

The plate was whole.

There were no seams. He held it up to the light and there were no seams anywhere. It was one thing, from the top to the bottom, cold, very large, very patient, and it was one piece. The envy was in it, and the hunger, and the child in the dark at the bottom, and they were all one cold. Nothing had been glued. Nothing had ever been broken. It was smaller than it had been, Harry thought. Smaller, and colder, and without a single crack in it anywhere for light to come through.

Voldemort opened his eyes.

He looked up the tiers at Harry, and Harry read what came up in him as he looked, and it was satisfaction, the plain satisfaction of a man who has finished a piece of work.

And under the satisfaction, very small, very cold, entirely new, there was something Harry had never once read in Voldemort in all the years of reading him.

Harry could not place it at first. He had read it in a great many people. He had read it in Fudge in a hospital wing, and in Uncle Vernon at a barrier, and in himself in a churchyard.

It was fear of dying.

It was very small. It was right at the bottom. It had not been there in the graveyard, or in the diary, or in any of the eleven nights he had gone down the well. It had not been able to be there, Harry thought, while there were six other places to go.

There was nowhere else to go now.

Voldemort did not seem to know it was there.

* * *

The room broke open.

The Death Eaters had been waiting. They had stood on the benches on the far side of the pit, very still, all through it, the way men stand in a church, and as the last shadow went into their master's hand they came down the tiers all at once, and the Order went to meet them, and the pit was full of light.

Harry got up.

He could not see properly. The blood was in his left eye and would not stop. He got up anyway, and somebody had his arm, and it was Evelyn, and she had not let go of him since the top of the steps.

"Can you stand?"

"Yes."

"Can you see?"

"Half."

"That'll do," said Evelyn.

He read the fight.

He read it all at once, the way he had in the Hall, forty things in the dark. Moody duelling two. Kingsley duelling one, a big man with a scarred face, and winning. Lupin, very fast and very white. Hermione hit by something purple across the chest and going down, and Ron catching her, and Ron's terror going up so high and so loud that it drowned everything else in the room for a second. Neville dragging somebody out of the way by the collar. Luna. Millicent.

A voice, behind a mask, by the dais. A man's voice, cheerful, saying *steady on* to another man beside him as they came down the steps.

He felt Evelyn go still beside him.

She did not say anything. She did not let go of his arm. But Harry had known her nearly five years, and he knew what it meant when Evelyn went still, and he knew whose voice that was, because he had heard it in a churchyard say *there we are*.

The man in the mask did not look at her. He went past, down the far tiers, ten yards away, and he did not look round once.

Nobody ever looked at Evelyn.

* * *

And by the arch, on the dais, Sirius was duelling Bellatrix Lestrange.

Harry saw them.

He saw them through the blood, half blind, across the pit, and he knew it at once, and something in him that had been holding very still since a Hall full of desks that afternoon went stiller.

He had seen this.

He had stood in another world, in this room, and watched it. A man with long black hair, laughing, duelling a woman with heavy-lidded eyes by an arch with a curtain in it. He had watched the woman's red light. He had watched where the man was standing when it hit him. One step to the left of the arch. He had watched him fall, slowly, backwards, with a look of mild surprise, through the ragged veil, and the veil sway as though in a breeze, and fall still.

He had never told anybody exactly where the man was standing.

He had not needed to. He had carried it. Every day for two years.

Sirius was laughing. He was laughing at Bellatrix, and Bellatrix was laughing back, high and wild, and they were circling each other on the stone dais in front of the arch, wands flashing, and Harry read Sirius, and it was the same laughter. It was exactly the same. The man let out of the house.

He read Bellatrix.

He did it the way he had done it on the lake rock to a woman in pink. All the way down, fast, to the place where the deciding was. He knew the shape of a decision. He had known it since his first year, in a classroom with Quirrell, a small hard bright thing like a coin being turned over.

He found it.

It was turning over.

Sirius stepped to his left.

* * *

Harry did not think.

He had no time and he did not need it. He had carried this for two years. He reached across the pit, half blind, with the blood running into his eye, and he did not reach for Sirius, because Sirius would not have understood and would not have moved.

He reached for Bellatrix.

He gave her Sirius.

He gave her Sirius exactly as he was, laughing, with his wand up and his hair flying. He gave her every detail, the stance and the robes and the light on his face. He made it as perfect as anything he had ever made.

He put it one step to the right of where Sirius really was.

It took no time. It took less time than the coin took to fall. He had done it in a third-floor classroom in another world for weeks, a face on a face, and on a dragon with fifty thousand people watching, and in a Room with a wall, and in a forest with a lamp. He had never done it on this. He had never done it with anything like this riding on it.

Bellatrix's red light went out of the end of her wand.

It went one step to the right of Sirius Black. It went through the air where she believed he was standing and hit nothing, and went on, and went through the veil of the arch, and the veil moved very slightly, as though in a breeze, and fell still.

Sirius did not fall.

Sirius stood on the dais in front of the arch, exactly where he was, alive, with his wand up, and he looked at the place where the light had gone, and then at Bellatrix, and then, for some reason, across the pit, at Harry.

Harry read him.

He read the laughter go out of him. He read what came in its place. He did not have a name for it, and he never found one.

* * *

Then Voldemort raised his wand.

He did it quite idly. He had not been watching the dais. He had been standing in the middle of the pit, below the fighting, with his new whole self, and he had been looking at his own hands, turning them over, the long white fingers, the way a man looks at a new pair of gloves.

He raised his wand to see what it would do now.

He did not look where he pointed it. He was looking at his hand. He flicked the wand once, carelessly, across the pit, at nothing in particular, the way a man swings a new cricket bat in his garden, to feel the weight.

A green light went out of the end of it.

It went across the pit, very fast, and it was not aimed at anybody, and it was not aimed at the dais, and it was not aimed at Sirius.

Tonks was beside Sirius.

She had come up the far steps of the dais to help him. Her hair was scarlet. She had just stunned a Death Eater and turned round, laughing, because she was enjoying it, even now, even here, she always did, and she had her mouth open to say something to Sirius, and she was standing just behind his left shoulder.

The green light hit her in the chest.

She fell.

She fell quite simply, backwards, onto the stone of the dais, with her arms out, and her wand rolled out of her hand and down the steps, and her hair went, as she fell, from scarlet to a dull mouse-brown, all at once, like a lamp going out.

She did not get up.

* * *

Harry read the room stop.

He read Sirius turn round. He read Lupin, on the far side of the pit, see. He read Moody. He read Kingsley. He read the students at the top of the steps, all of them, the same, all at once.

He read Tonks.

There was nothing. There was nothing at all. He had read her on a kitchen floor in Surrey, in a pile of potato peelings, enjoying herself all the way down. He had read her over the Thames, happy for twenty seconds with London under her feet. He had read her in a kitchen in Grimmauld Place being a tired sour man whose train had been cancelled.

He reached for her across the pit, and she was not there.

He did not understand it. He understood it perfectly and did not understand it. He had saved Sirius. He had moved Sirius one step, and the curse had gone through the veil, and Sirius was standing. And Tonks had been one step behind Sirius's left shoulder, laughing.

He did not know whether that mattered.

He did not know whether, if Sirius had been one step to the left, Tonks would have been one step somewhere else. He did not know whether Voldemort had been aiming at all, or whether he had looked at his own hand and flicked his wand and it had simply happened to cross the pit at that height, at that moment, through that space.

He would never know.

He knew that, kneeling on the top step. He knew it would be one of the things he carried. He did not know, yet, how long.

* * *

Voldemort lowered his wand.

He looked across the pit at what he had done. He did not seem very interested. Harry read him, and there was a small, cold, mild satisfaction in him, the satisfaction of a man who has swung a new bat and found that it is well balanced.

He turned away.

He walked to the far side of the pit, to a door in the wall, unhurried, and Pettigrew was beside him, small and fat and silver-handed, scuttling, and two Death Eaters fell in behind, and Bellatrix, still laughing, came down off the dais and went after them.

He was going up.

Harry read it. He did not have to try. Voldemort was going out of the pit and along a corridor and up, to the lifts, and he was going to the Atrium. He was going up to the top of the building, to the great gold hall with the fountain in it, and he was going there because he wanted to stand in it.

Harry was on his feet.

He did not remember getting up. Evelyn still had his arm. He was going across the top tier of the pit towards the far door, half blind, with blood in his eye, and Evelyn was going with him.

"Potter. *Potter.* Where are you…"

"He's going up," said Harry. "He's going to the Atrium."

"Your face…"

"I know."

He was at the door. He was through it. There were stairs beyond it, stone, going up into the dark, very steep, and he could hear Voldemort's footsteps a long way above, unhurried, and Pettigrew's, scuttling.

He started up them.

He was running. He did not know he could run. He went up the stairs two at a time with his hand on the wall and his eye full of blood, and Evelyn was beside him, and behind him he could hear the Order coming, Moody and Kingsley and others, and behind them, very far behind, on the dais, beside the arch, he could hear nothing at all from Sirius, and from Lupin, and from the one who did not get up.

And he noticed.

He noticed it on about the fortieth step. It came to him quite quietly, the way you notice that you have been hearing a clock tick all along.

He was reading.

He was still reading. He had felt the thing torn out of him and the hiss turn to English in his mouth, and he had knelt on the top step thinking *it had never been his*, all of it, and he had not checked.

He checked.

Behind him on the stairs, the Order. Moody, furious and decent. Kingsley, calm by decision. Ron, somewhere further back, carrying Hermione, terror and love together. Lupin, not on the stairs, still down in the pit, kneeling by the dais, and the thing in him was so large and so quiet that Harry turned away from it.

And beside him, on the stairs, keeping pace, step for step, with her hand still on his arm, a silence.

Evelyn's silence. The one he had never been able to read. He could feel the shape of it, exactly her shape, a hole in the noise of the whole building, beside him, climbing.

He was still reading everybody.

He did not stop to think about what that meant.

He ran.
