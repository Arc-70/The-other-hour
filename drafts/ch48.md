## Chapter 48: Educational Decrees

Professor Umbridge gave her first lesson to the Slytherin and Gryffindor fifth years on the Tuesday of the first week, at two o'clock, and the first thing she did was ask them to put their wands away.

"We shan't be needing those today," she said. "Or, I hope, very often at all."

She was standing at the front of the Defence classroom in a fluffy pink cardigan, with a black velvet bow on top of her head like a large fly that had landed there and decided to stay. She was smiling. She had written on the board, in a round neat hand, *Defence Against the Dark Arts: A Return to Basic Principles*, and underneath it three aims, numbered.

Harry put his wand away.

He read the room put its wands away. He read thirty people do it slowly and with suspicion, and he read the suspicion go up when they opened *Defensive Magical Theory* by Wilbert Slinkhard at chapter one and found that it was about the history of the word *defence*.

He read Professor Umbridge.

He did not have to look far. It was all in the same place it had been in Courtroom Ten, and in the Great Hall in another world over a boiled egg. She was pleased. She was quietly, steadily pleased, like a kettle a little off the boil, and she was certain the way a stone is certain. And under the certainty, warm and sweet and genuine, there was the thing he had never got used to.

She was enjoying them.

She was enjoying thirty fifteen-year-olds sitting in front of her with their wands away, reading about the history of a word. She was enjoying the suspicion. She was enjoying, very particularly, the girl in the third row on the Gryffindor side whose hand had gone up.

It was Hermione's.

"There's nothing in here about using defensive spells," said Hermione.

"Hand up, dear, if you wish to speak in my class."

"My hand *is* up."

"I can see that," said Professor Umbridge. "And now you may put it down, because I am going to answer you. You will not need to use defensive spells, Miss…?"

"Granger."

"Miss Granger. Because there is nothing, in a classroom, that you need to defend yourself against." She smiled round the room. "Who do you imagine is going to attack you, during your OWLs?"

Harry read the room.

It went quiet in a particular way. It went quiet the way a room goes quiet when everybody in it is thinking of the same person and nobody wants to say his name. Thirty of them. Every single one, at once, without looking, thinking about Harry.

Professor Umbridge felt it too. She could not read it, but she was not a fool. She turned her head and looked straight at him.

"Mr Potter," she said. "Perhaps you would like to tell the class who you imagine is going to attack them?"

Harry thought about the other Harry.

He had heard about this lesson from a boy in red and gold, in another world, in a common room, arguing over his head. He had heard what the other Harry had said. He had heard about the detentions afterwards, and the quill, and the words that went into the back of his hand. He had watched the other Harry's hand, across the Great Hall at breakfast, and seen the white letters there.

He looked at Professor Umbridge, and he gave her his best smile, the one he used on shop assistants and train guards and Madam Pince.

"Nobody, Professor," he said. "I think the exams are mostly theory. I'd rather be ready for them."

* * *

The room did not make a sound.

He read it not make a sound. He read the Gryffindor side of it go from suspicion to something cold and astonished, all at once, the whole bank of red, and he read Ron Weasley's astonishment go hard. He read Hermione not believe it. He read Neville Longbottom, at the back, go very small.

He read the Slytherin side. Theo, beside him, who did not move, and who was, under the stillness, thinking very hard. Millicent, who was angry, and the anger was aimed straight at the side of his head. Pansy Parkinson, delighted.

And Draco Malfoy, two seats along.

Draco had been sitting forward. He had been sitting forward with his hand half up, because he had been about to say something exactly like what Harry had just said, and it would have been his line, and she would have adored him for it.

Harry read him put his hand down.

It was fury. It was very hot, and very young, and very plain, and it was aimed at Harry so hard that Harry could have found Draco in the dark by it.

Professor Umbridge was smiling at him.

It was a new smile. He read what was under it, and the sweet enjoyment had gone somewhere else for a moment, and in its place, warm and pleased and a little surprised, there was something that was very nearly fondness.

"What a very sensible young man," she said. "Ten points to Slytherin."

* * *

Millicent hit him on the arm on the way out.

She did it harder than usual, and she did it without saying anything, and she went off down the corridor ahead of him with her bag banging against her legs.

Theo walked beside him.

He did not say anything until the end of the corridor, where the stairs went down. Then he said, "That was very good."

"Was it?"

"I've watched you do it to people for four years," said Theo. "I've never watched you do it to somebody you loathe. It's much more frightening." He shifted his bag on his shoulder. "You loathe her."

"Yes."

"You read her."

"Yes."

Theo nodded, as though that were the answer to a question he had not asked.

"Millicent won't speak to you for a week," he said. "And Weasley won't speak to you for longer. And Malfoy is going to do something stupid by Friday." He looked at Harry. "Do you know what you're doing?"

Harry read him. Curiosity. Bright and small and wanting nothing. And under it, steadily, for the first time since a dormitory in June, something other than the drop in the dark: trust, plain and quiet and slightly tired.

"Mostly," said Harry.

"That'll do," said Theo.

* * *

Malfoy did something stupid on the Thursday.

He did it at breakfast, at the Slytherin table, loudly, so that the Gryffindors could hear. He said that some people would lick a Ministry boot if it was pink enough. He said it to Crabbe, who laughed, a second late.

Harry did not answer.

Professor Umbridge, at the staff table, had heard. Harry read her hear. He read the warmth in her go a little cooler, very slightly, towards the Slytherin prefect who had said it, and a little warmer towards the boy who had said nothing.

On the Friday she asked Harry to tea.

* * *

Her office was the one he remembered.

It was the one from another world, and it was exactly the same, which he had known it would be and which still made him stop in the doorway for half a second. Lace on every surface. Dried flowers in vases. And on the walls, dozens and dozens of ornamental plates, each with a large coloured kitten on it, and every kitten wearing a different bow, and all of them moving, frolicking, licking their paws, peering out at him.

In another world he had sent a cat into this room that was not there. He had watched it knock the plates off the walls. He had watched her fire a curse at nothing and crack a real one.

He sat down on a chintz chair and took a cup of tea, and said it was lovely.

"Sugar, Mr Potter?"

"Two, please, Professor."

She talked. She talked for forty minutes. She talked about the Ministry, and about standards, and about the very great disappointment it had been to her to find that Hogwarts had been allowed to drift. She talked about the Minister, whom she admired very much, and who was under a great deal of strain this summer because of certain people spreading certain stories. She looked at Harry over her teacup when she said that.

Harry said that he had been under a great deal of strain too, at the end of last year. He said that he did not remember the end of the third task very clearly. He said that he was sure Professor Dumbledore meant well.

He read her the whole time.

He read her be pleased. He read her be gratified. He read her decide, somewhere around the second cup, that he was a boy who had been misled by a foolish old man and a great deal of attention, and that he could be saved. He read her want to save him. It was a sincere want. It sat right next to the enjoyment, and it was every bit as sincere, and neither of them was in the slightest bit embarrassed by the other.

He had read people be two things at once before. Sirius, in a hall, glad and furious. He had not read anybody be two things like these.

"You must come again, Mr Potter," she said, at the door. "I like to know what the students are thinking. You'll be my eyes and ears. Won't you?"

"Of course, Professor."

He went down the stairs.

He got as far as the first-floor landing before he had to stop, and put his hand on the wall, and stand there for a while.

He did not know what it was. He was not frightened of her. He was not angry. He had smiled for forty minutes and drunk two cups of tea with two sugars in each, and none of it had been difficult, and he had been very good at it.

He stood on the landing with his hand on the wall until a group of second-years came past and looked at him curiously. Then he went down to dinner.

* * *

Neville Longbottom got a detention in the third week.

He got it for saying, in the Defence lesson, in a very small voice that shook, that his gran said You-Know-Who was back. He said it to the desk. He did not even put his hand up. Professor Umbridge turned round from the board, and smiled, and said *Detention, Mr Longbottom. My office. Five o'clock. Every evening this week.*

Harry read her say it.

It was the enjoyment again. Only it was not spread out over the room any more. It had a direction. It was aimed at a round-faced boy in the back row whose parents were in St Mungo's, and it was so warm, and so sweet, and so entirely sincere, that Harry looked down at his book until he could stop reading it.

Neville came to the Great Hall at seven on the Friday with his right hand wrapped in a handkerchief.

He sat at the far end of the Gryffindor table and ate with his left. He did not say anything to anybody. Harry, across the hall, read him.

Pain. On the top. A small, steady, stinging pain, the kind that is not big enough to make anybody look up from their dinner and does not stop. Under it, shame. And under the shame, at the floor of Neville Longbottom, where Harry had read it on the train in his first year and in a greenhouse with blue tendrils in his fourth, there was the thing that was always there.

A room, and two people in it, and they did not know him.

Harry went over.

He did not usually cross to the Gryffindor table. Nobody from Slytherin did. He felt the hall notice him do it, a ripple, like a stone in a pond, and he ignored it and sat down on the bench beside Neville.

"Show me," he said.

Neville looked at him. Then, slowly, he unwrapped the handkerchief.

The words were cut into the back of his hand, red and raw, in a neat round script. *I must not tell lies.*

"She gives you a quill," said Neville. "A black one. You write lines with it. There's no ink. You don't need ink." He wrapped the handkerchief round again. "It's in your own writing. That's the thing. It's your own writing."

Harry did not say anything for a moment.

"Put essence of murtlap on it," he said. "Strained and pickled. Madam Pomfrey'll have some. Or Professor Sprout."

"How do you know that?"

Harry thought about a common room in another world. A girl with bushy hair, putting a bowl in front of a boy with his face, and the boy putting his hand in it.

"I read it somewhere," he said.

* * *

He started the ledger that night.

He had not kept one since June. The other ledger, the long one, the one he had carried in his head for four years and said out loud to Dumbledore in an office with a dog on the floor, was finished. He had closed it. Dumbledore had apologised once, and Harry had said yes to him at ten past two in the morning, and the ledger had stopped.

He started a new one.

He did not write it down. He never had. He lay in his four-poster with the curtains drawn and the lake moving green on the other side of the window, and he put the first line in.

*Second week of September. Professor Umbridge gave Neville Longbottom a detention for saying what his gran says. Every evening. The quill. His hand. She enjoyed it. I read her enjoy it.*

He lay there.

He thought about another world, and a boy with his face, and white letters on the back of a hand across a breakfast table. He thought about how the other Harry had stood up in the first lesson and said the true thing, and been punished for it, and gone on saying it.

He thought about how he had smiled, and said *nobody, Professor*, and drunk two cups of tea.

And about how Neville had said the true thing instead.

He added a second line. It was not about her.

*He said it to the desk. He said it anyway.*

* * *

Hermione Granger said it out loud in the library on the first Thursday of October, at the back table by the Restricted Section, at twenty past eight.

She did not say it to Harry. She said it to Ron, who was asleep on his Charms essay. But she said it loudly, because she was angry, and Harry was two shelves away looking for a book on Goblin rebellions that he did not need, and she knew perfectly well that he was there.

"Somebody," said Hermione, "should teach us actual defence. Properly. Not out of a book. Because she's not going to, and the OWL has a practical, and it's not *in* the book, and if You-Know-Who is…" She stopped. "Somebody should."

Ron woke up.

"Wha'?"

"Nothing."

Harry came round the end of the shelf.

He had not planned it. That was what he thought, afterwards. He had been in the library for an hour, two shelves away, not looking for a book about goblins, because he had known Hermione would be at that table at that time and he had wanted to hear what she said. He had not planned anything after that.

He sat down across from them.

Ron's face went closed. He had not spoken to Harry since the first Defence lesson. Harry read him. Anger, three weeks old and still hot, and under it, much smaller, disappointment, which was worse.

"You're right," said Harry, to Hermione. "Somebody should."

"Oh, *you* think so, do you?" said Ron. "That's funny. Because the last time anybody asked you, you said the exams were mostly theory."

"I did."

"Then why…"

"Because I wanted her to like me," said Harry. "And she does. She asked me to tea. She has me up every Friday. She calls me her eyes and ears." He looked at Ron. "She told me last week that the Ministry is drafting a decree about student groups. She was very pleased about it. She showed me the wording."

Ron's mouth was open.

Hermione was not looking at Ron. She was looking at Harry, and he read her, and the anger in her had stopped where it was, very suddenly, the way a person stops on a stair when they realise they have miscounted.

"You're spying on her," she said.

"I'm having tea with her."

"That's the same thing." Hermione's eyes had gone very bright. "That's *exactly* the same thing. You're inside. You're right inside, and she doesn't know." She stopped. "Does anybody know?"

"Theo. Evelyn. You, now." Harry looked at Ron. "And you."

Ron shut his mouth.

He read Ron for a long moment. The anger was still there. It was going down, slowly, the way a kettle goes down when you take it off the hob. And under it, where the disappointment had been, something else was coming up, something that did not like having been wrong, and liked even less that it was glad.

"You could've said," said Ron.

"Not in the Defence classroom."

"You could've said *after*."

"Yes," said Harry. "I'm sorry."

Ron looked at him. Then he looked at Hermione.

"Brilliant," he said, to nobody. "Now I've got to like him."

* * *

"It was my idea," said Hermione.

They were walking back from the library, the three of them, at ten to nine, along the third-floor corridor. Ron was ahead, yawning. Hermione was beside Harry and had been quiet for two staircases.

"Yes," said Harry.

"And you're going to run it."

"If you'll let me."

"Why should I?"

Harry thought about it. He thought about telling her that he had seen it done once, badly, by a boy with his face in a room on the seventh floor, a boy who had taught impatiently and well and could not feel a single person in front of him. He thought about telling her that he could.

"Because I'm a Slytherin," he said. "If you run it, it's a Gryffindor thing. Half of Hufflepuff will come, and some of Ravenclaw, and no Slytherins at all, and everybody who isn't in it will say it's Gryffindors playing at war. If I run it, it's nobody's thing. It's everybody's." He paused. "And she'll never look for it in me. She thinks I'm hers."

Hermione walked for a while.

"That's very cold," she said.

"Yes."

"It's also right." She did not sound pleased about it. Harry read her not be pleased about it, and under the not being pleased, small and surprised, something that was nearly respect. "I'm making a list. Of who to ask."

"Don't," said Harry. "Don't make a list."

"Why not?"

"Lists get found."

Hermione stopped walking. She looked at him with an expression he had seen before, on another face, plainer, under a lamp in a round study, when somebody had said something that she had been about to say herself.

"You sound like somebody," she said.

"Yes," said Harry. "I do."

* * *

The first meeting was at the Hog's Head, on the first Hogsmeade Saturday, the seventh of October, at noon.

Hermione had chosen it. It was off the main street, and nobody went there, and the barman did not ask questions. Harry had not argued. He had not been able to think of a reason that he could say out loud.

He walked in at five to twelve.

It was the same. It was one small dark room that smelled strongly of goats, and the windows were so thick with grime that the daylight came in brown. There were sawdust and stubs of candle on the rough wooden tables. There was a man in the corner wrapped in bandages from head to foot who was drinking something through the gap, and two hags in another corner, and nobody else.

And behind the bar, wiping a glass with a cloth that made the glass dirtier, there was a very old, tall, thin man with long grey hair and a long grey beard and bright blue eyes.

Harry stopped just inside the door.

He had last seen those eyes in another world, across this bar. He had last heard that voice say *He uses everyone he loves, and he loves you, so watch out.* He had slept fourteen hours upstairs, on a straw mattress, the first night after they lost their way, and eaten this man's bread and his hard cheese and drunk his goat's milk, and at the end the man had sent for his brother even though he hated him, because he was too old for this.

The barman looked up.

He looked at Harry for about a second, the way he would look at any boy who had come into his bar at noon on a Saturday, which was to say with irritation and no interest at all.

Harry read him.

Irritation. Under it, the old bitterness, aimed at somebody far off, that had grown on an even older grief the way moss grows on a stone. Under that, gruff and buried and very deep, the thing that had made him take two children in out of the dark in another world and give them a bed.

All of it the same. Every floor.

None of it aimed at Harry.

"Well?" said the barman.

"Butterbeer, please, Aber…"

Harry stopped.

The barman's eyes narrowed very slightly.

"…Aber… a bottle," said Harry. "A bottle of butterbeer. Please."

The barman looked at him for another second. Then he reached under the bar and put a dusty bottle on the counter and took Harry's two Sickles, and went back to his glass.

Harry took the bottle to the table in the far corner and sat down with his back to the wall.

He sat there for a long time, holding it.

He thought about a goat called Mabel. He thought about a girl the barman had once known, who could feel everybody, all the time, and who had died at fourteen, and how the barman had told him that in another world and would never tell him now. He thought about bread and hard cheese, and the warmth of a straw mattress, and *Well. That's new.*

He did not know why it should matter. The man had never known him. He had known another Harry, a little, in Gryffindor, with a face like a slapped backside, and he had known two Hufflepuffs who were not Hufflepuffs, and now he knew nobody.

At three minutes past twelve, Evelyn came in and sat down beside him without a word, and put her bag on the floor by her left foot.

She looked at him. She looked at the bar.

"He doesn't know you," she said.

"No."

"I thought he wouldn't."

"So did I."

She did not say anything else. She took a book out of her bag, *The Franchise Affair*, and opened it at a marker, and read, and Harry sat beside her and held his butterbeer, and after a while it was easier.

* * *

They came in twos and threes.

Ron and Hermione first, and Ginny, who sat down next to Evelyn and said hello to her and got a nod back. Neville, with his hand still bandaged. Theo, who sat down beside Harry on the other side from Evelyn and said nothing, and who had brought, Harry noticed, his travelling chess set. Millicent, who had spoken to him again since the second week and who sat down at the end of the bench so heavily that it tipped and everybody on it went up an inch.

Then a great many people Harry knew by face and not by name. Ernie Macmillan and Hannah Abbott from Hufflepuff. Terry Boot and Michael Corner and Anthony Goldstein from Ravenclaw. The Patil twins, one from each house. Dean Thomas and Seamus Finnigan, who had argued about coming on the way in and were still arguing. Cho Chang, with a friend. Lee Jordan. Fred and George, who said they were there for the entertainment.

And a small girl with straggly dirty-blonde hair, and a necklace made of butterbeer corks, and a copy of a magazine upside down under her arm, who sat down on Evelyn's other side without being asked and said, "You're very quiet. I like that. Most people's heads are terribly noisy."

Evelyn looked at her.

Harry read the girl. It was a very strange reading. She was curious, and calm, and quite happy, and there was something under the happiness that was old and sad and very well looked after, like a room somebody keeps exactly as it was. And all of it was perfectly still. There was nothing in her that was trying to be anything else.

"Luna Lovegood," said the girl. "Ravenclaw. Fourth year. You're Evelyn Selwyn. You read in the library on Saturday mornings at seven. I've seen you. I sit behind the Arithmancy section."

"I know," said Evelyn. "You hum."

"Do I?"

"Two bars of something. Over and over. You've been doing it since February."

Luna thought about this.

"I didn't know that," she said. "Thank you. I'll try to do the third bar."

Harry watched Evelyn's face.

It did nothing at all. It never did. But she had not opened her book again, and she had turned on the bench, very slightly, towards the girl with the corks.

* * *

At a quarter past twelve the door opened and Justin Finch-Fletchley came in.

He came in with two other Hufflepuffs, Zacharias Smith and a girl Harry did not know, and he looked round the room the way people look round a room when they have been told what they will find there and are hoping it is not true.

He saw Harry.

Harry read him.

He had read Justin a great many times. He had read him in his second year, in the corridor, when the whispering began. He had read him the summer after, when Justin came out of the hospital wing and told everybody who would listen that the Slytherin Parselmouth had done it to him, and went on telling them for two years after the truth came out, because the truth had come out quietly and the story had not.

Justin was frightened. He always was, of Harry. It was the top of him.

And under it, now, there was something that had not been there in second year. It had grown. It had been growing for three years, like something in a cellar.

It was hatred.

It was plain and cold and young, and it was aimed at Harry and at nothing else, and it was entirely sincere.

"I was told," said Justin, loudly, to the whole room, "that this was a group about defence. I was not told it was *his*."

Nobody said anything.

"I'm not learning to defend myself," said Justin, "from the person I'd need to defend myself *against*." He turned to Ernie and Hannah. "You're not seriously…"

"Justin," said Hannah. "Sit down."

"I won't. I won't sit in a room with him. I was in the hospital wing for four months. I couldn't move. I couldn't *see*. And everybody knows who…"

"It was a basilisk," said Evelyn.

She did not look up from her book. She had picked it up again at some point, and she was holding it open on her knee, and she said it to the page.

"It was a basilisk," she said, "set on the school by a memory in a diary, and the diary belonged to Lucius Malfoy, and Potter went down into the Chamber and killed it. You were Petrified on the twenty-first of November. So was I, on the first of May. Neither of us saw it. I've looked at the evidence. You've looked at a face."

Justin went red.

He looked at Evelyn. Harry read him look at her, and there was nothing in his look at all, no recognition. He did not know who she was. He had never known who she was. She had lain two beds along from him in a hospital wing for six weeks, Petrified, with her eyes open, and he did not know who she was.

"Who are *you*?" said Justin.

"Nobody," said Evelyn. She turned a page. "Sit down or go out. You're in the light."

Justin went out.

Zacharias Smith went with him. The girl Harry did not know looked back once from the door, and hesitated, and went too.

The room was very quiet.

Harry read the room. He read it be embarrassed, and a little afraid, and in some of the Hufflepuffs a small disloyal relief. He read Ernie Macmillan look at the door and then at Harry, and decide.

"Right," said Ernie. "Well. I'm staying."

* * *

They met in the Room of Requirement from the second week of October, on Tuesday and Friday nights at eight.

Harry found it for them. He did it alone, the first time, on a Monday night under the Cloak. He walked three times past the stretch of blank wall opposite the wall-hanging of a wizard trying to teach trolls to dance, and he thought about what he needed, and what he needed was a place where nobody could find them and where twenty-five people could learn to fight. The door came.

It was the room he had seen in another world. Very nearly. It was large and lit with torches, and there were cushions on the floor instead of chairs, and shelves of books along the walls, and at the far end there were dark wooden cabinets full of instruments, Sneakoscopes and Foe-Glasses and a Secrecy Sensor with a large crack in it.

There was no tall wooden screen at the back.

He had stood behind one, the last time he was in a room like this. He had stood behind it with Evelyn and watched a boy with his face teach a room full of people who trusted him, and had felt every one of them trust him, and had known that the boy could not feel it.

He did not need a screen. He was not watching.

* * *

He was good at it.

He had not known he would be. He had taught nobody anything in his life except a Hufflepuff second-year in another world how to stand when she was frightened. But on the first Tuesday he stood at the front of the Room of Requirement with twenty-three people sitting on cushions in front of him, looking up, and he read them, and he knew at once what each of them needed.

He knew because he could feel what each of them was afraid of.

Neville was afraid of his own wand. Not of the spell. Of the moment just before the spell, when everybody looked at him and waited to see him fail. So Harry did not make him go first. He paired him with Luna, who was not afraid of anything and did not look at people when they were trying, and left them alone in a corner, and went back after twenty minutes, and Neville had disarmed her four times.

Millicent was afraid of being laughed at. She had been laughed at since the Sorting. So Harry made her the target. He said, at the front, in front of everybody, *I want somebody who won't fall over when they're hit*, and looked at Millicent, and twenty-three people looked at Millicent, and nobody laughed, because what he had said was a compliment, and she stood up red to the ears and was hit with a Disarming Charm eleven times and did not fall over once.

Ron was afraid of not being as good as his brothers. Hermione was afraid of being good at the book and bad at the thing. Ginny was afraid of nothing that Harry could find, except a diary, and a dark place under the school, and she kept that so far down that he left it there. Cho Chang was afraid of something very large and very grey, and it was about Cedric, and Harry did not go near it at all. He gave her a partner who talked a great deal.

Theo was afraid of nothing in the room. Theo was afraid of a churchyard. Harry partnered Theo with himself.

They did Disarming for two weeks. Then Stunning. Then the Shield Charm, which was the one Harry cared about most, because a churchyard had taught him that the thing you need most is not to hit but to not be hit.

He read them every second. He read them get better. He read the fear in each of them change shape, a little, every time, the way a stone changes shape when water goes over it, and he taught to the shape.

He did not notice what he was feeling.

He could not read himself. He never had.

* * *

Evelyn sat on a cushion by the wall with Luna Lovegood on one side of her and a pile of books on the other, and watched.

She had been watching for three weeks. She had come to every meeting. She had done the work. She had disarmed Bulstrode once, which had surprised Bulstrode more than it surprised Evelyn, and she had stunned a dummy with a very small, very exact red light that hit it precisely in the centre of the chest, and Potter had said *good* without looking round, which was how she knew he had seen.

She had also counted.

She counted the number of times he smiled during a meeting. On the first Tuesday it was three. On the first Friday it was seven. Tonight, which was the fourth Friday, it was nineteen, and the meeting had only been going an hour.

She counted the number of times he laughed. Twice tonight. Once at Weasley, who had stunned himself by accident off a mirror on the wall. Once at nothing, as far as she could tell. He had been standing by Longbottom, watching him cast a Shield Charm that went up silver and held for six seconds, and he had laughed, short and surprised, and Longbottom had looked round, astonished, and then laughed too.

She counted the number of times he had looked at the door. On the first night it had been every four minutes. Tonight it had been twice.

She looked at him.

He was standing in the middle of the room in his shirt-sleeves with his tie undone, and his hair needed cutting, and he was explaining something to the Patil twins with his hands, and his face was doing a thing she had seen it do only a handful of times in four years.

It was doing nothing in particular. That was what she noticed. It had stopped arranging itself. It was not watching itself, or the room, or the door. It was simply his face, being used for talking, the way other people's faces were.

He was happy.

She had known him since he was eleven. She had watched him in the library and at the lake and in a round study under the school and in a room above a bar in another world, and on a flat rock in June, laughing into his knees. She had seen him pleased, and relieved, and interested, and amused. She did not think she had ever seen this.

And he did not know.

That was the second thing. He did not know. He had spent four years reading every face in every room he walked into, and he had no idea what his own was doing.

Evelyn looked at it for a while longer.

"He's very happy," said Luna, beside her.

Evelyn did not look round.

"How do you know?"

"He keeps forgetting to be careful," said Luna. "People only do that when they're happy. Or when they're very tired. And he isn't tired. He's been asleep for weeks, I think, and he's just woken up." She leaned back against the wall. "You knew already."

"Yes."

"Are you going to tell him?"

Evelyn thought about it.

She thought that if she told him, he would know. And if he knew, he would look at it. And if he looked at it, he would start to arrange it, the way he arranged everything, and it would stop being what it was.

"No," she said.

Luna nodded, as though that were what she would have said too.

Evelyn took the watch out of her cardigan pocket and looked at it. It was nineteen minutes past nine. The second hand moved round. The other hand, the fine one, did not.

She put it back.

A fourth-year with corks round her neck had seen the same thing she had, and Evelyn found that she did not object. She filed that, under the date, as an anomaly. She objected to nearly everybody.

The letter came on the last Monday of October, by school owl, at breakfast.

It was from Dumbledore. Harry knew the hand before he opened it. He had not seen Dumbledore since the courtroom except at the staff table, where Dumbledore had not looked at him once since the start of term and Harry had not looked at Dumbledore. He had read him, though. Every meal. He had read the respect from the courtroom still there, steady, under everything. And beside it, growing a little every week, something tired and careful, like a man walking along the top of a wall.

*Harry,*

*I had hoped to teach you myself this year. I find I cannot. I am watched, and so are you, and if I am seen to spend my evenings with you, it will be noticed by people whose noticing I would rather not invite. There are other reasons. I will tell you them when I can.*

*But there is one thing that will not wait.*

*The scar on your forehead is a door. You know this. I think you have known it since the summer. It is a door between your mind and his, and at present it opens in both directions, and he is, I believe, entirely unaware of it. I would like it to stay that way. I would very much like you to be able to close it.*

*I would like to ask you to take lessons in Occlumency with Professor Snape.*

*I am aware of what I am asking. I am aware of what you think of him and of what he thinks of you, and I will not pretend that either will make the lessons pleasant. But he is the finest Occlumens I know, and he has agreed, and I believe it will matter more than I can say.*

*I am asking, Harry. Not telling. If you say no, I will understand, and I will find another way.*

*A.D.*

Harry read it twice.

He read the word *asking* three times.

He looked up at the staff table. Dumbledore was eating porridge and talking to Professor Flitwick about something that made Flitwick laugh. He did not look at Harry.

Harry read him.

The respect. The tiredness. The man on the wall. And under it, very quiet, aimed at Harry, something that was waiting, the way it had waited in an office with a dog on the floor, for an answer.

Harry looked along the staff table to the end.

Snape was not eating. He was sitting with his hands folded in front of his plate and his eyes on nothing, and he was closed, the way he always was, smooth and complete. Around the rim of him there was dread, the old cold dread from the hospital wing. It had been there all term. It was there every morning. Harry thought he probably went to sleep with it.

He thought about the hurting thing under the loathing, in a Potions dungeon on a Friday in his first year. He thought about it coming up for a second in a hospital wing and being aimed past him.

He did not want to go anywhere near Severus Snape's mind.

He took out a quill and wrote on the back of the letter, in small letters: *Yes. H.*

He gave it to the owl.

The lesson was on the Wednesday, at six o'clock, in Snape's office.

Harry went down to the dungeons at five to. He went alone. Evelyn had offered to walk him to the door and he had said no, and she had said *good*, and gone to the library, which was how he knew she had wanted to.

The corridor was cold and dark and smelled of the potions stores. The door was shut.

He stood in front of it.

He could feel Snape on the other side. He could feel the closed smooth shape of him, and the dread round the rim. He could feel something else too, which was new, and which he had not felt in Snape before in four years of reading him across classrooms and corridors.

It was anticipation.

It was aimed at the door. It was aimed at the boy on the other side of the door. It was cold and very patient, and there was nothing kind in it anywhere.

Harry knocked.
