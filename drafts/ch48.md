## Chapter 48: Educational Decrees

The first Defence Against the Dark Arts lesson of the year was on a Tuesday, after lunch, with the Gryffindors, and Harry agreed with every word of it.

"Wands away," said Professor Umbridge.

She said it sweetly. She was standing at the front of the classroom in a fluffy pink cardigan over her robes, with the black velvet bow in her hair, and she had written on the board in a neat round hand: *Defence Against the Dark Arts. A Return to Basic Principles.*

Thirty wands went away.

She handed out a book. It was called *Defensive Magical Theory*, by Wilbert Slinkhard, and it was the dullest book Harry had ever opened, and he had opened Binns's set texts. She told them to read chapter one. She told them there would be no need to talk.

Hermione put up her hand.

It went up and stayed up, straight as a wand, for eleven minutes, while the rest of the class read about the importance of understanding the theoretical basis of a counter-jinx. Harry counted. At the end of the eleven minutes Professor Umbridge looked up from her desk with an air of mild surprise, as though a fly had landed on it.

"Did you want to ask something about the chapter, dear?"

"Not about the chapter, no," said Hermione. "There's nothing written here about using defensive spells."

"Using defensive spells?" Umbridge gave a little laugh. "Why, I can't imagine any situation arising in my classroom that would require you to use a defensive spell, Miss…"

"Granger."

"Miss Granger. You surely aren't expecting to be attacked during class?"

"We're not going to use magic?" said Ron loudly.

"Students raise their hands when they wish to speak in my class, Mr…"

"Weasley."

It went on. It went on for some time. It went the way Harry had known it would go, because he had seen the end of it, from the far side, in another world, where a boy in a red and gold scarf had stood up in a classroom like this and shouted that a dead boy had been murdered, and had been given a week of detentions for it, and had walked out of the room with his hand already hurting.

Harry read the room.

It was loud. The Gryffindors were angry, a hot crowded anger aimed at the front of the room. The Slytherins were bored, mostly, and some of them were pleased, and Draco Malfoy was pleased most of all. He was sitting two rows in front with his arms folded and his chin up, and he was waiting.

He was waiting for Harry.

Harry read it plainly. Draco had been waiting all summer for this. He had been waiting for Harry Potter to stand up in front of a Ministry witch and say *Voldemort is back*, and be put in his place for it, in front of everybody, and the waiting was so warm in him that it was nearly happiness.

And Umbridge was waiting too.

She was looking at Harry. She had been looking at him, on and off, since the lesson started, the way you keep looking at a step you are sure is going to creak.

"You have been told," she said, "by certain people, that a certain Dark wizard has returned from the dead."

The room went quiet.

"This is a lie," said Professor Umbridge.

And she looked at Harry.

So did everyone else.

Harry put up his hand.

He put it up quite slowly and politely, and waited, the way students were supposed to wait in her class. He read her see it go up. He read the pleasure in her, the sweet steady kind, rise up to meet it, the way it had risen in Courtroom Ten.

"Yes, Mr Potter?"

"I was in a maze, Professor," said Harry. "In June. At night. I was very frightened, and I'd been under a great many spells, and I'd seen somebody I knew die." He looked at his desk. "I know what I thought I saw. I've been told by people who know about these things that I may have been mistaken." He looked up at her. "I'd like to believe them. I think it would be better for everybody if they were right."

Nobody said anything at all.

Harry read the room again. He made himself do it. He went along it the way he had gone along the Wizengamot, and it was very different, and he made himself feel all of it.

The Gryffindors were disgusted. It was a cold clean disgust, aimed at him, and in Ron Weasley, three seats along, it was so large that there was nothing under it. Hermione was not disgusted. Hermione was something else, and he did not have time to find out what.

Theo, beside him, was very still. Theo was confused. Under the confusion, Theo had decided to wait and see.

Draco was furious.

He had turned round in his seat and he was staring at Harry, and the waiting in him had gone, all at once, the way a balloon goes. In its place there was something much hotter. It was the fury of a boy who has saved a seat all summer for the person who was supposed to be humiliated, and found that person sitting in his own.

And Umbridge was delighted.

She was more than delighted. She put her hand to the front of her pink cardigan, just under the brooch, and she looked at Harry the way a woman looks at a grandson who has remembered to write.

"That," she said softly, "is very sensible, Mr Potter. Very mature. Five points to Slytherin."

* * *

He was invited to tea on the Friday.

The invitation came on a pink card, folded once, and it said *Professor Umbridge would be so very glad*, and Harry went at five o'clock to the office that had belonged, in four years, to a man with a face on the back of his head, and a man who had written books about vampires, and a werewolf, and a Death Eater wearing the face of an Auror.

It had belonged to all of them. It did not look as though it had ever belonged to anybody but her.

Everything was pink. The walls were pink and the cushions were pink and the lace cloths on every surface were pink, and the walls were covered in plates. They were china plates, ornamental ones, and every plate had a kitten on it, and the kittens moved. They frisked and pawed and washed their faces. There were fifty-six of them. Harry counted.

"Do sit down, Mr Potter," said Professor Umbridge. "Tea?"

He drank her tea. He did not taste it first, because she would have seen him not taste it, and so he read it instead, the whole time he was lifting the cup, which was a trick of his own and was nearly as good. She had put nothing in it. She had nothing in her about the tea at all. She was simply pleased that he had come.

They talked for forty minutes.

She talked about the Ministry, and about standards, and about the importance of a well-ordered school, and about the dreadful influence of certain people on impressionable young minds. She talked about Dumbledore without once saying his name. She talked about Muggle-borns in a way that was very careful and very sweet and that Harry did not let himself react to at all. She asked him about his summer, and his relatives, and whether he found the Slytherin common room comfortable, and whether there was anybody in his house, any of his friends, any of the older students, who might be saying things that a good, sensible boy like himself would know were not true.

Harry said he was sure he would let her know.

He said a great many things. He said that he had never really liked Professor Lupin, which was a lie. He said that his marks in Defence had always been better than his friends' because he read ahead, which was true and which pleased her. He said that the Ministry had been very fair to him in August, very fair indeed, and that he thought perhaps people at Hogwarts had been a little unkind about the Minister.

He read her the whole time.

He gave her what she wanted. He did it the way he had done it in Courtroom Ten, a little at a time, watching her rise to each piece and settle and wait for the next. It was not hard. She wanted very simple things. She wanted to be agreed with, and she wanted to be admired, and she wanted to be told that she was right about the world, and she wanted, under all of it, very gently, for some of the people in that world to be made a little smaller.

He did not give her that one.

He came close. She asked him about Hermione Granger, whether Miss Granger was perhaps rather an influence in Gryffindor, and he felt what she wanted, and he could have given it to her in one sentence, and he did not.

"She reads a lot," he said. "That's all I know about her, really."

At a quarter to six Umbridge stood up and gave him a biscuit to take with him, wrapped in a pink napkin.

"I think," she said, "that you and I are going to get on very well, Mr Potter."

"I hope so, Professor."

He threw the biscuit in the lake on the way back.

* * *

He watched her give Neville a detention on the second Thursday of term.

Neville had not shouted. Neville had not said anything about Voldemort. Neville had dropped his copy of *Defensive Magical Theory* on the floor, loudly, and said "Oh, sorry, sorry," and bent to pick it up and knocked over his ink, and Umbridge had watched him do all of it with her head on one side, quite gently, and said, "Detention, I think, Mr Longbottom. My office. Five o'clock."

Harry saw him at breakfast the next morning.

He did not have to look for it. Neville came in late and sat at the end of the Gryffindor table and held his toast in his left hand, and his right hand was in his lap, under the table, and Harry read him from the other side of the hall without trying.

He was in pain, quietly. Steady and dull, and it was in the back of his right hand. And over the pain, much larger, there was shame. It was the shame of a boy who has been made to do something to himself and has done it, for two hours, without complaint, and who thinks that not complaining is the only thing about it he has done right.

Harry knew the quill.

He had never seen it. But he had read the other Harry in the other world. He had felt, on the other boy's right hand, a faint old soreness, words cut into the skin and healed over, and he had not understood it then. He understood it now.

He did not do anything.

He finished his porridge. He went to Charms. And that night, in his four-poster, with the curtains drawn, he opened his private notebook to a clean page.

He had kept a ledger for Dumbledore for four years. He had closed it on a night in June. He had thought he would not keep another.

At the top of the page he wrote: *D.U.*

Underneath he wrote: *Neville. Second Thursday. Two hours. Right hand. Did not complain.*

He looked at it for a while.

Then he closed the notebook and lay back and read the dormitory until Theo was asleep, and then for a long time after.

* * *

Hermione said it at a library table on the last Wednesday of September, at twenty past seven in the evening, in a voice she did not mean anybody but Ron to hear.

"Somebody's got to teach us," she said. "Properly. Actual defence. Not theory. Somebody who's actually done it."

Harry was at the next table.

He had chosen the next table. He had been sitting at it every evening for a week, with Theo, reading *Defensive Magical Theory* very slowly, because Hermione Granger was a person who said things out loud in libraries when she was angry enough, and she had been getting angrier every day.

"Who?" said Ron. "Lupin's gone. Moody wasn't Moody. Snape'd teach us how to curse each other."

"I don't know who. Somebody."

"I will," said Harry.

They both turned round.

Ron's disgust came up at once. It had not gone down since the first lesson. It had been there in the corridors and at meals and in the Great Hall, every time Harry passed, and it came up now so fast and so hard that Harry felt it before Ron had finished turning.

"You," said Ron. "You. The bloke who told Umbridge he *imagined* it."

"Yes."

"In front of everybody."

"Yes."

"Why should anybody learn anything off you?"

"Because I told Umbridge what she wanted to hear," said Harry, "and she believed me, and she'll never look for us." He closed his book. "And because I've done it. In a graveyard. With him standing in front of me. You haven't."

Ron opened his mouth.

He shut it again.

Hermione was looking at Harry. She had been looking at him since the first lesson, on and off, with the same thing in her that he had not had time to read in the classroom. He read it now.

It was not disgust. It had never been disgust. It was the thing that came up in her when she found a mistake in a book, the cautious, interested, very slightly delighted feeling of someone who has noticed that the story does not add up and wants to know why.

"You lied to her," said Hermione.

"Yes."

"On purpose. In front of the whole class. So she'd trust you."

"Yes."

"And you're telling us now. Here. In the library, where anybody could hear."

"Nobody can hear," said Harry. "Madam Pince is at the desk and she's thinking about her sister. The fifth-years by the window are revising. There's a Ravenclaw behind the Ancient Runes shelf who's asleep." He looked at her. "I'd know."

Hermione looked at him for a long time.

"I said I'd have to ask you to trust me," said Harry. "I'm asking."

"I know you are," said Hermione. "I'm thinking about it."

She thought about it for nine seconds. He felt her do it. He felt her go through it in order, the way she would go through an essay, point by point, and come out at the end of it with an answer she did not entirely like.

"Who else?" she said.

* * *

He built it in eleven days.

He did not do it the way Hermione would have done it. Hermione wanted a sign-up sheet and a list of aims and a name. He let her have the name, because it mattered to her, and the sign-up sheet, because it would matter later in a way she had not thought of yet, and he did the rest himself.

He went to people one at a time.

He went to Neville first, in the greenhouse, at the end of a Herbology lesson, while Neville was repotting a Mimbulus mimbletonia with his left hand. He did not say *join*. He said, "Does it still hurt?" and read Neville go very red and then very still, and then he said, "I'm going to teach some people to fight back. Properly. I'd like you to be one of them," and he read Neville's shame turn round in him, slowly, and become something with a spine in it.

He went to Millicent in the common room, and Millicent said, "Yes," before he had finished the sentence, and then, "What is it?"

He went to Theo last of the Slytherins, at night, in the dormitory, with the curtains drawn round both their beds and their heads in the gap between them.

"Your father would find out," said Harry. "If it went wrong."

"I know."

"He'd know you'd been in a room with me, learning to fight people like him."

"I know," said Theo. He was quiet for a while. "I'm going to have to decide that sooner or later. Aren't I? Which room I'm in."

Harry did not answer. He read Theo, the calm sadness, the bright care, the curiosity, and under all of them a new thing since June, a boy at the edge of a drop who had been standing there all summer and had got used to the view.

"Yes," said Theo, to the dark. "All right. Yes."

He went to the Hufflepuffs through Ernie Macmillan, who was pompous and decent and frightened, in that order, and who liked to be asked things formally. He went to the Ravenclaws through a quiet sixth-year girl in the library whose brother had been at the third task.

And he went to Evelyn.

He did not need to. She had known for a week. She had been sitting across from him at breakfast while he did all of it, eating her egg and her toast in four and her tea last, and on the evening he came to the fourth-floor classroom to tell her, she was already there, with a list.

"Twenty-six," she said. "If the Ravenclaw girl's brother comes. Twenty-five if he doesn't. I've put them in order of how likely they are to tell someone by accident. Smith's at the top."

"Zacharias Smith?"

"He talks in his sleep. I share a wall with a girl who shares a wall with him. I'd leave him out."

"He's already said yes."

"Then I'd give him something to do that keeps his mouth busy," said Evelyn. "Like an important job with no information in it."

He read her, out of habit. There was nothing. There never was. There was the silence, steady and restful as a closed door in a quiet house, and he sat down at the desk beside her and looked at her list, and found that he had been, for eleven days, more tired than he had noticed, and that he was not tired now.

"You're coming," he said. It was not a question.

"I'm in sixth year," said Evelyn. "I've got seven NEWT subjects and Professor Snape. I've no time at all." She turned a page of the list. "Of course I'm coming. Somebody's got to count."

* * *

They met for the first time in the Hog's Head, on the first Hogsmeade Saturday of October, at a quarter past eleven.

It was Harry's choice. Hermione had wanted the Three Broomsticks, because it was warm and full and nobody would notice. Harry had said that was exactly why. The Three Broomsticks was full of people from the school, and people from the school talked, and the Hog's Head was full of nobody at all.

He had been in the Hog's Head before.

He had been in it in another world, at thirteen, under a borrowed face, with a stolen watch and a girl who could not see the face he was wearing. He had sat in a dirty room upstairs that smelled of goats. He had been given soup, and a bed, and a warning about a brother, by an old man who had looked at a watch and known its maker and would not say.

He came in through the door and the smell was exactly the same.

It was one small dim room, very dirty, with a floor that looked like earth and windows so filthy that hardly any daylight got through. There were candle stubs on rough wooden tables. A man at the bar was wrapped in bandages from head to foot, with only a slit for his mouth, and was drinking something that smoked. Two hooded figures in a corner were speaking in thick Yorkshire accents. A witch with a heavy black veil sat by the fire.

And the barman came out from a back room.

He was a grumpy-looking old man with a great deal of long grey hair and a long grey beard, and he was tall and thin, and he wore glasses, and behind the glasses his eyes were a very bright, piercing blue.

Harry read him.

He knew what he would find. He found it.

Under the grumpiness, which was real and which was most of him, there was an old grief, very deep and very settled, aimed at a sister. Under that there was an anger aimed at a brother, and it had been there so long that it had worn a channel in him, the way water wears a channel in rock. And under the anger, quiet and stubborn and kept out of sight, there was a kindness that he would have denied to anybody's face.

He did not know Harry. There was nothing in him aimed at Harry at all, not recognition, not interest, only the faint ordinary dislike of a barman for a crowd of schoolchildren who were going to order butterbeer and make a noise.

"Well?" he said.

Harry looked at him.

He had a name in his mouth. It was right there. He had said it before, in another world, in a room over this bar, and been told off for it, and been given soup. He had it on his tongue, ready, and for one second it nearly came out, the way a word comes out that you have said a great many times to somebody who is not there any more.

"A butterbeer," said Harry. "Please."

"And?"

"And twenty-five more. They're coming."

The old man looked at him for a moment, the blue eyes very sharp, and Harry wondered for a second whether something had shown in his face.

It had not. The old man turned away and began to take dusty bottles down from behind the bar.

Harry stood at the bar and watched him do it. There were goats in a framed picture behind the bottles. There was a goat bleating somewhere at the back, faintly, through a wall. He thought of a man in this same apron in another world, who had looked at a watch and gone still, and said *my brother will use you, boy*, and given him soup anyway.

That man had known him.

This one did not, and never would. He would serve Harry butterbeer, and take his money, and forget his face before the door had shut behind him.

Harry paid for twenty-six butterbeers and did not leave a tip, because the other Aberforth had told him once that he hated tips, and that people who left them were trying to buy something.

* * *

They came in twos and threes.

Neville and Ginny and Luna Lovegood together. Ron and Hermione. Fred and George, who had not been asked and who came anyway and sat on the bar. Millicent, who came in alone and sat down next to Harry without saying anything and took his butterbeer. Theo. Ernie Macmillan and Hannah Abbott and Susan Bones, who looked at Harry with the same square-jawed attention as her aunt. Terry Boot and Michael Corner and the quiet Ravenclaw sixth-year, whose brother had come after all. Zacharias Smith, who came in with his arms folded and his chin up and the expression of somebody who had been dragged.

And Evelyn, last, at eleven twenty-two, with a book under her arm, who sat down at the end of the table nearest the door and did not take a butterbeer.

Twenty-six.

Harry stood up.

He read the room before he said anything. It was crowded and nervous and curious, and some of it was hostile, and most of it was waiting to be convinced. It had something of the Wizengamot in it, and a great deal more of the stands on the day of the first task, before the dragon, when nobody had decided yet what they thought of him.

Luna Lovegood was looking at the man in the bandages.

Harry read her, because she was the only one in the room he had never read before. He had seen her once, in another world, at a meeting very like this one, with a necklace of corks. She was wearing one now. He did not know what he expected.

She was calm.

Nothing like Evelyn's silence. A real calm, open, all the way through, with nothing held down at all. She was interested in the man in the bandages. She was interested in the dirty window and the goat behind the wall. She was interested, quite mildly, in Harry. And under the calm, very lightly, the way you might wear a scarf you had stopped noticing, she was lonely, and had been for a long time, and did not seem to mind it.

She saw him looking at her and smiled.

"You've got a lot of Wrackspurts round you," she said. "It's the smoke."

"Thank you," said Harry.

"It's all right. They go away if you stop thinking about them."

Harry stopped thinking about them.

"The Ministry," he said, "doesn't want us to learn to defend ourselves. Because the Ministry doesn't want anybody to think there's anything to defend ourselves against. I think there is. I've met it. So I'm going to teach anybody who wants to learn. Properly. Spells. Not theory." He looked round the table. "That's all. That's it. If you want to come, come. If you don't, finish your butterbeer and go, and don't tell anybody."

Nobody moved.

"Is it true?" said Michael Corner. "About the Patronus? That you can do a full one?"

"Yes."

"Is it true you can fight off You-Know-Who?"

"No," said Harry. "I ran. I had some luck, and I ran. That's most of what I'm going to teach you. How to stay alive long enough to run."

He read the room take that.

It changed. Not all at once. It changed the way a crowd changes, in patches, one person and then the next, the hostile ones last, and what it changed into was not admiration. It was something more useful than that. It was the feeling of people who have been waiting for somebody to stop pretending.

Hermione had a piece of parchment. She put it on the table.

"Everybody signs," she said. "So we know who's in."

They signed. All of them. Zacharias Smith signed last, slowly, as though somebody might stop him.

Evelyn signed her name very small, at the bottom, in the space nobody else had wanted.

* * *

Justin Finch-Fletchley found out on the Monday.

He found out because Ernie Macmillan told him, in the Hufflepuff common room, because Ernie was decent and frightened and liked to be asked things formally, and had thought it only right to ask. And on the Monday evening, in the entrance hall, at ten past six, with half the school on its way into dinner, Justin stopped in front of Harry.

He was taller than he had been. He still had curly hair. He did not have anything else that Harry remembered from a boy who had been down for Eton, and who had been found on his back on a third-floor floor in a snowstorm with his arms half raised and his eyes open.

"I heard," he said.

He did not say it quietly.

Harry read him. He had read him every time he passed him in a corridor for three years, and it had never changed. It was hatred. It was clean and settled and aimed at Harry, and it had been aimed at Harry since the night a snake had looked up at him in the Great Hall and waited to be told what to do. Under the hatred, there was something that had been frightened for a very long time, and had decided that being frightened of Harry was safer than being frightened of the dark.

"Justin," said Harry. "Not here."

"I'm not joining anything you run," said Justin, loudly. People were stopping. "I'm not going to stand in a room with you while you teach us all how to… what? How to talk to snakes? I lay in the hospital wing for four months because of you."

"That wasn't me."

"*Everybody* knows it was you. Everybody except the teachers." Justin looked at the people who had stopped. "He talks to them. You all saw it. He told it to come at me and it came. And now he wants a room full of us." He turned back to Harry. "I'd rather be cursed by You-Know-Who. At least I'd see it coming."

He went into the Great Hall.

The entrance hall was very quiet.

Harry stood in it, and read it, and nobody in it was hostile. That was the thing. A year ago it would have been. A year ago it would have turned on him like a tide. It was embarrassed now. It was embarrassed for Justin, mostly, and a little for Harry, and it went into dinner without looking at either of them.

Nobody followed Justin.

Harry thought about that, going into dinner, and found he could not decide whether it was good.

* * *

They met on Wednesday nights, on the seventh floor, opposite the great woven hanging of Barnabas the Barmy being clubbed by trolls in tutus, in a room that had not been there until Harry walked past the wall three times.

He had found it in another world. He had found it in this one by the same hint, from a diary he had destroyed, and he had never told anybody that.

It made itself into exactly what they needed. Cushions on the floor, and bookshelves of defensive magic, and a set of Dark Detectors on a table at the end, Sneakoscopes and Foe-Glasses and a Secrecy Sensor, which Harry turned to face the wall because they made him think of a trunk with seven locks.

He taught.

He found, on the first night, that he was good at it.

He had not expected that. He had expected to know the spells, which he did, and to be able to show them, which he could. He had not expected the rest. He had not expected that when he stood at the front of the room and twenty-five people tried a Disarming Charm at the same time, he would be able to read every one of them, all at once, and know exactly why each of them was getting it wrong.

Neville was afraid of failing in front of people. So Harry put him at the back, facing the wall, with Millicent, who did not care what anybody saw, and in twenty minutes Neville disarmed her twice.

Ginny was afraid of something being inside her again. She did not know she was. It was very old and very deep and it sat behind her wand arm like a hand on her elbow, and it made her pull every spell back an inch before it was finished, in case it was not hers. Harry did not tell her that. He told her to shout the incantation instead of saying it, so that she could hear it was her own voice, and her Stunner went across the room and knocked Zacharias Smith into the bookshelf.

"Sorry," said Ginny, who was not.

Ron was afraid of being ordinary. No sharp fear, either. A long, low, tired one, worn in, and it made him stop trying the moment anybody near him did better. So Harry paired him with Hermione, who did everything better, and told Ron to watch her wand arm and not her face. Ron, watching Hermione's wand arm, saw she was turning her wrist too early, and said so. She looked at him as though he had grown a second head.

"He's right," said Harry.

He read Ron, after that. He did not stop trying for the rest of the evening.

Hermione was afraid of being wrong. It was everywhere in her and it made her careful, and careful was slow. Harry made her duel Fred, who never stopped talking and who made her laugh, and she forgot to be careful for three seconds and disarmed him so hard his wand hit the ceiling.

Theo was afraid of his father.

Harry could not do anything about that. He did not try. He paired Theo with himself and they practised Shield Charms for an hour without speaking, the way they played chess, and at the end of the hour Theo's shield was the best in the room.

Luna was not afraid of anything that Harry could find. He looked for a long time. She cast a Disarming Charm on the third try that sent Terry Boot's wand into the fire, and said, "Oh, dear," and went and got it out with the tongs.

He read them all, every night, the whole room, at once.

He did not think about what it was like. He only did it.

* * *

Evelyn sat on a cushion by the door on the third Wednesday and counted.

She had counted the first two Wednesdays as well. Twenty-six on the first, with the brother. Twenty-five on the second, because Hannah Abbott had a cold. Twenty-six again tonight, at eight o'clock, with Hannah back and sniffing.

She counted the Disarming Charms that worked. In the first quarter of an hour, eleven out of a possible fifty. In the second quarter, twenty-three. In the third, thirty-eight, because Potter had gone round the room and said one sentence to each pair and moved on, and she had written down each sentence, and none of them were about wands.

*Look at her arm, not her face.*

*Shout it.*

*You're doing it to the wall, not to him. Do it to him.*

*Don't wait for it to be right.*

She was not doing the spells herself. She had disarmed Bulstrode twice on the first night, to be counted, and since then she had sat by the door and kept the list. Nobody had asked her to. Nobody had asked her not to.

She watched Potter.

She had watched him for four years and three months. She had watched him in a great many rooms. She had watched him in the Great Hall, reading the tables, and in Courtroom Ten, which he had described to her in a letter in a kind of code that she had understood perfectly, and in the dark under the stands at the first task, with his face white, holding a dragon's attention on the wrong end of a rock. She knew every face he had. She knew the polite one and the listening one and the one he used on teachers, and the one he used on her, which was the same as the others but with the guard taken off.

She did not know this one.

He was standing in the middle of the room with his sleeves pushed up, turning slowly, the way a person turns in a garden to look at everything at once. He had a hand out, palm down, flat, as though he were feeling for warmth over a stove. And he was talking, quickly and easily, to whoever was nearest. Not the polite voice. Another voice, lighter, with no edges on it, that she had heard perhaps three times in four years.

He was happy.

She wrote it down. She wrote it under the count for the third quarter, in pencil, very small. *8.47 p.m. P. happy.* And then, because she was accurate, she added: *Has not noticed.*

She looked at it.

It was the first time she had ever seen it. She thought about the boy in the cupboard, which he had told her about once, flatly, without any feeling at all. She thought about the boy on the rock by the lake in June, laughing until his eyes were wet, which was not the same thing. She thought about a white ward in another world.

He had not noticed. He was turning in the middle of the room with twenty-five people round him, reading all of them, and he was happier than she had ever seen him, and he did not know.

She thought about telling him.

She did not. It was not hers to tell. If she told him, he would notice, and if he noticed, he would start to watch it, and then it would be one more thing he watched.

She shut the notebook.

At the front of the room, Potter laughed at something Weasley had said, and turned round, and saw her by the door, and lifted a hand.

She lifted one back.

She did not smile. She was quite sure she did not.

* * *

Hermione's coins came on the fourth Wednesday.

She had been working on them for a fortnight. They were fake Galleons, twenty-six of them, and round the edge of each, where a real Galleon had a serial number, there were numerals that changed.

"Protean Charm," she said. "It's NEWT level. When you change the numbers on yours, everybody's changes. Date and time. So you don't have to send notes."

"You did a NEWT-level charm," said Ron. "On twenty-six coins."

"Twenty-seven. One spare."

Harry turned his over in his fingers.

He read her while he did it. She was proud, very proud, in a way she was trying to hide, and under it she was frightened that he would find something wrong with them, because she was always frightened of that. And under that, very small, she wanted him to say something. Not *well done*. She had been told *well done* her whole life. Something else.

"Can it say words?" said Harry.

Hermione blinked.

"Not just times. A word. If I needed everyone somewhere. Fast. Could it say a word?"

"It could," said Hermione slowly. "If I changed the… yes. A short one. One word. It'd take a week." She was looking at him with the book-mistake look again. "Why would you need a word?"

"I don't know yet," said Harry.

She went away and did it in five days.

* * *

Dumbledore wrote at the end of October.

It came at breakfast, on the Thursday, by Fawkes, who landed on the Slytherin table in a blaze of red and gold in front of the entire school, and stood among the toast racks while Harry took the letter from his leg, and then looked round the Great Hall with great interest and went up in flame.

Everybody stared.

Harry read the Hall stare. He read the Slytherin table, which was alarmed. He read Draco, who was furious again, the way he had been furious in the first Defence lesson, and who was also, under it, for the first time, curious. He read the staff table. Umbridge was at the end of it, with her tea. She had seen the phoenix. She had seen where it landed. And there was something in her that was not pleased at all, and that went down at once, quick and small, like a hand under a tablecloth.

He did not open the letter until he was in the dormitory.

*My dear Harry,*

*I promised in June to teach you myself. I find that I cannot, and I would rather tell you that plainly than pretend otherwise. The reasons are several. Some of them are the Ministry's, and you can guess them. One of them is mine, and I will tell you what I can.*

*The scar on your forehead is not only a scar. You have felt that it carries his feelings to you. I believe it may carry more than feelings, and in both directions. I believe it would be wise for you to learn to close it.*

*There is an art for this, which you have encountered in Miss Selwyn and in me. It is called Occlumency. I am not, at present, the right person to teach it to you, for reasons I hope to explain one day. Professor Snape is. He has agreed.*

*I am asking you, not telling you. You may say no. I would understand it if you did.*

*A.D.*

Harry read it four times.

He read the words *I hope to explain one day* and thought about a drawer at Privet Drive with a letter in it under his school socks. *You will be told why, in time.* He thought about the night in June. *I will say it once.* He thought about an old man in a chintz chair, behind him and to one side, not speaking.

He was being kept in the dark again.

He knew it. He could feel the edges of it through the paper, the place where the letter stopped short. *One of them is mine.* There was something Dumbledore was not saying, and it was about him, and it was the same thing that had been under the fear in the office in June, which Harry had noted and had not understood.

And he was being asked.

*I am asking you, not telling you.*

He had never once been asked. Not by Dumbledore. Not in four years. He had been steered, and placed, and kept, and protected, and told *be patient*. He had been waited for, once, on a night in June, for twenty minutes.

He wrote back on the bottom of the same sheet.

*Yes.*

He did not write anything else. He did not need to.

* * *

The first lesson was on the first Monday of November, at six o'clock in the evening, in Professor Snape's office in the dungeons.

Harry came down the corridor at three minutes to six. It was cold. The torches were low. He had been down this corridor a great many times in four years, for detentions and for Potions essays and once, at twelve, to tell a lie about a missing fang.

He stopped outside the door.

He read it. Out of habit. Out of four years of reading every door before he went through it.

Behind it, Snape was waiting. Harry could feel him, the way you feel a cold draught under a closed door. He was closed, as he always was. Smooth and complete and shut. But around the rim of him there was the edge that was always there, and Harry, standing in the cold corridor with his hand raised to knock, read what was on the edge tonight.

It was not dread. It was not the old hurting thing.

It was readiness.

It was the readiness of a man who has been asked to open a door, and has been told that the person on the other side can read him, and has spent a week deciding exactly how much of himself he is going to let them see.

Harry knocked.

"Enter," said Snape.
