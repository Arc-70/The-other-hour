## Chapter 69: The Majestic

Evelyn explained in the morning, as she had said she would, at eight o'clock, in the projection room, with a pot of tea she had made on a camping stove she had bought in Leeds in the summer of 1995 and never used until today.

"I found it in July nineteen ninety-five," she said. "On the eighteenth. I came up on the train to look at a bookshop on Call Lane that had a first edition of *Busman's Honeymoon* in its catalogue, and it had been sold before I got there, and I walked back to the station the long way because I was annoyed, and I came past the front."

Ron was sitting on an upturned crate with a mug in both hands. He had not slept. None of them had, except Hermione, for an hour, at about five, with her head on Ron's shoulder in the back row. Ron's eyes were red and his face was grey and he was looking at Evelyn as though she were a long way off.

"The front of what?"

"Of this. The Majestic. It has a very fine front. Nineteen twenty-two. There are elephants on it." She poured a second cup. "It was boarded up. There was a notice from the council in the window. The owners had gone into liquidation in nineteen eighty-five and it had been empty for ten years, and nobody wanted it, because it's very large and very old and the roof leaks. I stood and looked at it for eleven minutes."

"Why?" said Hermione.

Evelyn looked at her.

Harry could not read her. He never could. But he had watched her answer questions for six years, and he watched her decide whether to answer this one, and decide that she would, because it was morning and they were here and there was no longer any reason not to.

"Because I'd just been told my grandfather was alive," she said. "That summer. And who he was. And I thought that if my family were the kind of family that had that in it, then I ought to have somewhere to go that wasn't my family. Somewhere nobody knew about. Not my father. Not my brother. Not anyone." She put the teapot down. "It's called a contingency. My grandmother taught me the word when I was seven. She had several."

Ron stared at her.

"You bought a *cinema*," he said. "When you were *sixteen*. In case your family turned out to be evil."

"I didn't buy it. I rent it. From the council. Through a solicitor in Wakefield called Mr Ackroyd, who thinks I'm a Miss Gwendolyn Cuthbert of Ludlow who wants to turn it into an arts centre when she comes into her money." Evelyn drank some tea. "I sold a first edition of *Strong Poison* that my grandmother left me. That pays the rates. Mr Ackroyd sends me a letter every quarter to an address in Ludlow, a bookshop, and the woman who runs it keeps them for me and doesn't ask. I've been paying it for two years and two weeks."

"And the wards," said Harry.

It was the first thing he had said since the back row.

Evelyn looked at him over her cup.

"Elias Varga taught me," she said. "By letter. In the summer of nineteen ninety-six, after you'd met my grandfather. I asked him for the strongest ones a single person could set and keep. He sent me nine pages and a diagram, and a postscript saying he hoped I would never need them." She put her cup down. "I came up in August that year, and again at Christmas, and set them. Muggle-repelling, on the doors and the front. Anti-Apparition, except in the alley, and only for my blood or anyone holding my hand. A watching charm on the roof. And one of my grandfather's, which I won't explain, because I don't entirely understand it."

Nobody said anything.

"Nobody knows it's here," said Evelyn. "Except the four of us. And Mr Ackroyd, who thinks it's an arts centre. And the woman in the bookshop, who thinks I'm having an affair with a solicitor."

Ron put his mug down on the floor.

He looked round the projection room. It was a narrow room high up at the back of the auditorium, with two enormous old projectors standing in it like iron animals asleep, and reels of film in flat tin cans stacked against one wall, and a row of small square windows looking down into the dark. There was a camp bed in the corner, folded, still in its plastic. There was a box of tins. There was a shelf with eleven paperbacks on it, all with green spines.

"That," said Ron slowly, "is the most mental thing I've ever heard."

"Thank you."

"I didn't say it was good."

"No," said Evelyn. "But you meant it."

Ron opened his mouth, and shut it again. Then he picked up his mug, and looked into it, and said, in a much smaller voice, "It's quite good, actually."

* * *

They lived in the Majestic for eleven weeks.

They lived in it the way you live in a ship, which is to say mostly in the parts that were not meant for living in. They slept upstairs, on Evelyn's camp bed and three more that Hermione bought on the second day from a shop on Briggate, paying in Muggle money from a roll of notes she kept in the beaded bag. The bag held everything. It held clothes and books and tins and a tent they had not needed, and a kettle, and the entire portrait of Phineas Nigellus Black, which Hermione had taken off the wall at Grimmauld Place in July without asking anybody because she thought it might be useful, and which spent most of August face down under a camp bed, complaining.

They cooked on the camping stove.

That was the first argument, and the longest. Ron could not cook. Harry could cook, after a fashion, the way he had cooked for the Dursleys, which was to say breakfast for four people who did not like him, and he did not want to. Hermione could follow a recipe exactly and produce something that tasted of nothing. Evelyn ate the same thing every morning, which was a slice of toast with Marmite cut into two triangles, and the same thing every evening, which was a tin of tomato soup, and said she did not see the problem.

"The *problem*," said Ron, on the fourth night, holding up a saucepan of something grey, "is that I'm *starving*. I've been starving for four days. My mum feeds people. That's what she *does*. She'd look at this and she'd cry."

He stopped.

Nobody said anything. Ron put the saucepan down, quite carefully, on the camping stove, and went down the projection-room stairs into the dark of the auditorium, and they heard him walk all the way down the slope to the front row and sit down, and not come back for an hour.

Harry read him from the projection room.

He did not mean to. He read everyone now, all the time, from everywhere in the building. Ron was homesick. It was huge and simple and aimed at a kitchen in Devon with a clock on the wall with nine hands, and under it, very close to the top, he was frightened, all the time, that one of the nine hands had moved to a place he did not want it to be. He could not go home. Home was watched. Muriel's was watched. His family were in hiding at an aunt's who hated all of them, and he was in a dead cinema in Leeds eating grey stew.

On the fifth night Harry went to Kirkgate Market at four in the afternoon and bought sausages and potatoes and onions and a pound of butter, and came back and cooked them on the camping stove in the projection room, the way he had cooked bacon at Privet Drive when he was eight, and did not burn any of it.

Ron ate four sausages without looking up.

Then he looked up, at Harry, across the stove, and Harry did not read him, and Ron said, with his mouth full, "Where'd you learn that?"

"My aunt's."

"Did she teach you?"

"No," said Harry. "I just watched."

* * *

The newsagent on the corner of the next street opened at six.

Harry went every morning. He bought a *Yorkshire Post* and, from the third week, a copy of the *Daily Prophet* that came to him folded inside it by an arrangement with a hag who ran a stall in the market, and who had a cousin, and who did not ask questions in return for Harry not asking about the cousin. The newsagent was a short bald man called Mr Akhtar with a cricket bat behind the counter, and he looked at Harry every morning over his glasses with mild interest.

"You at the college?" he said, on the first Monday.

"Sorry?"

"The college. The university. You've got a student look about you."

Harry smiled at him. It was the smile he used on shop assistants and train guards and Madam Pince. He had been using it since he was six.

"Something like that," he said.

"What are you studying?"

"People, mostly."

Mr Akhtar laughed and gave him his change.

He asked every morning after that. *You at the college? How's the studying?* And Harry smiled and said something like that, and took his papers, and walked back round the corner to the fire door with the chain on it, and read the *Prophet* up in the box while the others slept.

It was very bad.

He read it the way Evelyn had taught him to read it, years ago: for the shape of the sentences, and for what was left out. The Minister had resigned for reasons of health. He had not died. Nobody in the *Prophet* had died. Pius Thicknesse was Minister for Magic and spoke of continuity and calm. There was to be a census of all witches and wizards, for their own protection. There was to be a Commission.

*The Muggle-born Registration Commission*, said the *Prophet* on the eleventh of August, on page two, *will be chaired by Mr Corvin Selwyn of Ludlow, a distinguished member of the Wizengamot for twenty-one years, who has long argued for a realistic approach to the question of magical heritage. Mr Selwyn's son, Mr Lucan Selwyn, has been appointed Senior Undersecretary to the Minister.*

There was a photograph.

Corvin Selwyn stood on the steps of the Ministry beside the new Minister, very still and very grey and very lean, with his hands folded in front of him, and he was not smiling. He did not need to. Beside him, half a step behind, his son was smiling with all his white teeth.

Harry looked at it for a long time.

Then he took it up to the top of the stairs, to where Evelyn was sitting on the end of her camp bed winding her watch, at seven o'clock, as she had every morning since she was eleven, and held it out without saying anything.

She finished winding the watch first. She put it back in her pocket. Then she took the paper, and read the paragraph, and looked at the photograph, and gave the paper back to him.

"*Realistic*," she said. "He'll have chosen that word himself."

That was all she said about it.

* * *

On the twenty-first of August the coin in Ron's pocket went warm at breakfast.

*SIRIUS BURIED THURS GODRIC'S HOLLOW BESIDE J & L. WATCHED. DON'T COME. BUCKBEAK WITH HAGRID. R.L.*

Ron read it out loud before he understood what it was. Then he stopped, with the coin in his hand, and looked at Harry.

Harry was eating a triangle of Evelyn's toast. She had started making four triangles, the second week, without anybody asking. He finished it. He drank some tea.

"All right," he said.

He did not go.

He did not ask to go. He did not argue about it. He had known, since the back row on the first night, that there would be a funeral and that he could not go to it, because every Death Eater in Britain would be at the edges of it, watching, waiting for exactly one person to come. He had known it and put it somewhere. He did not take it out now.

On Thursday he trained for fourteen hours.

He read them, all that day. He could not help it. Ron was grieving in the front row, quietly, with his radio in his lap, turned off. Hermione was grieving in the projection room, over a book she was not reading, with her hand flat on the page. Evelyn was in the foyer, sitting on the stairs to the circle, with *The Nine Tailors* open on her knee, and she was silent. She had been silent all day. She had been silent for six years.

He read the grief of two people he had known well for a year and one he had known for six, and it went up and down in the building like breathing, and none of it was his.

He did not cry.

He went down to the front of the auditorium, to the screen, and worked.

* * *

Evelyn kept a notebook.

She had kept notebooks since she was nine. This was the fifteenth. It was a cheap red one from the newsagent on the corner, because she had left the fourteenth at Grimmauld Place, in the room on the second floor with the ugly wardrobe, next to a hippogriff who was now with Hagrid.

She did not think about the fourteenth.

She wrote in the fifteenth every night, at eleven, upstairs, by the light of her wand, after the others were asleep or pretending. She wrote the date. She wrote what had happened that day, in the order it had happened, the way her grandmother had taught her to write things, so that they could be checked. And from the second week of August she began to write two numbers at the bottom of every page.

The first was the number of hours Potter had spent in front of the screen.

The second was the number of hours he had slept.

She did not decide to do it. She noticed on the ninth of August, at a quarter to midnight, that she had been listening to him down there for nine hours without stopping, and had not heard him come up to bed, and she wrote down *9* without thinking, and then looked at it. And after that she counted.

*Friday 15 August. Screen: 10. Sleep: 5.*

*Wednesday 20 August. Screen: 11. Sleep: 4.*

*Thursday 21 August. Screen: 14. Sleep: 2. (Funeral.)*

*Sunday 31 August. Screen: 11. Sleep: 4.*

*Monday 1 September. Screen: 12. Sleep: 4. (Term began. Snape is headmaster.)*

She did not show the notebook to anybody. She did not mention the numbers. She wrote them down every night at eleven, and underlined the second one, and closed the notebook, and put it under her pillow with the watch.

She did not mind, particularly, that he trained.

He had to. She understood that perfectly well. They were four people in a cinema, and there was a regime in London with her father at a long desk in it, and Potter was the only one of the four of them who could do anything that the regime could not do better. She had watched him in the orchard at the Burrow throw fifty bad Potters across the grass with blood coming out of his nose, and she had counted the cracks at the bottom of the hedge. Sixty-one. She did not know how many of those people would have been dead without the fifty bad Potters. She thought it was most of them.

So she did not mind that he trained.

She minded the second number.

It was a strange thing to notice. She had never minded what Potter did with his sleep before. She had never, in six years, known how much he slept, or thought about it. It was not her business. She wrote it down every night anyway, and underlined it, and lay awake for some time afterwards on her camp bed in the projection room, listening for him to come up the stairs.

He came up, most nights, at about four.

She heard him every time. She did not let him know. She lay with her face to the wall and her hand on the watch under her pillow and listened to him lie down on the camp bed by the projectors, and to his breathing slow, and she did not go to sleep herself until it had.

That was not in the notebook.

* * *
The screen was forty-one feet across and twenty high, and it had been white once.

It was grey now, the grey of a sheet left out in the rain, with a long brown stain down the left-hand side where the roof had leaked in some winter in the eighties, and a small rip near the bottom right corner. Harry had walked up to it on the second morning and put his hand flat on it and felt the cold canvas give under his palm, and turned round, and looked up the long dark slope of the auditorium at eleven hundred empty seats.

He began with a candle.

One candle, in the middle of the screen, about the size of a real one. He made it the way he had made his first coin in Slytherin's study four years ago, by pushing the idea of it out of his own head into the place where the eye meets the world. Except that there was nobody here to push it into. There was only the screen, and the dark, and the seats, and himself.

So he pushed it into himself.

He made a candle that he could see, on a screen in an empty cinema, with nobody else watching, and he held it, and it burned, and it was the first illusion he had ever made for an audience of one, and the one was the person making it. It was very strange. It was like talking to yourself in a mirror and having the mirror answer.

He held it for an hour.

Then he made a second one. Then a row. Then a room with the row of candles in it, and a table, and a window, and rain on the window. Then a street.

By the end of August he could fill the screen.

By the middle of September he did not need the screen.

He would stand in the orchestra pit with his back to the folded chairs and make the whole of the auditorium be somewhere else. Not to anybody else. To himself. He made it Diagon Alley on a Saturday morning, with the cobbles wet and the shops open and two hundred people walking up and down it, buying things, each one a little different, each one with a different face and a different walk and a different feeling coming off them, because a crowd that did not feel anything was a painting and a painting would not fool anybody. He made it the Great Hall at the Sorting. He made it a beach in Cornwall at low tide. He made it Privet Drive at four in the afternoon on a Tuesday, with Aunt Petunia's net curtains, and stood in the middle of the road and looked at number four for a long time, and then let it go.

He did not make Grimmauld Place.

He did not make the Burrow.

He trained eleven hours a day, and his nose bled most days, and he learned to stop it with a charm Hermione found in the beaded bag, and learned to work through it when the charm did not hold. He learned how long he could hold a hundred people. He learned how long he could hold a thousand. He learned that a thousand was easy if they did not have to do anything, and very hard if they did, and that the hardest thing in the world was one person walking across a room in a way that was quite ordinary and not quite the same as the last time.

He learned that Tonks had been right. The face was the least of it.

He did not think about Tonks very much. He thought about her when he was making faces, and then he stopped.

* * *

Ron came down to watch in the third week of September.

He sat in the middle of the eleventh row with his feet on the seat in front and a bag of chips from the shop on the corner, and he watched Harry work for an hour without saying anything, which for Ron was a long time. Harry was making a railway station. It was King's Cross, at eleven on a September morning, with the platforms and the trains and the pigeons, and he was trying to get the pigeons right, because the pigeons kept moving the same way, and real pigeons did not.

"Can you do anything?" said Ron.

"Not anything."

"Can you do Quidditch?"

Harry looked round at him.

Ron's face was quite serious. He had a chip halfway to his mouth. Harry read him, because he could not not, and found under the homesickness and the fear, which were always there now, a small hopeful thing, very young, the size of the boy Ron had been at eleven.

"Who's playing?" said Harry.

"The Cannons."

"Against?"

"Anyone. Puddlemere. Doesn't matter. The final. Of the League." Ron put the chip in his mouth. "They've never won it. Not since eighteen ninety-two. They come bottom nearly every year. I've supported them since I was four." He chewed. "Just once. I'd like to see them win it. Just once. Even if it's not real."

Harry turned back to the screen.

He let King's Cross go. He stood in the orchestra pit with his back to Ron and his hands at his sides and his eyes shut, and he built it.

He built a stadium. He had been in one only once, in a box with Evelyn's father, and he built that one, the great oval of it with the stands going up into the sky in tiers of gold and the pitch below, green as a billiard table, and the hoops at either end. He filled the stands with a hundred thousand people in orange, Chudley orange, a whole sea of it, waving, roaring. He put Puddlemere United in their navy and gold at one end. He put the Cannons at the other, in orange, with their black cannonballs on their chests, and gave them faces, and gave them names that Ron would know from the posters on his bedroom wall, which Harry had read off him that morning without meaning to.

Then he let them play.

He let them play for an hour and a half. He did not let the Cannons win easily. He let Puddlemere go eighty points up in the first half, and heard Ron groan behind him, and then he let the Cannons come back, slowly, goal by goal, the Keeper making saves that no Keeper could make, the Chasers threading the Quaffle through gaps that were not there, the crowd going wilder and wilder, the whole stadium shaking with it. And at a hundred and seventy to a hundred and sixty, with Puddlemere's Seeker diving, he let the Cannons' Seeker see the Snitch first, a glint of gold by the left-hand hoops, and go for it, flat out, across the whole width of the pitch, and catch it six inches from the ground, and roll, and come up with it held high.

The stadium went mad.

Harry let it. He let a hundred thousand people in orange go mad, all of them, at once, in a dead cinema in Leeds, and he heard behind him, over the roar he had made, a real noise, a single real one, which was Ron Weasley standing up on his seat in the eleventh row and shouting.

He let it go.

The stadium went out. The screen was grey again, with its stain and its small rip. The auditorium was dark and dusty and empty, and Ron was standing on his seat in the eleventh row with his fists in the air and chip papers all round his feet and tears running down his face.

He did not get down for a while.

When he did, he sat down slowly and looked at the screen, and then at Harry, standing down in the pit with blood on his upper lip.

"That," said Ron, "was the best thing I've ever seen in my life."

"Good."

"And the most worrying."

Harry wiped his lip.

"Why worrying?"

"Because I *believed* it," said Ron. "The whole time. I knew it wasn't real. I knew you were doing it. I could see you standing there. And I believed it anyway." He looked at the screen. "If you can do that to me when I know, what can you do to somebody who doesn't?"

Harry did not answer.

He read Ron, and found him delighted and frightened at once, in almost exactly equal parts, and could not tell which was on top.

* * *

The regime came to them through the papers and the radio and the coins.

The papers were the *Prophet*, every morning, folded inside the *Yorkshire Post*. The radio was Ron's, an old wooden wireless he had taken from Muriel's in the spring and carried in his pocket to the wedding because he had forgotten to take it out, and which they discovered, in the third week, could pick up a programme that came on at irregular times on irregular frequencies and had to be found with a password that changed. It was run by Lee Jordan, whom Ron recognised in four words, and the twins, and sometimes Kingsley, and once, gloriously, for six minutes, Lockhart, who said he was alive and well and in disguise, and could not say where, but that it was somewhere with a very good view.

The coins were Hermione's, from the defence group, two years ago.

There were eleven of them still out there that Hermione knew of. Neville had one. Luna had one. Ginny had one, at Hogwarts, which she was not supposed to have. Millicent had one, at home. Theo had one.

Theo's came at odd hours. The numbers round the rim changed into letters, and the letters were never words. They were squares on a chessboard. *E4. QB3. KN5.* Harry would sit with the coin in his hand and Theo's code in his head, the one they had made up over a Christmas holiday in a dormitory, and it would take him forty minutes to turn six squares into one line.

The lines were bad.

*CREATURE REGISTER NEXT.* *GOBLINS REFUSING.* *HOGWARTS: CARROWS TEACHING. CRUCIO IN CLASS.* *YOUR FACE ON THE WALL IN THE ATRIUM.*

And on the second of October, after an hour of squares: *GRANGER ON THE LIST.*

Hermione read it over Harry's shoulder.

She did not say anything. She went quite white, and then quite red, and then she sat down on the camp bed behind him and took out her wand and looked at it for a while. Harry knew what list. It had been in the *Prophet* the day before, on page six, under a headline that said *COMMISSION THANKS COMPLIANT*: a list of Muggle-born witches and wizards who had failed to present themselves to the Muggle-born Registration Commission for interview, and were asked to do so at their earliest convenience, and were in the meantime to be regarded as being in illegal possession of a wand.

There were forty-one names on it.

Hermione's was the eleventh.

"It's alphabetical," said Hermione, after a while, quite steadily. "They've put Granger after Goldstein. Anthony Goldstein. He's a half-blood. They've got that wrong."

"They'll have done it on purpose," said Evelyn, from her camp bed, without looking up from *The Nine Tailors*. "My father doesn't make mistakes like that. Somebody under him does, and he lets them, and then he can say it was somebody under him."

Hermione looked at her.

"He'll interview them himself," Evelyn went on. "He likes interviewing. He sits very still and lets them talk, and they always talk too much." She turned a page. "He did it to me at breakfast every morning for seventeen years."

Nobody said anything after that.

Harry read Hermione. She was frightened, which she had been since the Burrow, and under the fear she was angry, and under the anger, at the very bottom, very quiet, she was thinking about a cup of tea with two sugars in it, in a kitchen in Hampstead, and two people who did not know her.

He thought about it, that night, in front of the screen.

He thought about a man sitting very still at a long desk, and letting frightened people talk.

He made Courtroom Ten on the screen, the way he had seen it in a Pensieve, the tiers of benches going up into the dark and the chair with the chains in the middle, and stood and looked at it until three in the morning, and then let it go.

* * *

The doe came on the night of the fourteenth of October.

It was eleven o'clock. Harry was in the orchestra pit, making rain. He had been making rain for two hours, because rain was very difficult, every drop different and all of them falling, and he was not getting it right. Ron was asleep in the back row with the radio on his chest. Hermione was up in the box. Evelyn was sitting in the front row, which she sometimes did late at night now, without explanation, with a book she was not reading open on her knee.

The doe came through the wall.

It came through the left-hand wall of the auditorium, by the side boxes with the gold rails, quite slowly, as though it had all the time in the world. It was silver. It was very bright in the dark. It walked down the side aisle between the seats and the wall, past the boxes, past the torn curtains, past the orchestra pit, and stopped in front of the screen, and turned its head, and looked at Harry.

Harry let the rain go.

He stood in the orchestra pit with his wand down and looked at it.

He knew what it was. It was a Patronus. He had made enough of them himself to know what one looked like. But he had never stood in front of somebody else's and read it, and he did not decide to now. He did it the way he did everything. He reached out to the silver thing standing in front of the screen.

A Patronus was made of feeling. He had known that since he was thirteen, standing on the shore of the lake with a hundred and fifty Dementors coming over the water. You did not make a Patronus out of a memory. You made it out of what the memory made you feel, made larger, and sent out. That was all it was. A feeling, sent.

He could read it.

He read it, standing below the screen of the Majestic with Evelyn Selwyn three yards away in the front row and her book shut on her knee.

It was love.

It was one love, and only one, and it was very old, and very pure, and it had been kept for a very long time in a very small closed place where nothing else was allowed to touch it. It was so clear it was almost painful to read. It was aimed at a girl with red hair, who had been dead for sixteen years, and it had not changed in all that time. Not grown. Not faded. Not become anything else. It had been kept exactly as it was on the day it was made, like a flower pressed in a book.

Harry had read it once before.

He had read it in a room in the dungeons, two years ago, inside another man's head, in a memory of a playground and a boy in clothes too big for him watching a girl swing higher and higher, and let go, and fly. He had read it, and been thrown out of the man's head and out of the room for it, and the man had never forgiven him.

He knew whose it was.

The doe opened its mouth.

It did not speak in a voice he knew. It spoke in a voice with no colour in it at all, low and flat, a voice that had been made not to be recognised. But the feeling under the voice did not change. Harry could have known it anywhere.

"Do not go to the library on Saturday," said the doe. "Either of them. The Central or the Brotherton. There will be eight of them. They have been told a Muggle-born girl reads there on Saturdays. They do not know which girl. They will take any of them."

Then it stood, and looked at him.

Harry did not move.

He thought about Hermione in the projection room. Hermione had been to the Central Library on Calverley Street four Saturdays in a row, in a hat and a raincoat, to read old newspapers on microfilm, looking for any report of anything that might be a Death Eater in a Muggle town. She had been planning to go this Saturday. She had said so at supper.

He thought about a courtyard at the foot of a tower, four months ago, and a man standing in it alone, thinking himself unseen, looking up at a parapet, unable to close.

He thought about a wand held out across a tower roof without a word.

"Thank you," said Harry, to the doe.

He did not know whether it could hear him. He did not know whether the man at the other end of it was listening. He said it anyway, quite quietly, in the dark.

The doe looked at him a moment longer. Then it turned, and walked into the screen.

It did not go through it. It went into it, the way a figure goes into a painting, and stood there, silver on the grey, forty feet across, with the brown stain behind it and the small rip by its feet, and for a few seconds it was the only thing in the whole dark cinema that was bright.

Then it began to fade.

Behind Harry, in the front row, Evelyn said nothing at all.

He did not turn round. He did not need to read her to know what the nothing was. She had seen a Patronus come through a wall from a man nobody trusted and tell Potter where not to go, and she had seen Potter thank it, and she did not trust anybody, and she had not said so.

He trusted it.

He stood in the orchestra pit and watched the doe go out on the screen like the last light going out of an evening, slowly, from the edges in, until there was only the grey, and the stain, and the rip, and he trusted it completely.
