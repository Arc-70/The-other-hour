## Chapter 51: The Diadem

The *Prophet* came at breakfast on the fifteenth of January, and the owl that brought it to the Slytherin table landed in the marmalade.

Nobody laughed.

There were ten faces on the front page. They were the sort of photographs the Ministry takes of people it does not expect to see again, black and white, with numbers held up under the chin, and the faces in them were moving, slowly, the way faces move in old photographs. Some of them sneered. Some of them stared. One of them, a woman with heavy-lidded eyes and long thick black hair, was smiling, and she kept smiling, over and over, the same small smile, as though she were remembering a joke.

MASS BREAKOUT FROM AZKABAN, said the headline.

Underneath, smaller: MINISTRY NAMES SIRIUS BLACK AS SUSPECTED RINGLEADER.

Harry read the room.

He did it without meaning to. He could not have stopped it. The Great Hall at breakfast was always loud, a roar he turned down without thinking, and that morning it went up and up, table by table, as the papers were passed along, the way the sound of rain goes up when it comes in across a field.

It was fear.

It was fear in the Hufflepuffs, plain and frightened and young. It was fear in the Ravenclaws, mixed with something that was trying very hard to be interested. It was fear in the Gryffindors, and anger with it, hot, aimed at the staff table, at the Ministry, at a woman in pink. It was fear in the teachers. Harry read McGonagall, and it was fear held so hard that it had turned to iron, and he read Hagrid, back since November with his face cut and bruised from wherever Dumbledore had sent him, and Hagrid's fear was enormous and was all for other people.

And it was fear at the Slytherin table.

But not only.

He read along his own table and it was there, the thing he had first found in the common room in June. Small and secret and trying not to be there. Gladness. In the sixth-year by the fire. In two girls who had been on the sofa. In a great many places, faint.

He read Draco Malfoy.

Draco had the paper flat on the table in front of him and was looking at the woman with the heavy-lidded eyes. Harry read him, and he was frightened, a little. He was always frightened, a little. But the fear had gone very small, and on top of it, swelling, filling him up to the brim, there was pride.

It was the pride of a boy whose family has just been given something back. It was aimed at the photograph.

"My aunt," said Draco, to Crabbe, quite quietly, not to the table.

Harry looked down at his own paper.

The woman with the black hair was called Bellatrix Lestrange. He had heard her laugh, in another world, by an arch with a curtain in it. He had watched her raise her wand.

He folded the paper twice and put it under his plate.

* * *

"You knew," said Evelyn.

They were in the library at four, at the back table. She had not said anything at breakfast, or at lunch. She had waited until the table was empty and Madam Pince had gone to deal with a fourth-year who was eating behind the History of Magic shelves.

"I knew something was planned for the prison," said Harry. "After Christmas. I didn't know when."

"How?"

He looked at her.

He had not told her about the well. He had not told her about the eleven nights, or the room with the dust sheets, or Avery on the hearthrug, or the notes in the empty classroom. He had told her about the snake, because everybody knew about the snake. He had told her nothing else.

She was looking at him with no expression at all. She never had one. But she had asked *how*, and she had asked it in a particular voice, and he had known her four and a half years.

"I've been going in," he said.

Evelyn did not move.

"Into him. Through the scar. On purpose. Since November." He kept his voice very low. "I learned things. Names. Podmore. The prison. I told Dumbledore some of it."

"Some of it."

"Most."

"Did you tell him about the prison?"

"Yes. A note. *Azkaban. After Christmas.* He took it to Fudge." Harry looked at the table. "Fudge laughed. Dumbledore told me. He said Fudge said that Azkaban had never been broken in three hundred years and that he was not going to double the guard on the strength of a schoolboy's nightmare."

Evelyn was silent for some time.

"And Dumbledore asked you to close it," she said.

"Yes."

"And you didn't."

"No."

"And you didn't tell me."

"No," said Harry. "Because I knew what you'd say."

"What would I say?"

He thought about it. He thought about lying. He did not lie to her, ever, not since a locked classroom in March. But he thought about it, and she watched him think about it, and that was its own kind of answer.

"That it goes both ways," he said. "A door."

Evelyn picked up her quill. She dipped it. She wrote something at the bottom of her Arithmancy, very small, and he did not see what.

"Yes," she said. "That's what I'd say."

She did not say anything else about it. She did not look at him for the rest of the afternoon. At six she packed her bag and stood up, and at the end of the table she stopped.

"Is it closed now?"

"I haven't been in since before Christmas," said Harry. "Not on purpose."

She waited.

"No," said Harry. "It isn't closed."

Evelyn looked at him for a long moment. Then she nodded once, as though he had told her the time, and went.

* * *

Professor Umbridge asked him on the Friday.

It was in the pink office, at a quarter past four, over the second cup. She had been very quiet for most of the first. The breakout had not been good for her. Harry read her, and the certainty was still there, the stone, but there were small cracks in the top of it now, hairline, the way a cup cracks before it breaks. She did not like the faces on the front of the *Prophet*. They did not fit.

"I have heard," she said, "a rumour."

"Professor?"

"A rumour that there is a group." She put her cup down very precisely in its saucer. "A student group. Meeting in secret. In direct contravention of Educational Decree Number Twenty-Four." She smiled at him. "I wondered whether my eyes and ears had heard anything."

Harry read her.

She did not suspect him. That was the first thing. He went all the way down her to check, and there was nothing aimed at him but the warm, patient fondness that had been there since September, and a hope, sincere and slightly anxious, that he would help her.

He had heard about this, too, in another world. In a common room. A girl from Ravenclaw who had told, and a word on her face afterwards in spots.

"I've heard something," he said.

Professor Umbridge leaned forward.

"Not much," said Harry. "A few Hufflepuffs. Some fourth-years, I think. There's talk in the library about a revision club for the OWL practicals. They meet in the old Charms classroom on the third floor, the one that floods. On Thursdays." He frowned, as though he were trying to remember. "I don't think they're doing anything dangerous, Professor. I think they're just frightened about the exams."

It was a very good lie.

He had built it on the way up the stairs. He had built it out of real things: a classroom that really did flood, on a floor where nobody went. A night when nobody met. A group of real fourth-years whose real fear about the practicals he had read in the library on Tuesday. He had made it small, so that she would believe it, and harmless, so that she would not rush, and specific, so that she would think she had caught something.

He read her believe it.

He read it go into her all the way down, and fit, and settle. He read her be grateful. Sincerely, warmly grateful. He read her be, under the gratitude, very slightly disappointed that the group sounded so harmless, and he read her decide that she would go and look anyway, on Thursday, herself, with a few trusted students.

"Thank you, Mr Potter," she said. "You have been very helpful."

"I hope so, Professor."

She gave him a third cup of tea.

He drank it. Two sugars.

* * *

Draco was on the stairs when he came down.

He was leaning on the banister at the first-floor landing with his prefect's badge on, as though he had been waiting for somebody, and he had. He straightened up when Harry came round the corner.

"Tea," said Draco. "Again."

"She asked me."

"She always asks you." Draco looked at him. "What did she want?"

"To know about a revision club in the old Charms room on the third floor."

Draco did not say anything.

Harry read him.

The fury was there, as it had been since September. Hot and young and plain. But it had changed. It had been changing for weeks, slowly, and Harry had noticed it the way you notice a clock in another room, and had not looked properly. He looked now.

Under the fury, Draco was thinking.

He was thinking about Harry. He had been thinking about Harry for a long time, a great deal longer than Harry had been noticing. He was thinking about a boy who went to tea every Friday and came back and nothing ever happened to anybody. About a boy who said *nobody, Professor* in a Defence lesson and then crossed the hall to sit beside Longbottom. About a boy who was out of the common room on Tuesday and Friday nights, every Tuesday and every Friday, from a quarter to eight until ten.

Draco did not know anything.

But he had started to put things side by side.

"There's no revision club in the Charms room," said Draco. "Nobody uses the third floor. It floods."

"That's what I told her."

Draco looked at him a moment longer. Then he pushed himself off the banister and went down the stairs ahead of Harry, and at the bottom he turned round.

"You do it to her too," he said. "Don't you."

"Do what?"

"Whatever it is you do."

He went off towards the dungeons. Harry stood on the stairs and read him go, and the thinking was still there under the fury, and it had not stopped.

He did not like that. He noted it.

He did not, then, do anything about it.

* * *

Professor Umbridge raided the third-floor Charms classroom on the Thursday at eight with Filch and four Slytherin prefects, including Draco, and found two inches of water on the floor and a family of grindylows that Hagrid had been keeping in the old basin and had forgotten about.

She thanked Harry on the Friday for his help. She said that she had clearly been misinformed about the night, and that she would be looking again.

On the Tuesday, from a quarter to eight until ten, the group met in the Room of Requirement as usual, and Neville's Shield Charm held for forty-four seconds.

* * *

Draco was given an errand at the end of January.

Harry read it on him at breakfast on the twenty-ninth. He did not know what it was. But it was there, all at once, on a Monday morning, after a weekend in which Draco had had a letter by eagle owl and had taken it up to the dormitory to read alone.

Draco came down to breakfast and he was frightened.

He was always frightened a little. This was more. It sat on the top of him, high and tight, like a hat that does not fit. And right beside it, holding it up, there was pride, very large, very new. And under them both, filling the whole floor of him, there was something Harry had read in Draco only once before, on a night in June, in the common room by the window.

Importance.

Somebody had asked Draco Malfoy to do something. Somebody whose asking mattered more than anybody's had ever mattered. And Draco was afraid he would not be good enough, and proud that he had been asked, and under both of those, so far down that Draco himself did not seem to know it was there, he was very, very frightened of what would happen if he failed.

Harry read it every day for a week.

It did not go away. It went up and down. It went up on the Wednesday after another letter. It went up on the Friday, sharply, when Draco came back from somewhere after dinner and sat down by the fire with his hands between his knees, and stared at the coals, and did not talk to Crabbe or Goyle or Pansy Parkinson for the whole evening.

On the Saturday Harry told Evelyn.

"He's been given something to do," he said. "By his father. Or by somebody his father answers to. He's terrified and he's never been so pleased with himself."

"What sort of thing?"

"I don't know."

"Where does he go?"

Harry had been watching that too. He had watched it on the Map, in the evenings, in the dormitory, with the curtains drawn. He had watched a small dot labelled *Draco Malfoy* go out of the common room after dinner on three nights out of the last five, up through the castle, all the way to the seventh floor, and walk up and down a stretch of corridor three times, and vanish.

"The seventh floor," he said. "Opposite the trolls."

Evelyn looked at him.

"The Room," she said.

"Not ours. He's not getting into ours. He doesn't know what we ask for." Harry turned the Map over in his hands. "He's asking it for something else."

* * *

They followed him on the Tuesday, the sixth of February.

It was not a group night. Harry had moved the group to Wednesday that week, with a word to Hermione and a word to Ernie and a word to Luna, who said *oh good, Wednesdays are better for my nargle traps*, and Harry did not ask. He and Evelyn waited under the Cloak at the corner of the seventh-floor corridor, by a suit of armour that hummed to itself when nobody was near.

It was very dark. The torches on the seventh floor burned low after nine.

Draco came at twenty past.

He came alone, quickly, looking over his shoulder. Harry read him from forty yards. The errand-fear was very high tonight, higher than it had been all week, and the importance under it was high too, the two of them pushing against each other. Draco walked past the blank stretch of wall opposite the wall-hanging of the wizard and the trolls. Once. Twice. Three times. His lips were moving.

A door came.

This door was larger than the one Harry knew, and older, a heavy wooden door with iron studs, and it came out of the wall slowly, as though reluctant, and Draco opened it and went in, and did not quite close it behind him, because he did not know that it mattered.

Harry and Evelyn went through the gap.

* * *

It was a city.

That was the first thing Harry thought, standing just inside the door under the Cloak with Evelyn's shoulder against his. It was a city made of things. The room was the size of a cathedral, and high windows let in thin shafts of moonlight, and in the moonlight there were streets and alleys and towers, and the towers were made of everything that had ever been hidden at Hogwarts.

Books. Thousands of books, banned and defaced and stolen. Broken furniture, piled to the roof. Winged catapults. Fanged Frisbees, some still twitching. Bottles of congealed potion. Hats. Jewels. Cloaks. A stuffed troll. A sword with blood on it. The skeleton of something with far too many legs. Cracked cauldrons, rusted cages, old trunks, broomsticks, rolled-up rugs, rows and rows of empty bottles with handwritten labels, a mountain of what looked like a thousand years of confiscated Dungbombs.

Harry read it, out of habit, the way he read every room.

There was nothing in it. There never was, in things. It was the quietest room he had ever been in, because it was so full and none of it was alive.

Evelyn's lips were moving. He knew, without asking, that she was counting something, and that she had given up.

Draco was ahead of them, walking fast down the widest of the alleys between the towers, with his wand lit. They followed.

He went a long way in. He turned left at a tower of broken chairs and right at a stuffed troll. He went past a great tall black cabinet with a door that stood very slightly open, and he stopped, and looked at it, for perhaps four seconds, curiously, with his head on one side, the way you look at something you have heard about.

Then he went on.

Harry noted the cabinet. He did not know why. It was tall and black and plain, and it looked like a wardrobe. Draco had looked at it as though it were the answer to a question he had not asked yet.

Harry put it away and followed.

* * *

Draco stopped at a bust.

It was a pockmarked stone bust of an ugly old warlock, standing on a crate in a little clearing between a tower of books and a tower of broken desks, and somebody, a long time ago, had put a dusty old wig on its head and a tarnished tiara on top of the wig.

Draco looked at the tiara.

Harry read him. The errand-fear went up so high and so fast that it was nearly a sound. The importance went up under it. This was it, then. Whatever Draco had been sent to find, it was this. He had been coming up here for a week and walking past it and not knowing, and somebody had written to him on the Friday and told him what to look for.

He reached out and took the tiara off the wig.

And Harry felt it.

He felt it from twenty feet, under the Cloak, in a room full of a thousand years of dead things. He felt it the way he had felt only one thing before, in his second year, in his own trunk, in his own hands, every night for weeks: a small black book with a name on the back.

It was hungry.

There was nothing else in it. No fear. No pleasure. No thought. It was an object, and objects had nothing in them, ever, and this one had one thing, and it was a hunger that did not want food, or warmth, or company. It wanted the boy who was holding it. It wanted anybody. It wanted the way a mouth wants.

Harry knew it.

He knew it the way he would have known the diary. They were the same. Not the same hunger. The same *maker*. The same cold small careful hand, a long time ago, putting something into a thing that should not have had anything in it.

And then something in Harry answered.

* * *

He did not do it. That was what he told himself afterwards, over and over. He did not reach for it. He did not want it. He did not think about it at all.

It was just there.

It was very small. It was cold. It was somewhere inside him, low down, further down than he had ever looked, a place he had not known had a floor. And it moved.

It moved towards the tiara.

It moved the way a compass needle moves when you bring a magnet near, all at once, without any choice in it, the whole of it swinging round to point. It pointed at the thing in Draco's hand. It strained towards it. It *wanted* it, with exactly the same wanting, the mouth-wanting, as though the thing in Draco's hand were calling and something inside Harry had heard its name.

He had felt it before.

He had felt it in another world, in a mirror, in a boy with his face. He had felt a smaller sliver than that in himself, on a Thursday night in his third year, and called it a stain, and torn up a list in eight pieces, and said *it's his, it's not mine*.

It had been small then. Faint. A cold point.

It was not faint now.

Harry stood under the Cloak with his hand on Evelyn's arm and did not move. He could not. He had never in his life been so frightened of anything as he was of the small cold thing inside himself that had just turned round to face a tiara.

Draco put the tiara inside his robes.

He turned. He came back down the alley, fast, past the bust, past the tower of desks, straight towards them. Harry did not move. He knew he should. He knew he should do something. He had a wand in his hand and an illusion half made in his head and Draco Malfoy was eight feet away and alone and carrying something that should not be carried by anybody, and Harry had stopped him before and could stop him now.

He stood there.

The cold thing in him was still pointing. It turned as Draco passed, the way a needle turns, following.

Draco went by them so close that his robes brushed the hem of the Cloak.

Then he was gone, round the stuffed troll, towards the door, and Harry heard the door, very faint, a long way back, open and close.

* * *

"Potter."

Evelyn's hand was on his wrist. It was very cold. Or his was.

"Potter. He's gone. He went past us. You let him go past us."

Harry did not answer.

He was reading the room again. There was nothing in it. There was nothing in the bust, or the wig, or the tower of desks. Whatever had been calling had gone out of the door in Draco's robes, and the cold thing in him had turned to follow it as far as it could and then, slowly, the way a needle settles when the magnet is taken away, had gone still.

It was still there. He could feel it. It had stopped pointing.

It was waiting.

"Potter," said Evelyn. "Look at me."

He looked at her. In the moonlight from the high windows, under the Cloak, her face was very close and told him nothing, and he had never been so glad of anything.

"Something in me," he said. "It went towards it. The tiara. It wanted it. The same way the tiara wanted… It was the same, Evelyn. It was the same as the diary. And something in me wanted it back."

She did not say anything.

She did not say *don't be silly*, or *you imagined it*, or *it's all right*. She looked at him, with nothing in her face, and she kept her hand on his wrist.

"We're going to Dumbledore," she said.

"It's half past nine."

"I know what time it is."

* * *

By the time they got to the corridor, Draco had gone. The Map showed him in the Owlery.

The next morning an eagle owl left the Owlery at six with a long flat parcel tied to its leg. Evelyn saw it go. She was there. She had been there since a quarter to six, in her coat, and she had counted every owl that left, and she said afterwards that it had been the ninth.

But that was the next morning.

That night, at a quarter to ten, Harry stood in front of the gargoyle on the second floor and said *Fizzing Whizzbee*, and it was still the password, and it moved.

He went up alone. Evelyn had stopped at the bottom of the stairs.

"I'll wait," she said. "Tell him everything."

"Everything?"

"Everything you've told me," said Evelyn. "And everything you haven't."

* * *

He told him everything.

He told it standing up, in the middle of the office, with Fawkes on his perch and the silver instruments puffing and whirring on their spindly tables, and Dumbledore behind the desk in his midnight-blue robes with his hands folded. He did not sit down. Dumbledore did not ask him to.

He told him about the diary. Not the version he had told in the hospital wing in his second year. The real one. The weeks of talking to it. Tom, sixteen, charming, patient, lonely. The way the diary had felt, in his hands, every night, the only object he had ever been able to read: hungry.

He told him about the diadem. The Room of Hidden Things. Draco. The hunger, the same hunger, the same maker.

He told him about the thing in himself that had answered.

He told him about the line in Slytherin's black book, on the last pages, in the larger hand and the bad Latin. *The roads are open to many, but no divided thing may stand upon a summit.* He told him that he and Evelyn had never understood it. He told him that a divided thing could not stand on a summit, and that he thought now that he knew what a divided thing was.

And then he told him about the fourteenth of December.

He told him about the well, and the room with the dust sheets, and the thinking that stopped. The attention, turning round on itself. Something riding up the well with him. A shelf. A lamp. A black desk. A small black book open at its last pages.

"I told myself I imagined it," said Harry. "I don't think I did."

Dumbledore had not moved.

He had not moved since Harry began. He had sat behind the desk with his hands folded and his blue eyes on Harry's face, and he had not said a word, and he had not closed. Harry had read him the whole time. He had read him get older.

That was how it felt. There was no other way to say it. Harry had read a great many people take in bad news, and he had never read it do this. It went into Dumbledore and it did not stop anywhere. It went all the way down, through every floor of him, and every floor it went through, something in him was a little more tired than it had been.

"Sit down, Harry," said Dumbledore.

Harry sat down.

* * *

"Horcruxes," said Dumbledore.

He said it plainly. He did not lower his voice. He did not look away. He said it the way McGonagall said *Transfiguration*, as the name of a thing.

"A Horcrux is an object in which a wizard has hidden a piece of his own soul. It is the darkest magic there is. To make one, you must first tear the soul, and the only way to tear a soul is to commit the supreme act of evil. To murder. The piece that is torn away is then put into an object, and kept, and so long as the object survives, the wizard cannot wholly die. His body may be destroyed. He will go on." He paused. "You heard him boast of it, in the churchyard. That nobody had ever gone so far down that road as he had."

"The graveyard," said Harry.

"The graveyard."

"The diary was one."

"The diary was one," said Dumbledore. "I suspected it in your second year, when you brought it to me with a hole through its heart. I have spent three years trying to find out how many more there are. I believe he made six. The diary. A ring that belonged to his mother's family. A locket that belonged to Salazar Slytherin, which I have been looking for for some years without success. A cup that belonged to Helga Hufflepuff. And, I thought, very possibly, something of Rowena Ravenclaw's. Her diadem was lost centuries ago." He looked at Harry. "Tonight you have told me where it was. And where it has gone."

"And the sixth?"

Dumbledore did not answer at once.

"I believe," he said, "that he keeps a snake."

Harry thought about a long cool body on a stone floor. The taste of the air. Wanting a sleeping man the way he had never wanted anything.

He did not say anything.

"For fifty years," said Dumbledore, "he has kept them hidden, and separate, and safe. That is the whole point of them. He made them to be scattered. A man who has hidden his soul in six places cannot be killed by a man who finds one." He was quiet for a moment. "And now Draco Malfoy has been sent to fetch one from under our noses, and give it to his father, and his father will give it to him."

"He's gathering them," said Harry.

"He is gathering them."

"Why?"

Dumbledore looked at him.

Harry read him. He read the question go into him, and he read Dumbledore know the answer, and he read him, very carefully, decide how to say it.

Harry said it first.

"Because he can't stand on a summit," he said. "He's walking a Path. You told me so in your office in June. I felt him recognise mine in the churchyard. He knows what a Pinnacle is. He's been trying to reach one for fifty years and he never could, and he never knew why." Harry's voice had gone very quiet. "And then he went into my head and found a line in a book. *No divided thing may stand upon a summit.*"

Dumbledore did not say anything.

"He means to put himself back together," said Harry. "All of it. Every piece. He means to make himself whole again, so that he can open whatever it is he's been walking towards. And he learned that he had to from me."

He stopped.

"He learned it from my head," he said. "Because I went into his. Because you asked me to close the door and I didn't."

* * *

Dumbledore was silent for a long time.

Fawkes moved on his perch. One of the silver instruments on the table by the window puffed a small cloud of smoke and was still. In the portraits on the walls, the old headmasters and headmistresses were all very carefully pretending to be asleep, and none of them were.

Harry read Dumbledore.

He read horror first. It was on the top, and it was not loud, because Dumbledore was not a loud man, but it was very wide and very deep, and it went a long way down. Horror at the gathering. Horror at a man who had spent fifty years making himself impossible to kill, and was now going to give that up, on purpose, for something worse.

Under the horror, agreement.

Dumbledore agreed with him. He had reached the same place, perhaps a second before Harry said it, perhaps a second after. He had put the diadem and the line and the something that looked back side by side, and he had come out where Harry had come out, and he did not argue.

And under the agreement, there was a third thing.

Harry looked at it.

He could not see what it was. That was the strange thing. Dumbledore was open. He had been open to Harry since June. Harry could read every floor of him, all the way down to the bottom. And at the bottom, there was something that Harry could see was there, and could not see into.

It was not closed. It was held.

It was held very hard, the way a man holds a door shut with his whole weight when there is something on the other side of it. And it was aimed at Harry. All of it. Not at Voldemort, or the diadem, or the snake, or the line in the book.

At Harry.

At the cold thing in him that had turned to face the tiara.

"Professor," said Harry.

Dumbledore looked at him.

"The thing in me," said Harry. "That went towards it. In the Room."

"Yes."

"Do you know what it is?"

Dumbledore did not look away. He did not close. He did not do any of the things Harry had read him do, over four and a half years, when he did not want to answer something. He sat behind his desk with his hands folded, and he looked at Harry, and Harry read him, and the door at the bottom of him was held shut with his whole weight, and on the other side of it there was something that had Harry's name on it.

"It is very late," said Dumbledore. "And you have done a great deal tonight. Go to bed, Harry. We will speak again. Soon. I promise you that."

Harry stood up.

He went to the door. He put his hand on it. Then he turned round.

Dumbledore had not moved. He was sitting behind the desk, looking at the place where Harry had been, and he had not unfolded his hands, and he was holding the door shut, still, at the bottom of himself, with everything he had.

Harry read him do it.

He did not say anything. He went down the stairs to where Evelyn was waiting, and she looked at his face, and she did not ask.
