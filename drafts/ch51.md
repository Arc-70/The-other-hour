## Chapter 51: The Diadem

The *Prophet* came on the second Tuesday of January with ten faces on the front of it, and the Great Hall went quiet in a way Harry had never heard it go quiet before.

He read it go.

It went from the Ravenclaw table first, because the Ravenclaws took the *Prophet* more than anybody, and then it went along the Hufflepuffs, and the Gryffindors, and up to the staff table, and last of all to the Slytherins, and it went the way a cold goes into a house when somebody has left the front door open. It did not happen all at once. It happened one person at a time, each one picking up the paper or leaning over to look at a neighbour's, each one reading the headline and then the faces, and each one going quiet.

MASS BREAKOUT FROM AZKABAN.

Ten of them. Ten photographs in two rows of five, black and white, moving. Some of them sneered. Some of them tapped their fingers on the edge of the frame. One of them, in the middle of the top row, had long dark hair that had once been thick and shining and was now matted and tangled, and heavy-lidded eyes, and a thin mouth that curled at the corners, and she looked out of the photograph at the Great Hall with an expression of enormous and private amusement.

BELLATRIX LESTRANGE, said the caption. CONVICTED OF THE TORTURE AND PERMANENT INCAPACITATION OF FRANK AND ALICE LONGBOTTOM.

Harry looked down the Hall at the Gryffindor table.

Neville was holding the paper in both hands. He was not moving at all.

Harry read him. He could not have stopped himself if he had tried. He had read Neville every day for four and a half years, and he had read the woman in the bed on the fourth floor of St Mungo's on Christmas afternoon, with one small clear light left in her. He read Neville now.

He was not afraid. That was the strange thing. The whole Hall was afraid, and Neville was not. Neville was something Harry had never once felt in him, not in four and a half years, and it was so quiet and so steady and so new that for a moment Harry did not recognise it.

It was hate.

Harry looked away.

He read the rest of the Hall instead. Fear, mostly. It was rising. It was coming up through the school the way water comes up through a cellar floor, slowly and from everywhere at once, and it was not going to go down again. The Ministry said it was Sirius Black. The Ministry said Black had helped them out, as he had helped himself out two and a half years ago. Harry read that nobody in the Hall believed it. He read that nobody in the Hall was going to say so.

And at the Slytherin table, among the fear, something else was rising too.

He found it in a dozen places. He found it most of all three seats along, in a pale pointed face bent over the front page.

Draco Malfoy was proud.

A small feeling, yet. Small and tight and very bright, and it was aimed at the photographs, and at one of them in particular, the woman in the middle of the top row with the amused eyes. His aunt. His mother's sister. And under the pride, as there was always something under everything in Draco, there was a thing Harry had felt in him since the summer, since a churchyard Draco had not been in.

It was eagerness.

It was the eagerness of a boy who has been told there is a game, and that his father is playing it, and that one day soon he will be allowed to play too.

* * *

Educational Decree Number Twenty-Four went up on the noticeboard in the Slytherin common room the following Monday. It had already gone up on every other noticeboard in the school, and on the walls of the Great Hall, and on the door of the Owlery, in large black letters.

*All Student Organisations, Societies, Teams, Groups and Clubs are henceforth disbanded. An Organisation, Society, Team, Group or Club is hereby defined as a regular meeting of three or more students.*

There was more. It said that permission to re-form could be sought from the High Inquisitor, which was Umbridge, and that any student found to have formed or belonged to an organisation that had not been approved would be expelled.

Evelyn read it twice, at breakfast, and then read the small print at the bottom, which nobody else ever read, and said, "Twenty-four."

"What about it?" said Millicent.

"It's the third one since September. Twenty-two was in August, about appointing staff. Twenty-three made her High Inquisitor. This is twenty-four." Evelyn turned her teacup a quarter turn on its saucer. "They're numbered from nineteen seventy-one. There were twenty-one decrees in twenty-four years, and there have been three in five months."

"So?"

"So she's in a hurry," said Evelyn.

Millicent thought about that for a while.

"Is she going to stop us?"

"She's going to try," said Evelyn. "She'll need to know about us first." She looked at Harry over her toast. "Who do you think she'll ask?"

* * *

Umbridge asked him on Thursday.

She asked him over tea, in the pink office, with the fifty-six kittens frisking on their plates. It was the fifth time he had been there since September. He knew the cups now, and the doilies, and the way she put three sugars in her own tea and none in his, because he had told her once he did not take it, and she had remembered.

She was not happy today.

He read it before she had poured. The sweet steady pleasure was there, the way it always was. But it was thin, and under it there was something tight and anxious and cross, aimed at nobody in particular, aimed at the whole school, which would not do as it was told. And under that, very small, aimed at him, there was hope.

She was hoping he would help her.

"I have reason to believe," she said, stirring, "that there is a student association in this school. An illegal one. That has been meeting since the autumn. In defiance of the Ministry's very clear wishes." She put down her spoon. "I believe it may be teaching students to use spells. Offensive spells, Mr Potter. Against whom, I wonder?"

"That's dreadful, Professor," said Harry.

"Isn't it?"

"Do you know who's in it?"

"I have my suspicions." She smiled at him. "I had hoped that you, being so well placed, might have heard something. You know so many people. All the houses like you, I think. Even the Gryffindors, now, a little."

He read her.

She did not suspect him. That was the first thing. She had never once suspected him. She had been certain, since the first Defence lesson, that he was hers, and the certainty had no cracks in it. She was asking him because he was useful, the way you ask the most useful person in the room.

He gave her what she wanted.

He did it slowly, the way he had done it in Courtroom Ten, one piece at a time. He said that he had heard something. He said it reluctantly, as though it cost him a little to be telling tales. He said that he had heard some of the Ravenclaws talking in the library about a club. He said he thought it was the fifth- and sixth-year Ravenclaws, but he was not sure. He said that he thought they met in the evenings. In one of the classrooms on the second floor, possibly. Near the old Arithmancy room. He said he had seen lights there late at night, once or twice, on his way back from the library, and had thought nothing of it at the time.

All of it was false.

He read her take it. He read the anxiety in her go down, and the pleasure come up under it, warm and grateful, and the hope turn into something firmer.

"The second floor," she said. "The *Ravenclaws*."

"I could be wrong, Professor."

"Oh, I doubt that very much, Mr Potter." She reached across the table and patted his hand, twice, with her short fingers. "I doubt that very much indeed. Thank you. Thank you so much. I knew I could rely on you."

"I'm glad I could help."

"You've been more help than you know," said Umbridge, and she meant it, all of it, all the way down.

He went out of the office and down the stairs with a biscuit in a pink napkin.

Draco Malfoy was on the landing below.

* * *

He was leaning against the wall by the window with his arms folded, and he did not move when Harry came down. He had been waiting. Harry read that he had been waiting for some time.

"Potter," he said.

"Malfoy."

"Tea with the High Inquisitor again?"

"Fifth time."

"I know," said Draco. "I've been counting."

Harry stopped on the step.

He read him. Draco was angry. That was the top of him, as it had been since September, since the first Defence lesson, since the seat he had saved all summer had been taken. It was an old, settled anger, worn in.

But under it there was something Harry had not felt in Draco before, and it took him a moment to put a name to it, because it was so unlike anything Draco usually felt.

It was attention.

Not jealousy. Not contempt. Plain, cold, careful attention, aimed at Harry, the way you look at a card trick for the second time, watching the hands.

"You're very good at it," said Draco. "Aren't you?"

"Good at what?"

"Telling people what they want." Draco pushed himself off the wall. "I've been watching you. Since September. You go in there and you say exactly the right thing, every time. You say it to Umbridge. You said it to the Wizengamot, my father says. You say it to Snape, when you can be bothered. You say it to the Gryffindors, now. You say it to everybody." He tilted his head. "You look at them first, don't you? Just before. For about a second. Like you're listening to something."

Harry said nothing.

"I always wondered what that was," said Draco. "I've been wondering for five years. You look at me like that. You've always looked at me like that." He smiled, thinly. "And then you always know exactly what to say. Don't you?"

He went past Harry up the stairs, towards the pink office, without waiting for an answer.

Harry stood on the step.

He read Draco all the way up the stairs. The attention did not go down. It went with him, cold and careful, and it stayed aimed at Harry right up to the moment Draco knocked on Umbridge's door.

* * *

Umbridge searched the second floor that Saturday night with Filch and four members of a new group she was calling the Inquisitorial Squad, all of them Slytherins, all of them wearing small silver I's on their robes. Draco was one of them. They found nothing. She searched it again on the Sunday, and on the Tuesday, and caught two sixth-year Ravenclaws in the old Arithmancy room at ten o'clock at night doing something that had nothing to do with defence, and gave them a week of detentions each, and was very pleased.

The group met on Wednesday, in the Room on the seventh floor, as usual.

Harry taught them the Patronus.

It went badly for an hour, the way it always went badly, and then it went well very suddenly, the way it always did. Hermione got hers first, a silver otter that went round and round her in circles and made her laugh out loud. Then Luna, a hare, which sat up in front of her and looked at everybody with interest. Then Ron, quite unexpectedly, a small energetic terrier that ran straight to Hermione's otter and tried to play with it, and Ron went red to the ears.

"Shut up," said Ron, to Fred and George, before either of them had said a word.

"We didn't say anything."

"You were going to."

"We were," agreed George. "We were going to say it was a lovely little dog."

Millicent produced a large silver shape that might have been a bear, or might have been a very big dog, and which knocked over a bookcase.

"Sorry," said Millicent, loudly, to the bookcase.

Neville tried for an hour and got nothing, and at the end of the hour Harry went over to him and said, quietly, so that nobody else could hear, "Not a memory. Somebody. Think of somebody, and think about what they'd feel if they saw you do it."

Neville looked at him.

Harry read him, and knew who Neville was thinking of before Neville did. He read the small clear light. He read it in Neville, now, the same light, aimed back.

Neville lifted his wand.

No shape came, not yet. A shining silver mist came instead, very bright, about the size of a door, and it hung in front of Neville for nearly five seconds before it faded, and the whole room had stopped to watch it.

Neville lowered his wand. He was very pink.

"Well," he said. "That's… well."

"Next week," said Harry. "That'll have a shape next week."

He read Evelyn, by the door, without meaning to. There was nothing. There never was. But she was writing something in her notebook, very small, and he would have given a great deal to know what it was.

* * *

Draco was given an errand at the end of February.

Harry knew the day it happened. It was a Monday. It was a letter, at breakfast, by the Malfoy eagle owl, and Draco read it at the Slytherin table and folded it up very small and put it in the inside pocket of his robes, and Harry, three seats along, read him do it.

Fear.

It was the first thing, and it was large. It came up in Draco the moment he read the letter and it stayed up, and it was not the fear Harry was used to in him, the old fear of not being good enough. It was simpler than that. It was the fear of a boy who has been asked to do something real.

Under the fear there was pride.

It was the pride from January, from the front page of the *Prophet*, only it was larger now, and it was aimed at himself. He had been chosen. Of all the sons of all the people who had stood in a churchyard, he had been asked.

And under the pride, at the bottom, there was importance. It was the feeling of a boy who has been given a task by someone who matters, and who is going to carry it around inside his robes, folded up very small, until it is done.

Harry read it every day for a week.

He read it at breakfast and in Potions and at the edge of the Quidditch pitch, where Draco stood with his broom over his shoulder and did not seem to hear a word Montague was saying. He read it come up whenever Draco touched the inside pocket of his robes, which was a great many times a day. He read the fear stay large, and the pride stay large, and the importance under both, steady.

On the Friday he read something new.

It was resolve. Draco had made up his mind. It came up in him at lunch, quite suddenly, while he was eating a sausage, and stayed.

"Tonight," said Harry.

"What?" said Theo.

"Nothing."

He went to find Evelyn.

* * *

"You want me to come," said Evelyn.

"Yes."

"Under the Cloak. Following Malfoy. At night. Through a castle with an Inquisitorial Squad in it that he belongs to."

"Yes."

Evelyn looked at him across the desk in the fourth-floor classroom. The moon was not up yet. There was a candle on the desk between them, and she had been reading by it when he came in, and she had put a finger in her book to keep the place.

"Why?" she said.

"Because whatever he's doing, I want somebody there who'll see it," said Harry. "Not feel it. See it. You see things I don't."

She thought about that.

"And because," said Harry, "if it goes wrong, you're the only person in this school I can't be made to stop seeing."

She looked at him for a long moment. Then she took her finger out of the book and closed it.

"What time?" she said.

* * *

Draco left the common room at twenty past eleven.

They followed him on the Map and under the Cloak, close together, Evelyn's shoulder against Harry's arm, their feet showing at the bottom if anybody had cared to look. Nobody did. Draco went up through the dungeons and the entrance hall and up the marble staircase, and he did not use the Inquisitorial Squad's routes, which went past the staff rooms. He used the back stairs. He went past the second-floor classrooms that Umbridge had searched three times, and the third-floor corridor, and up and up, and on the seventh floor he stopped.

He was in the corridor with the great woven hanging of Barnabas the Barmy.

Harry felt Evelyn go still beside him.

Draco walked past the blank stretch of wall opposite the hanging. He turned at the end of the corridor and walked back. He turned again.

Three times.

A door appeared in the wall. Not the door Harry knew. Taller and older, black and heavy, with iron hinges, and Draco looked up and down the corridor once, very quickly, and opened it and went in, and it closed behind him.

The wall was blank again.

"That's the Room," whispered Evelyn.

"Yes."

"It isn't our door."

"No. He asked for something else."

"Can you get in?"

Harry thought about it. He had learned the Room from a diary, three years ago, and from another world, and he knew how it worked. It gave you what you needed. It did not give you somebody else's room unless you needed the same thing they did.

He thought about Draco, and the letter in the inside pocket, and the importance.

Draco did not need a place to hide. He did not need a place to practise. He had been sent to fetch something. And in a castle a thousand years old, with ten generations of students hiding things from ten generations of teachers, there was only one place Harry could think of that you would go to fetch something you had been told was there.

He walked past the wall three times, with Evelyn under the Cloak beside him, and thought: *I need the place where things are hidden.*

The door appeared. Tall, black, iron hinges.

They went in.

* * *

It was the size of a cathedral.

That was the first thing. The second thing was that it was full. It was so full that at first Harry could not see the walls, or the ceiling, or anything but the things. There were towers of them. There were streets of them. There were whole cities made of things that students had hidden from teachers over a thousand years, stacked twenty and thirty feet high, with narrow alleys running between them and light coming down from high windows somewhere very far above.

Broken furniture. Thousands of books, many of them marked BANNED or CONFISCATED. Winged catapults. Fanged frisbees, some still twitching. Chipped bottles of potion. Hats and jewels and cloaks. Dragon eggshells. Corked bottles of something that still shimmered. A rusting sword. A heavy, bloodstained axe.

And the smell of dust, everywhere, very old.

Evelyn did not say anything. Harry felt her look, from under the Cloak, at everything, and felt her start to count, and felt her stop, because there was too much.

Draco was ahead of them.

Harry did not need the Map. He could feel him. He was moving down one of the alleys between the towers of junk, quickly, the way you move in a place you have been told how to find your way through, and he was afraid, and he was proud, and he was counting turnings under his breath.

They followed.

Left at a stuffed troll. Right at a broken cabinet. Left again at a pile of old school robes with a dead bird on top of them.

Draco stopped.

There was a cabinet in front of him. It was tall and black and its doors were shut, and it was very old, and it did not look like anything in particular. Draco stood in front of it for a long moment and looked at it, curiously, with his head on one side, the way he had looked at the Map in Harry's hands once in third year without knowing what it was. He put out a hand and touched one of the doors. He opened it an inch. He looked inside.

Then he shut it again and went on.

Harry noted it. He did not know why. He noted it the way Evelyn noted things, because it was there and because Draco had looked at it.

They went round another corner.

* * *

There was a bust.

It was the bust of an ugly old warlock, pocked and stained, standing on a crate at the junction of two alleys. Somebody had put a dusty old wig on it, long ago, and a tarnished tiara on top of the wig.

Draco stopped in front of it.

He looked at it for a long time. Harry read him look. The fear and the pride and the importance were all there, and on top of them now there was something else, a kind of hush, the hush of a boy who has found the thing he was sent for and is a little afraid to touch it.

Harry was twenty feet away, under the Cloak, at the corner of a tower of broken chairs.

And he felt it.

Not Draco. Not anything in Draco. He felt something else, and it was coming from the bust, from the thing on top of the wig, from a tarnished diadem with a blue stone set in the front of it and words engraved round the band that he could not read from here.

It was hungry.

He knew it at once. He had felt it once before, in his second year, in a small black book with a dead boy's name on the back. He had held that book in his hands every night for weeks, and talked to it, and been talked to, and every time he had opened it he had felt the same thing coming up off the page, before anything else, before the charm or the patience or the loneliness or the envy.

Hunger.

Objects did not have feelings. Harry had known that since he was four. A chair was a chair, and a book was a book, and a crown was a crown, and you could no more read them than you could read the floor. That was the rule. There had been only one exception in his life, and he had driven a basilisk fang through the middle of it.

This was the second.

The diadem was hungry. It sat on top of the old wig on the ugly bust, tarnished and dusty, and it was hungry exactly the way the diary had been hungry, a thin cold endless wanting that did not stop and had no bottom.

Draco reached up and took it off the bust.

* * *

And something in Harry answered.

He did not do it. He did not decide anything. It happened the way a cough happens, or the way your eyes go to your own name on a page. Something in him, very small, very far down, below anything he had ever read in himself, moved.

It moved towards the diadem.

It was cold. It was small and cold and it moved the way a compass needle moves when you bring a magnet near it, a little shiver and then a turn and then a steady pull, all in one direction. It pulled towards the thing in Draco's hands. It pulled towards the hunger. It knew it. It knew it the way you know a voice in a crowd.

Harry had felt it before.

He had felt it once in a boy in a red and gold scarf in another world, at the very bottom of him, a cold separate splinter, and been sick in a bathroom on the fourth floor. He had felt a smaller version of it, once, in himself, afterwards, and called it a stain, and told himself it was nothing. He had felt it again in the summer, lying in his bed at Privet Drive. He had told himself it was nothing then too.

It was not nothing.

It was in him, and it was pulling towards a tarnished crown in Malfoy's hands, and it was very much larger than it had been in the summer. It was so much larger that for a moment it was the only thing he could feel. Not Evelyn beside him. Not Draco twenty feet away. Not the dust or the dark or the great cathedral of hidden things. Only the pull, small and cold and certain, from the bottom of him towards the hunger.

He stood still.

He did not know he had stood still. He did not know how long he had stood still. He knew, afterwards, that Evelyn had taken hold of his arm under the Cloak and was gripping it very hard, and that she had been gripping it for some time, and that she had said "Potter" twice, very quietly, close to his ear.

Draco was gone.

He had wrapped the diadem in a cloth and put it inside his robes, and turned and gone back down the alley the way he had come, quickly, counting his turnings, and Harry had stood at the corner of a tower of broken chairs and watched him go and done nothing at all.

The door at the far end of the room opened and shut.

"Potter," said Evelyn, for the third time.

He looked at her.

She was very close, under the Cloak, in the dark, and her face was the face it always was, and he could not read anything in it, and he never would.

"What happened?" she said.

"I don't know."

"You stopped. You just stopped. You let him go."

"I know."

"Why?"

He did not answer for a long time.

"I need to see Dumbledore," he said.

* * *

It was a quarter to one when the gargoyle let him up.

He did not know the password. He did not need it. He said his name and the gargoyle moved, the way it had moved for him in December, and he went up the moving stair alone. Evelyn had gone back to the dungeons. She had not asked to come. She had looked at him in the corridor outside the Room for a long moment, with nothing in her face, and then she had said, "Tell him all of it," and gone.

Dumbledore was at his desk, writing. He looked up.

He looked at Harry's face, and put down his quill.

"Sit down, Harry," he said.

Harry sat down.

He told him all of it.

He had not told anybody all of it. He had told Evelyn most of it, in pieces, over years, and he had told Dumbledore some of it, on a night in June, in a great rush, with Sirius on his knees and a dead boy's hand still cold on his. But he had never told anybody all of it, in order, from the beginning.

He did now.

He told him about the diary, and the hunger he had felt off it the first time he opened it, before anything else. He told him about the weeks he had talked to Tom in it, and how much he had wanted what Tom offered. He told him about the study, and the black book, and the second hand at the end of it, which Evelyn had read to him in Latin in his third year.

*The Paths are open to many, but no divided thing may stand upon a summit.*

There was the boy in the other world, and the cold splinter at the bottom of him, and being sick. He told him about the smaller one he had felt in himself, afterwards, and dismissed.

He told him about the scar. He told him what he had not told him in December. That he had gone out along it on purpose, seven times, in three weeks, every few nights, and come back with the notes he had given Fawkes. That on the fifteenth of December he had gone out and something had turned round and looked back at him. That it had gone along his memories like a man along a shelf. That it had found the round room, and the black desk, and the book, and opened it, and turned to the last page.

That it had gone still.

Then tonight. Draco and the errand. The Room. The cabinet Draco had looked at. The bust and the wig and the tarnished diadem with the blue stone. The hunger coming off it.

And the thing in himself that had answered.

He told it last. He told it very plainly, the way you put down something heavy you have carried a long way. He did not look at Dumbledore while he said it. He looked at the silver instruments on the shelf behind the desk, which were whirring and puffing as they always did, and he told them.

Then he stopped.

The office was very quiet.

* * *

Dumbledore did not say anything for a long time.

He sat behind his desk with his long fingers together, and he looked at Harry over them, and Harry did not try to read him. He did not need to. Dumbledore was not closed. He had not been closed to Harry when they were alone since June, and he was not closed now, and what was in him was so large that it filled the office.

Then he began to talk.

He did not talk the way he usually talked to Harry. He did not talk the way he talked to students, or to Fudge, or to the Order. He did not talk around anything. He talked the way Harry had heard him talk once before, on the night in June, after the graveyard, when he had said *I will say it once*.

He talked as though Harry were his equal.

"There is a kind of magic," he said, "that the books in this library will not tell you about. It is the darkest magic there is. A wizard who has committed murder, and who knows how, may take the damage that the murder has done to his soul, and use it. He may split the soul along the crack the murder has made, and put a piece of it into an object, and hide the object. And so long as the object survives, the wizard cannot wholly die. His body may be destroyed. But a piece of him remains, anchored to the earth, and from that piece he may come back."

"A Horcrux," said Harry.

Dumbledore looked at him over his spectacles.

"Where did you hear that word?"

"The study. There's a book on the second shelf from the left, in the bottom row, that Evelyn wouldn't read me past the first page. She said it was the only book in there that made her want to wash her hands. That was the word on the first page."

"Yes," said Dumbledore quietly. "I imagine it would be."

He went on.

He told Harry that he had suspected for a very long time that Tom Riddle had made a Horcrux. He told him that the diary had confirmed it, in Harry's second year, and that what Harry had described tonight had confirmed something worse. Tom had not made one. Tom had made several. He had split himself again and again, each time with a murder, each time into something precious, something that mattered to him.

"A ring," said Dumbledore, "that belonged to his mother's family. I am almost certain of that one. A locket, which belonged to Salazar Slytherin, and which I have been looking for, without success, for some years. A cup, I think, that belonged to Helga Hufflepuff. And the diadem of Rowena Ravenclaw, which has been lost for a thousand years, and which Tom found, and which you saw tonight, in the hands of Draco Malfoy." He paused. "And I believe the snake. The one you saw in December."

"The diary was one."

"The diary was one. You destroyed it." Dumbledore looked at him. "There is nothing left of that piece. Nothing at all."

Harry sat very still.

He counted. He did not mean to. He thought about Evelyn and her lists. *A ring. A locket. A cup. A diadem. A snake. A diary, gone.* Five left. Six pieces, and the man himself. Seven.

"He's gathering them," said Harry.

"Yes. I believe so."

"Why? If they're what keeps him alive. If he's hidden them all over the country so nobody can find them. Why would he want them back in one place? Where somebody could…"

He stopped.

He stopped because he knew.

He knew it before Dumbledore could say it. He knew it the way he had known, in December, lying awake after the cold turned round, that it was not a dream. He had told himself it was a dream. He had told himself very carefully, one piece at a time, and he had very nearly believed himself.

"He read the book," said Harry.

Dumbledore did not say anything.

"In my head. On the fifteenth. He went into the study, in my memory, and he opened the book and he read the last line." Harry heard his own voice. It was very quiet. "*No divided thing may stand upon a summit.* He's on a Path. He recognised mine in the churchyard. He said it out loud. *That is not a spell, that is a Path.* He's been walking his for fifty years, and he's never got to the top, and he never knew why."

Dumbledore was very still.

"And now he does," said Harry. "Because he read it. In my head. Because I went into his, and left the door open, and he came back through it and walked down my shelves and found the one book in the world that told him why." He looked at Dumbledore. "He's going to put himself back together. All the pieces. He's going to give up the Horcruxes, and being impossible to kill, so that he can be whole. So that he can stand on a summit."

The silver instruments whirred.

"Yes," said Dumbledore. "I think that is exactly what he means to do."

"And I told him how."

Dumbledore did not answer that.

* * *

Harry read him.

He had not read him since he started talking. He read him now, because he could not stop himself, and because he wanted to know, and because the office was very quiet and Dumbledore was open and there was nothing in the way.

There was horror.

It was at the top, and it was very large, and none of it was for Harry. All of it was for the thing itself. At a whole soul, made out of all those pieces, walking up a Path to its summit. At what would be standing at the top when it got there.

Under the horror there was agreement.

It was cold and quick and certain. It was the agreement of a man who has already worked out everything the boy in front of him has just said, and who has been sitting in this office alone for some time, with his quill, writing it down.

And under the agreement, there was something else.

Harry could not get at it.

It was not closed. Dumbledore had not closed. It was held. It was held the way it had been held in December, in this office, at ten past midnight, when Harry had asked whether he was the snake. It was held down with both hands, very hard, under everything else, and it was very large, larger than the horror, larger than anything in him.

And it was about Harry.

Harry knew that. He did not know how he knew it. He could not see what it was. He could only feel the shape of it pressing up against the hands that held it down, and the shape was the shape of something about him. Something Dumbledore knew. Something that had to do with the hunger, and the cold thing in Harry that had moved towards the diadem like a compass needle, and the scar.

Dumbledore looked at him across the desk.

Harry waited.

He waited the way Dumbledore had waited for him, once, on a night in June, for twenty minutes, with the fire low and Sirius on his knees. He waited without moving, and without asking, and without pushing, because he could have pushed, and they both knew it.

Dumbledore opened his mouth.

He closed it again.

"It is very late," he said, after a moment. "And you have had a great shock. I would like you to go to bed, Harry. I would like us to speak again tomorrow. There are things I must think about first."

Harry did not move.

"You haven't said anything about the scar," he said.

"No," said Dumbledore.

He said it quite gently. He did not look away. He sat behind his desk in the quiet office with the silver instruments whirring round him, and he looked at Harry with something in his face that was very old and very tired and very sorry.

And he did not say anything else.
