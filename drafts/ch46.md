## Chapter 46: Little Whinging

The news came on at nine o'clock, and Harry watched it every night from the end of the sofa.

Uncle Vernon did not like it. Uncle Vernon did not like Harry in the sitting room at all, and had said so on the first evening, at some length, with the word *boy* in it eleven times. But Uncle Vernon had also said it from the far side of the room, standing behind his own armchair with one hand on the back of it, and Harry had read him while he said it, and had sat down on the end of the sofa anyway.

Uncle Vernon was afraid. It was the top of him, and it was larger than it had been at Easter, and it filled him up the way water fills a ditch after rain. He had not said *boy* since.

So Harry watched the news.

He watched it every night for four weeks. He watched a hosepipe ban in Yorkshire, and a lorry on its side on the M25 near Clacket Lane, and a budgie in Sunderland that could say the names of all the Beatles.

He was looking for something else.

A man found dead in his house with nothing wrong with him. A bridge that had come down without anybody touching it. Something said in the puzzled voice the newsreader used at the end, just before the weather.

There was nothing.

There was nothing in the *Daily Prophet*, either, which came every morning to the kitchen window and which Aunt Petunia had stopped screaming at in the second week. On the eleventh of July there was a piece on page six by Rita Skeeter about a boy who had been through a great deal and might be suffering from delusions, and who was, the piece said, very much to be pitied.

Harry read it twice.

It was the third piece. The one Evelyn had kept in her cardigan pocket, folded twice. Somebody had put the adjectives back in.

He cut it out and put it in the drawer of his bedside table, under his socks, and did not know why he wanted to keep it, and kept it.

* * *

Evelyn wrote every eleven days.

The first letter came on the second of July, and the second on the thirteenth, and the third on the twenty-fourth, each at about a quarter past seven in the morning, by a school owl that sat on the end of his bed afterwards and looked at him with contempt until he gave it a biscuit.

They were about the weather.

*It is very hot here. The yew walk is the only cool place and Pinch says the roses are suffering. I am reading Josephine Tey, The Daughter of Time, for the second time. It is about a man in a hospital bed who decides that a king everybody believes was a murderer was nothing of the kind. He works it out from portraits. I think it is cleverer than it is fair. Father has had people to dinner three times this fortnight and I have passed the salt on each occasion. Mother is well. E.S.*

Harry read it four times.

He could not read her anywhere. But he had known her four years, and he knew what she put in and what she left out.

People to dinner three times in a fortnight. That was not ordinary. Corvin Selwyn did not have people to dinner. He had people to his study, one at a time, with the door shut.

And Evelyn had not named them.

Evelyn named everything. She named the edition of a book and the exact Hufflepuff who had been sick on a train at Peterborough. She had not named the people at her father's table.

*Mother is well.* Evelyn had never once in four years written that her mother was well.

The second letter was about a book on bees she had found in the library at the Hall, and how hot it was, and that Lucan had come home for a weekend and gone back to London on the Sunday. *He has been made something at the Ministry. I did not ask what. He was very pleased about it and very tired and he fell asleep in the garden after lunch in his shirt-sleeves, which I have never seen him do. Pinch put a hat over his face.*

The third letter was four lines long, and the last of them said: *My uncle came to lunch. He was very cheerful. He talked about the cricket.*

Harry sat on his bed and looked at that line for a long time.

He thought about a churchyard, and a voice under a mask saying *there we are* to the man beside him, as though they had both just found the right seats at a concert.

He wrote back that it was hot in Surrey as well, and that he had read *The Daughter of Time* in his second year and still thought the king had done it. He did not write that he watched the news every night for bodies, because letters could be read, and somebody in the Ministry was very interested now in Harry Potter's state of mind.

He wrote: *I hope the cricket improves.*

She would know what he meant. She always knew what he meant. That was the one comfort of writing to a person he could not read: he never had to wonder whether she had read him.

* * *

Dumbledore wrote once.

It came in the first week of July, by Fawkes, who arrived in Harry's room at four in the morning in a small burst of gold flame that singed the curtain and woke Hedwig, who did not forgive either of them. It was one sheet. The handwriting was thin and sloping, in green ink.

*Not yet, I am afraid. I am watched a little more closely than I would like this summer, and I think it very likely that you are too. I would rather not lead anybody to your door.*

*In the meantime, the first exercise. You will know the one I mean. Do it every morning, before you read anybody. Before you read the house. It is dull, and it is the foundation of everything else, and I have never known anybody who did it enough.*

*I have not forgotten what I owe you. A.D.*

Harry knew the one he meant.

It was the first thing in the small black book in Slytherin's study, which he had done for about a fortnight in his third year, until he could do other things. *Be quiet in thyself.* Turn everybody down, and then turn himself down too, until there was nothing at all.

He did it at six every morning, sitting on the floor with his back against the bed. It was the dullest thing he had ever done, and on most mornings he managed it for about a minute before something came up in him (Cedric, or a jam jar, or a white hand on a gravestone) and he had to start again.

He did it anyway.

He did not tell Dumbledore about the scar.

He had meant to. He had begun a letter three times. But the scar had been quiet since the first night home, except twice, both times late, both times when he was nearly asleep. Once it had been impatience, cold and very patient at the same time, which he had not known was possible. Once it had been something like irritation, aimed far away at somebody who had been slow.

Both times it had gone in a few seconds.

Both times he had lain awake afterwards and told himself that a letter could be read, and that Dumbledore was being watched, and that the Ministry already thought he was mad.

All of which was true.

None of which was why he did not write it.

* * *

Dudley had a gang.

At fifteen it was four boys who smoked behind the parade of shops on Magnolia Road and kicked the bus shelter until the glass came out. Piers Polkiss was still one of them, taller and no less thin, and he still laughed a second after Dudley did. One of the others was called Malcolm and had a moped he could not start.

Dudley boxed now. There was a photograph of him on the mantelpiece, in shorts and a vest, holding a cup, between Aunt Marge and the clock, and Aunt Petunia dusted it twice a day. He was not soft any more, or not all of it. He looked at people in the street until they looked away.

Harry read him most days, because he could not help it.

Dudley was bored.

That was the top of him, and it was always there, and it was very loud. It was the boredom of a boy who had been given everything he had ever asked for and had found that the bus shelter had only so much glass in it.

Under the boredom, Dudley was cruel.

Harry knew this one. He had known it since the cupboard. Uncle Vernon's anger was hot. Dudley's cruelty was cool and idle and pleased with itself, and it went looking for things the way a cat goes along a hedge, and when it found something smaller it stopped.

And under that, this summer, there was something new.

Harry found it on the fourth day and did not at first believe it. He read it again at breakfast on the fifth. It was small, and it was very far down, and it was the last thing Harry would have expected to find in his cousin.

Dudley was afraid of himself.

He was afraid of what his hands did. Harry read it come up once, very briefly, when Dudley came in late from the park on a Thursday night with the knuckles of his right hand split, and washed them at the kitchen sink with his back to the room, and Aunt Petunia said *Did you fall, Duddykins?* and he said *Yeah*.

It came up, and Dudley pushed it down, and it went.

Harry did not say anything.

He did not feel sorry for him.

He was almost sure he did not feel sorry for him.

* * *

The second of August was a Thursday. The man on the news said it was the hottest day in Surrey since 1976.

Harry spent most of it in the play park at the end of Magnolia Crescent, on the one swing that had not been broken, because Uncle Vernon had come home early from Grunnings and was sitting in his vest in front of the cricket with the curtains drawn, and the fear in him had gone sour in the heat.

He read the street, the way other people listen to traffic. A woman two gardens away who was angry at a hose. A man washing a car who was thinking about somebody who was not his wife.

He read Mrs Figg.

She lived on Wisteria Walk, two streets over, in a house full of cats and cabbage, and he had read her since he was small. Soft and grey and sad, always the same, the way a cat is always the same. And with an idea, which he had had since he was about seven and never been able to explain, that the sadness was something to do with him.

She was sad now. She was also, which was new, rather anxious, and the anxiety was aimed at the park.

Harry looked round. There was nobody there but him and a cat on the wall by the gate that he thought might be one of hers.

He decided it was the heat.

* * *

Dudley's gang came through the park at about nine.

Harry heard them before he read them. They came along the path from the far gate in a line, loud, and Malcolm was pushing the moped, and somebody had a radio playing something with a great deal of bass in it. Dudley was at the front. Dudley was always at the front.

They did not see Harry at first. Then Piers did.

Harry read Piers see him. It was very quick. Something small and spiteful, looking up at Dudley to see what Dudley would do.

Dudley did nothing.

He looked at Harry on the swing for about two seconds and looked away and went on walking. The gang went with him, out of the far gate and up Magnolia Crescent, and Harry heard Piers say something and somebody laugh, a second late.

Harry sat on the swing until the street lights came on.

He gave them ten minutes. Then he got up and went home the long way, round by Magnolia Crescent and the alley that ran between it and Wisteria Walk, because the long way took him past fewer windows.

Dudley was at the corner.

He was alone. The gang had gone off in different directions along Magnolia Road, and Dudley was walking home with his hands in his pockets, and he heard Harry behind him and turned round.

"Followed me, have you?"

"No," said Harry. "I'm going home."

They walked. There was nothing else to do. They were going the same way, and neither of them would let the other go first, and so they walked side by side up Magnolia Crescent, which they had not done since primary school, and neither of them said anything for about a hundred yards.

Then Dudley said, "You were talking in your sleep last night."

Harry did not answer.

"Moaning. *Don't*, you said. *Cedric.* Who's Cedric? Your boyfriend?"

Harry read him.

Bored. Cruel, cool and idle, along the hedge. He had found something smaller. He was enjoying it.

"Somebody who died," said Harry.

Dudley stopped walking.

It was very short. It was half a step. Harry read it, and it was a jolt of something under the cruelty, as though Dudley had put his foot down on a stair that was not there. Then he started walking again, faster.

"Liar," he said.

"Yes," said Harry. "Probably."

They turned into the alley.

* * *

The alley ran between the high back fences of Magnolia Crescent and Wisteria Walk, and it had no lights in it, and it was about two hundred yards long. Harry had walked it a thousand times. It smelled of creosote and dustbins and, in summer, of the honeysuckle somebody had let grow over their fence halfway down.

They were about a third of the way along when the stars went out.

Harry looked up.

There had been stars. There had been a strip of dark blue sky above the alley, and a few stars in it, and the orange glow of Magnolia Road at the far end. Now there was nothing. No cloud had come over. The sky had simply been taken away, and something put in its place that did not let any light through.

The orange glow went too.

"What're you doing?" said Dudley. His voice had gone up. "Stop it. Stop it!"

"I'm not doing anything."

It was cold.

It was the hottest night of the year and it was cold, and the cold had come down the alley all at once, from both ends, and Harry knew it.

He had felt it on a train in his third year. He had felt it on a lake shore. He had felt it on a summer night by the lake, on a Thursday in June, with Sirius on the ground.

He read the alley.

He read Dudley, who was terrified. He read, further off, Mrs Figg, who had gone from anxious to frantic, and was moving.

And he read the other thing.

There were two of them. One at each end of the alley. He could not see them. He could not see anything. But he could read them, the way he always could, and there was nothing in them, nothing at all, no fear and no pleasure and no grief, nothing on top and nothing underneath.

There was only appetite.

It was the plainest thing he had ever read in anything alive, if they were alive. It did not want Harry. It did not want Dudley. It did not want anything in particular. It wanted, the way water wants to go downhill, and the two of them were coming down the alley towards the only warm things in it.

"Dudley," said Harry. "Don't move. Stay with me."

He had his wand out. He did not remember taking it out.

Dudley moved.

Harry heard him do it. He heard a sound like a large boy swinging a fist at nothing in the dark, and then feet, running, the wrong way, towards the Wisteria Walk end, straight into what was coming.

"Dudley, *no*…"

He heard Dudley fall.

He did not hear him hit the ground. He heard a kind of cry, very short, and then nothing, and he read Dudley, and the terror in him went suddenly very small and very far away, the way a voice does when somebody shuts a door on it.

The cold was on Harry now.

It was the one from the Wisteria Walk end, bending over Dudley, and the other one from behind, very close. Harry could hear it breathing. A long, rattling, wet breath, like air being drawn through something that had been under water for a long time.

And the voices started.

He had known they would. He had heard them on the train in his third year, and on the lake shore. A woman screaming. A high cold voice. A flash of green light.

He heard them again.

He also heard, this time, under them, a voice saying *kill the spare*.

He heard it say it quite calmly, the way a man asks somebody to pass him a newspaper, and he heard the green light again, and he saw a boy lying on the grass with his eyes open, and for a second he could not find his wand hand.

He found it.

He thought about the lake.

He did not think about the other Sirius, though it was there, and it had always been there, a love he had read once in a room above a bar and kept. He did not think about the stag in the dormitory curtains. He thought about a flat rock on the far side of the lake a month ago, and the sun going down behind the castle, and a plain girl in a grey cardigan saying *I signed for the jar*.

He thought about laughing until his eyes were wet.

"*Expecto patronum*," said Harry.

The stag came out of the end of his wand like something breaking a window.

It was not as big as it had been on the lake shore in his third year. It did not need to be. It was silver, and it was very bright, and it filled the alley from fence to fence, and it put its head down and went at the thing behind Harry with its antlers, and the thing went backwards.

Harry read the appetite turn. It did not turn the way a person turns. It turned the way a draught turns when a door is shut on it. It simply went somewhere else.

"*Dudley!*"

The stag wheeled. It went past Harry down the alley in three long strides, low, its antlers lowered, and it came at the one bent over Dudley, and the one bent over Dudley straightened up and went too, up and over the fence, into the dark over the gardens of Wisteria Walk, like a sheet blown off a line.

The stars came back.

The orange glow came back at the end of the alley. It was hot again, and the honeysuckle smelled of honeysuckle, and somewhere along Wisteria Walk a television was showing a quiz programme with the volume up.

The stag stood in the alley for a moment longer, looking back at him.

Then it went.

* * *

Dudley was on the ground.

He was curled up on his side with his hands over his face and his knees drawn up, and he was shaking. Harry knelt down beside him. He did not touch him. He did not know whether he should.

He read him.

Terror. All of it. There was nothing else in Dudley at all, for a long time, nothing on top and nothing under it. Harry had only ever read terror like that in a few people, and one of them had been himself, in a graveyard, at the moment he understood that there was a body on the grass and that it was not going to get up.

"Dudley," he said. "It's gone. They've gone."

Dudley made a sound.

There were no words in it. It was the sound a very small child makes when it has been crying for so long that it has forgotten what started it.

Then somebody was coming down the alley at a run, in carpet slippers, and Harry was on his feet with his wand up before he saw who it was.

"Don't put it away, you stupid boy!"

It was Mrs Figg.

She was in a hairnet and a dressing gown and her tartan slippers, and she was out of breath, and she was holding a string shopping bag full of tins of cat food, which she seemed to have forgotten she was holding. She had come round the corner from Wisteria Walk at the far end and run the whole length of the alley, and she was frightened and furious and, under both, so guilty that it filled her right up to the top.

"Keep it out," she said. "There might be more of them. Oh, I'm going to *kill* Mundungus Fletcher."

Harry stared at her.

"You…"

"Squib," said Mrs Figg briskly. "Since I was born. Don't look like that, there's no time. I've been keeping an eye on you since you were a baby, haven't I? Dumbledore's orders. And there was somebody else supposed to be watching you tonight, and he's gone off to see about a load of cauldrons that fell off the back of a broomstick, and I *told* him. I told him." She looked down at Dudley. "Oh, the poor lamb. Is he hurt?"

"I don't know."

"Well, get him up. Get him up, we can't stop here. Get him up and get him home, and don't you dare put that wand away."

* * *

Dudley could not walk.

He could stand, if Harry held him, and he could put one foot in front of the other, but it took him a long time to remember which foot came next. He was very heavy. He was much heavier than he looked, and he looked very heavy, and Harry had his arm round his shoulders and Dudley's weight on his own shoulder and his wand in his other hand, and Mrs Figg walked beside them with the cat food, talking.

She talked the whole way.

"I put Mr Tibbles on Mundungus in the spring. I said, he'll wander off, that one. Dumbledore will hear about this. Oh, there'll be trouble." She shifted the cat food to her other hand. "I wanted to tell you. All those afternoons. I wasn't allowed."

"The photographs of cats," said Harry.

"I made it dull on purpose," said Mrs Figg. "If you'd enjoyed yourself, they'd never have left you with me again. And the cake was stale on purpose too. Mostly."

Harry read her while she said it.

It was all true. And under it, the soft grey sadness he had known since he was small, which was about him, which had always been about him. It was the sadness of a woman who had watched a little boy come round to her house for ten years, and had known who he was, and had not been allowed to say.

He did not know what to do with it.

So he held Dudley up, and walked.

At the corner of Privet Drive she stopped.

"I'll go home and send word," she said. "You go in. You stay in. Whatever happens, you stay in that house. Do you hear me?"

"Yes, Mrs Figg."

She looked at him for a moment, in the light from the street lamp, in her hairnet.

"You did that very well," she said. "That stag. Very well indeed. I've never seen one."

Then she went off up the road in her slippers, very fast.

* * *

He got Dudley up the drive and through the front door and as far as the hall.

Dudley leaned against the wall by the coats. He was a bad colour, grey and sweating, and his eyes were open and he was not looking at anything. Harry stood beside him with his wand still out, because Mrs Figg had said, and listened to the television in the sitting room.

He read Dudley again.

The terror was still there. It had gone down a little. It was not filling him to the top any more, and as it went down, there was room underneath it, and something had come up into the room.

Harry looked at it.

He looked at it for quite a long time, standing in the hall, with the news coming through the sitting-room door.

It was gratitude.

It was very small, and it was very confused, and it did not know what it was. It had never been in Dudley before. Harry was quite certain of that. He had read Dudley every day for fifteen years and there had never been anything like it in him, aimed at Harry, aimed at anybody. It sat under the terror like something that had come in from the garden by mistake and did not know where the door was.

Dudley did not know what to do with it.

Harry could feel him not knowing. He could feel him turn it over, in the place under the terror, as though it were a coin from a country he had never been to.

Then Dudley was sick on the doormat.

* * *

Uncle Vernon came out of the sitting room at the noise.

He saw Dudley first. He saw the doormat. He saw Harry, standing over his son, with his wand in his hand.

What came up in Uncle Vernon was so enormous and so fast that Harry did not, for a second, have time to read what was under it.

"What have you done to him?"

"Nothing. It wasn't me. There were…"

"*Petunia!* PETUNIA!"

Aunt Petunia came out of the kitchen with a tea towel. She saw Dudley and made a sound Harry had never heard her make, high and thin, and she ran to him and took his face in both her hands, and Dudley looked at her and did not seem to know her.

They got him into the kitchen between them. Harry went too. Nobody told him to. Nobody told him not to. Uncle Vernon sat Dudley down in a kitchen chair and Aunt Petunia ran him a glass of water and held it to his mouth, and most of it went down his front.

"What happened? Duddy? Duddy, darling, what happened? What did he do to you?"

"Wasn't him," said Dudley.

It was the first thing he had said.

The kitchen went quiet.

"Something," said Dudley. He was not looking at anybody. "It went cold. It went dark. And I heard… I heard…" He stopped. He put his hands over his face again. "I don't want to say."

Uncle Vernon looked at Harry.

The anger had come up again. It was the old anger, the purple one, but there was so much fear in it now that Harry could hardly tell the two apart, and both of them were aimed at him.

"Right," said Uncle Vernon, very quietly, which was worse than shouting. "Right. You're going to tell me exactly what…"

"There were two of them," said Harry. "In the alley. They went for Dudley. I drove them off."

"Two *what*?"

"Dementors," said Aunt Petunia.

Everybody looked at her.

She was standing by the sink, with her hand at her throat. She had gone completely white. She had said the word the way people say a word they have not said in a very long time, and have never forgotten, and have never once said out loud in this house.

"They guard the wizard prison," she said. "Azkaban." She was not looking at anybody either. She was looking at the window over the sink, at the dark garden, at nothing. "I heard… that awful boy. Telling her about them. Years ago."

Uncle Vernon's mouth was open.

"How do you…"

Harry read her.

He had read her his whole life. He had read the disgust on top, every day, at breakfast and at dinner and through the door of a cupboard under the stairs. He had read the jealousy under it, like grit in a graze. And under the jealousy, all his life, there had been the other thing. The thing with no name. The thing aimed at him and also at somebody standing just behind him, a little to one side, so that when he was seven he had turned round in the kitchen to see who it was and been sent to the cupboard for staring.

It was not under anything now.

It had come up through all of it, the disgust and the jealousy, through every floor of her, and it was at the top, and it was so plain that he did not know how he had ever not been able to name it.

It was grief.

It was grief for her sister.

It had been there for fourteen years, every day, in this kitchen. It had been aimed at Harry because he was there, and because he was the only thing left of her that Petunia could see, and because every morning of his life he had come into the kitchen and looked up at her with Lily's eyes.

He did not say anything.

He stood in the kitchen with his wand in his hand and Dudley's sick on his trainers, and looked at his aunt, and did not say one word.

She turned her head and looked at him.

She knew. He did not know how. She was not a witch, and she could not read him, and he had not moved. But she looked at him across the kitchen and her face did something very small, and she knew that he knew.

Neither of them said anything.

Then an owl came through the kitchen window.

* * *

It was a Ministry owl, a large tawny one with a brass ring on its leg, and it came in through the open top of the window over the sink, past Aunt Petunia's head, so close that she ducked. It dropped a letter on the table in front of Harry and went straight back out again.

Harry opened it.

*Dear Mr Potter,*

*We have received intelligence that you performed the Patronus Charm at twenty-three minutes past nine this evening in a Muggle-inhabited area and in the presence of a Muggle.*

*The severity of this breach of the Decree for the Reasonable Restriction of Underage Sorcery has resulted in your expulsion from Hogwarts School of Witchcraft and Wizardry. Ministry representatives will be calling at your place of residence shortly to destroy your wand.*

*As you have already received an official warning for a previous offence under section thirteen of the International Confederation of Warlocks' Statute of Secrecy, we regret to inform you that your presence is required at a disciplinary hearing at the Ministry of Magic.*

*Hoping you are well,*

*Yours sincerely,*

*Mafalda Hopkirk*
*Improper Use of Magic Office*

Harry read it twice.

He read the part about the wand three times.

*Hoping you are well.*

He put the letter down on the table. He did not say anything. He thought about a house-elf with tennis-ball eyes in his bedroom three summers ago, and a pudding on the ceiling, and a letter with this name at the bottom of it, and how he had sat on his bed afterwards with the bars going on the window and thought that this was the worst thing that could happen.

He thought about the stag, filling the alley, fence to fence.

He did not put the wand away.

"What does it say?" said Uncle Vernon.

"I've been expelled."

Something came up in Uncle Vernon that was so large and so bright and so sudden that, for a moment, it pushed everything else out of him, the fear and the anger and the fright for his son, and it was joy.

Harry read it and was not surprised.

He was surprised at what came after it. It came after, very fast, before the joy had finished. It was the fear again. It was the realisation, in Uncle Vernon, that a boy who has been expelled from a school for wizards is a boy who will not be going back to a school for wizards in September.

That he would be here.

All year.

The joy went out of Uncle Vernon like a light.

* * *

The second owl came eleven minutes later.

It was a different owl, a small brown one, ragged, and it was carrying a scrap of parchment in a round, untidy hand that Harry did not know.

*Harry. Dumbledore's just arrived at the Ministry and he's sorting it out. Do NOT leave your aunt and uncle's house. Do NOT do any more magic. Do NOT surrender your wand. Arthur Weasley.*

The third came twenty minutes after that.

It was the Ministry owl again, the tawny one, looking put out, and the letter it brought was shorter than the first and was signed by the same person and said, in a great many more words than it needed, that the Ministry had reconsidered. That Mr Potter would not be expelled at this time. That he could keep his wand until the twelfth of August, when a hearing would be held at nine o'clock in the morning to decide the matter. That he should attend.

*Hoping you are well.*

Harry put the three letters side by side on the kitchen table.

"So you're not expelled," said Uncle Vernon. He had been reading over Harry's shoulder. He did not seem to know whether this was good or bad. Harry read him not knowing.

"Not yet."

"And these… these things. These Dementors. They were… they came for…"

"For me," said Harry. "I think. Dudley was just there."

Uncle Vernon looked at Dudley. Dudley was still in the kitchen chair, with his hands in his lap now, looking at the table. He had not said anything else.

Uncle Vernon's face went very slowly, very carefully, the colour of the inside of a plum.

"Then you'll go," he said.

Harry had been expecting it. He had read it coming, through the joy and the fear, all the way up from the bottom of Uncle Vernon. He was almost relieved to hear it said.

"You'll pack your things," said Uncle Vernon, "and you'll go, tonight, and you'll take your owl and your wand and your… your *Dementors* with you, and you'll not come back. I've had enough. I've had fifteen years of it. I've had a boy in my house who makes things happen, and a giant through my door, and a… a pig's tail on my son, and my sister blown up like a…"

"He stays," said Aunt Petunia.

Uncle Vernon stopped.

She had not moved from the sink. Her hand was still at her throat. She was not looking at Harry, or at her husband. She was looking at the dark window over the sink, and the garden, and she said it to the window.

"Petunia…"

"He stays, Vernon."

"But…"

"He *stays*."

Harry read her.

He did not have to look very far down. It was all at the top now. It had been at the top since she said *Dementors*. And he read her say *he stays* and knew exactly why she said it, and knew that it was not for him.

He was not sure, afterwards, that it was for her sister either.

He thought it might have been for a girl at a kitchen window in a town in the north, in a house he had never seen, nineteen or twenty years ago, who had heard a boy tell her sister about the prison and the things that guarded it, and who had stood very still at the sink, and listened, and been afraid for her.

Uncle Vernon looked at his wife for a long moment.

Then he sat down in a kitchen chair, quite suddenly, as though his legs had decided for him.

"Go to bed," he said to Harry, without looking at him.

Harry went to bed.

* * *

They came on Sunday night.

He had spent three days in his room. He had been told to by Mrs Figg, and by Mr Weasley, and by a letter from Sirius that said *Stay where you are. Don't do anything stupid. I mean it. S.*, which Harry thought rich, from Sirius.

Uncle Vernon had not spoken to anybody. The fear in him was so constant now that it was simply the level of him, the way the sea is the level of the sea.

Dudley had not gone out. He came down for meals and went back up. And on the Saturday, at lunch, he passed Harry the ketchup without being asked.

Nobody at the table said anything about it.

Harry read him pass it. He read the small confused thing, still there, under the place where the terror had been, still not knowing where the door was.

He said thank you.

Dudley did not say anything back. But the thing under the terror moved, a little, as though it had heard.

And Aunt Petunia had not looked at Harry for three days.

On the Saturday afternoon she came in with a pile of his shirts, ironed, which she had not done since he was ten, and put them on the chair, and went out without a word.

It was still at the top of her. He did not think, now, that it would ever quite go back down.

At five o'clock on the Sunday, she announced at tea that they were going out.

"Out?" said Uncle Vernon.

"To Yvonne's," said Aunt Petunia. "For supper. All three of us. She asked weeks ago and I said we might." She put the teapot down. "We'll be back late. Very late. Ten, I should think. Eleven."

She did not look at Harry when she said it.

Harry read her not look at him.

They went at half past six. Uncle Vernon went first, confused and relieved at once, which seemed to Harry the most honest thing he had ever felt. Dudley went second. At the door he turned round and looked back up the hall at Harry, on the stairs, for about a second.

Then Aunt Petunia went out, and she did look back. She looked back at the house, not at Harry. At the hall, and the stairs, and the cupboard. As though she were checking she had turned everything off.

Then she shut the door.

* * *

At ten past eight Harry was lying on his bed with the window open, reading *Which Broomstick?* for the third time, when the house went silent in a way that had nothing to do with the Dursleys being out.

He sat up.

He read the house. It was empty. He read the street. It was the street.

He read downstairs.

There were people in the kitchen. Seven of them, eight. They had not come in through the door. He had heard no door. They were simply there, very quietly, all at once, as though they had been poured in through the keyhole.

He went to the top of the stairs with his wand out.

"Harry?" said somebody in the dark of the hall. "It's all right. It's me. It's Remus."

Harry read him.

Kind. Tired. The old guilt, aimed at Harry, that he had read in a Defence classroom in his third year. And under it, very large, relief, as though Lupin had been holding something heavy for a long time and had just been allowed to put it on a table.

"Professor Lupin?"

"Not any more. Remus will do." A wand-light came on in the hall. Lupin was greyer than he had been. His robes were more patched. "Come down. Slowly. There's somebody here who'd like to make sure you're you."

Harry came down.

There were eight of them in the kitchen. Lupin. A tall black wizard in long blue robes, with one gold earring and a deep slow voice, who said his name was Kingsley Shacklebolt and that it was a pleasure, and meant it. A witch with a square jaw and a violet bonnet. A wizard with a sad face and straw-coloured hair. Two others Harry did not know.

And Moody.

Harry stopped on the bottom step.

He was the same and he was not. The same scars, the same face like a piece of old wood that somebody had been at with an axe, the same wooden leg and the same magical eye, blue and round and spinning. He was thinner. He was a good deal thinner. He had spent ten months at the bottom of a trunk with seven locks, and it showed in the way he stood, a little forward, the way people stand who have been cold for a very long time and have not yet stopped expecting to be.

The eye spun round and fixed on Harry.

"Potter," growled Moody. "What did I give you for Christmas last year?"

Harry looked at him.

"Nothing," he said. "You weren't there. You were in a trunk."

The kitchen went quiet.

Then Moody made a sound. It was a short sound, rough, and it took Harry a second to realise it was a laugh.

"Good answer," he said. "Damn good answer."

Harry read him.

He had read the man who had worn this face for ten months, and found a blazing devotion, and thought it was an old Auror's care.

He read the real one.

Moody was suspicious. It was so loud that it filled the whole kitchen. He was suspicious of Harry, of Lupin, of the fridge. He was suspicious, very faintly, of himself, as though somewhere in the back of his own head there was a door he had not checked.

Harry had expected that.

He had not expected what was under it.

It was very plain. It was very quiet. It was the plainest thing in the kitchen, and it had nothing to do with Harry in particular. It was the feeling of a man who had spent forty years putting himself between other people and the worst things in the world, and had lost an eye and a leg and a piece of his nose doing it, and had been put at the bottom of a trunk for it, and had come out, and was doing it again tonight, because somebody had to and he was there.

Harry thought it was the most decent thing he had read in a grown man for a long time.

He did not say so.

He came down off the bottom step and into the kitchen, and somebody behind him, in the dark by the back door, said, "Wotcher, Harry," and stepped forward to shake his hand, and fell over the kitchen bin.

* * *

It went over with a crash like a drum kit falling downstairs. The lid rolled across the lino in a wide circle and came to rest at Moody's wooden foot. There were potato peelings everywhere.

"Sorry," said the person on the floor. "Sorry. Sorry, sorry. That's always happening."

"Tonks," said Moody, without turning round.

"I *know*, Mad-Eye."

She got up. She was young, younger than any of the others, not much older than Lucan. She had a pale heart-shaped face and very dark twinkling eyes and short spiky hair of a violent bubblegum pink, and she put the bin back the right way up and put the lid back on it and brushed the potato peelings off her front, and then she held out her hand again.

"Tonks," she said. "Nymphadora, but don't. Seriously. Don't."

Harry shook it.

He read her.

She was embarrassed, which was on the top and was very small and was gone almost before he found it. And under that, all the way down, she was enjoying herself. She was enjoying herself enormously. She was enjoying the kitchen, and the bin, and the potato peelings, and the strange green-eyed boy on the stairs, and the whole ridiculous dangerous business of being sent to Surrey in the dark to fetch somebody, and there was nothing under it that minded.

Harry found that he was smiling.

He did not know why.

"Are you all coming?" he said. "To wherever I'm going?"

"Ten of us," said Tonks cheerfully. "Two more outside. Overkill, I said. Mad-Eye said there's no such thing." She looked round the kitchen. "Nice and clean, isn't it? Very clean. Unnaturally clean. My mum would *hate* it."

"My aunt cleans when she's upset," said Harry.

He did not know why he had said it. He had never said anything about Aunt Petunia to anybody, not even Evelyn.

Tonks looked at him for a second, and something in her went quiet, and then came back.

"Mine bakes," she said. "Bread. Whole loaves of it. We had nineteen in the house once."

* * *

They went by broom, from the back garden, at a quarter to nine.

Moody had a piece of parchment and made Harry learn the plan off it, and then made him learn it again, and then set fire to the parchment in Aunt Petunia's sink. They would fly in a guard. Moody behind. Lupin and Kingsley at the sides. Tonks beside him. If anybody was killed, the others were to keep flying.

"Is that likely?" said Harry.

"Everything's likely," said Moody.

Harry went upstairs and packed his trunk, which Tonks Shrank to the size of a matchbox and put in her pocket. He put Hedwig in her cage. He sent her ahead with a note to say where he was going, and then remembered he did not know, and sent her anyway. He took the Firebolt out of the cupboard under the stairs, where Uncle Vernon locked it every summer, with a charm Tonks did on the padlock that made it say *thank you* in a small grateful voice before it opened.

He wrote a note to the Dursleys and left it on the kitchen table, propped against the teapot.

He thought for a long time about what to put in it.

In the end he wrote: *I've gone. Thank you for supper. H.*

He did not know which of them he meant it for. He decided, as he went out of the back door, that it did not matter, and that the one it was for would know.

* * *

It was a clear night, and very warm.

They went up off the lawn in a line, eleven of them, and Little Whinging fell away under Harry's feet, the gardens and the square lit windows and the street lamps in lines, and the alley, a dark strip between the fences, which he looked at once and then did not look at again. The heat came off the roofs. Somebody was having a barbecue on Wisteria Walk.

Then they were higher, and Surrey was a sheet of lights going out towards London on every side, and the air was cold, and Tonks was beside him on his left, on a broom that looked about as old as Moody.

She talked.

She talked the whole way.

"I've been an Auror a year. Nearly failed Stealth and Tracking. Kept falling over. Got top marks in Concealment and Disguise, mind you, so it evened out." She swerved round a pigeon. "Mum's a Black. Married a Muggle-born, got blasted off the family tree for it. Sirius is her cousin. Which makes you and me something. Second cousins once removed by godfather. Possibly nothing."

"Possibly," said Harry.

"Hufflepuff. Eleven seconds. The Hat said *Oh, definitely*, and I've never known whether to be pleased." She looked across at him. "What did it say to you?"

"It asked me where I wanted to go."

"And you said Slytherin."

"Yes."

"Brave," said Tonks. "Or something. Mad-Eye, are we going the long way round on purpose, or are you lost?"

"*Long way*," came Moody's voice from behind, "*on purpose*."

They went the long way round. They went south first and then east, and then north, and then west again, and over the river twice, and Moody made them drop into a cloud over Croydon to see if anybody followed them out of it. Nobody did. It was very wet in the cloud. Harry came out of it soaked, and cold, and laughing at something Tonks had said about a goblin in her Auror training who had bitten an examiner.

He read her laugh with him.

He read the others, too. He could not help it, up here in the dark with nothing else to read. Kingsley, on his right, calm all the way down, the calm of a man who has decided very carefully what he will be frightened of and is not frightened of anything else. Lupin, tired and watchful, and under the watchfulness, every time Harry laughed, something that hurt him and that he did not mind being hurt by. Moody, behind, suspicious of the sky.

And Tonks.

He found her not talking, at one point, somewhere over the Thames, for perhaps twenty seconds. He read her in the silence.

She was looking at the lights of London spread out under them, the bridges and the dark loop of the river and the long lit roads, and she was happy. Not about anything. She was simply happy to be up here, on a terrible old broom, at night, in the cold, with all of London under her feet.

Harry looked at the lights too.

He thought he might have been happy as well, for about twenty seconds. He could not have said. He could not read himself. But he noticed that he had not thought about a graveyard since Croydon, and he noticed that that was the longest he had gone since June.

"Down there," said Moody. "Now."

* * *

They landed in a small square of tall, tired houses, with a patch of brown grass in the middle and a broken swing and an overflowing bin. It was very late. Most of the windows were dark. Somewhere a dog was barking, two streets away, and somewhere else a stereo was playing behind a closed window.

Moody stuck a piece of parchment under Harry's nose and held up his wand-light.

The handwriting was thin and sloping. He knew it from a single sheet that had come by phoenix at four in the morning.

*The headquarters of the Order of the Phoenix may be found at number twelve, Grimmauld Place, London.*

"Read it. Learn it. Think it," said Moody. He lit the parchment with the tip of his wand and let it burn. "Now look."

Harry looked.

The houses on the side of the square in front of him were numbered on their doors. Eleven had a dustbin outside it and a light on in the basement. Thirteen had a broken bell and a sticker in the window for a band Harry had never heard of. There was no twelve. There was nothing between them at all.

He thought about it. *Number twelve, Grimmauld Place.*

He read the square while he thought about it, the way he always read a place he did not know. He read a sleeping old woman in eleven and a man in thirteen who was awake and drunk and sad. He read Moody behind him, suspicious of the bin.

And he read, between eleven and thirteen, where there was nothing at all, somebody who was waiting.

It was very loud. It was the loudest thing in the square, and it was impatient, and it was joyful, and it was furious, all three, all at the top, all at once, and Harry knew it. He had read it in a cave above Hogsmeade, and on a lake shore, and in a room above a bar in a different world, and by a fire in an old man's office with a dog's head pressed into his stomach.

A battered door was coming out of the wall between number eleven and number thirteen, pushing the two houses apart as it came, the way somebody shoulders through a crowd.

It was black. The paint was peeling. It had a silver knocker in the shape of a twisted serpent.

It was not quite all the way out of the wall when it opened.
