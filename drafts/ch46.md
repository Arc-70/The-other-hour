## Chapter 46: Little Whinging

The Nine O'Clock News came through the sitting-room window at nine o'clock every night, and every night Harry lay in the flowerbed underneath it and listened.

It was the hottest summer anybody on Privet Drive could remember. That was what they said to each other over their fences, and it was true, more or less. There had been no rain since the second week of June. There was a hosepipe ban in the south-east, and a worse one in Yorkshire, where the reservoirs had shrunk back to show old stone walls and a drowned church spire, and every night the newsreader said a little more about it in the same grave voice he used for Bosnia. The lawns of Privet Drive had gone the colour of digestive biscuits. Uncle Vernon watered his hydrangeas after dark with a watering can, because a man two doors down had been reported to the water board by a neighbour, and Uncle Vernon did not trust Mr Prentice at number seven.

Harry lay under the window, between the hydrangeas and the wall, with the dry earth under his back and the stars coming out, and waited for something that was not about the weather.

It did not come.

It had not come for thirty-three days. He had counted. There had been a train crash in Hampshire and a bomb scare at Victoria and a man in Hertfordshire who had won eleven million pounds on the new lottery. There had been the drought, and the drought, and the drought. There had not been a single thing that a person who knew what to look for could have looked at twice.

He had looked anyway. He had looked for families found asleep in their own kitchens and unable to say why. He had looked for bridges, and fogs in July, and people going missing in threes. He had looked for anyone, anywhere, who had died with no mark on them.

Nothing.

The *Daily Prophet* came every morning, by a tired brown owl that Harry paid in Knuts out of a sock, and it had nothing in it either. It had Quidditch. It had a long piece about cauldron-bottom thickness. It had, twice, a small sneering paragraph about a certain boy who had been through a great deal and who was believed, by those who knew about such things, to be suffering from what one Healer called "a fixed and attention-seeking fantasy".

Rita Skeeter's year had ended on the last day of June.

She had not wasted any of the summer.

* * *

He read the house every morning when he came down the stairs, the way other people looked out of the window to see what kind of day it was.

Uncle Vernon was angry, which was ordinary. He had been angry since King's Cross. But the fear under the anger had grown again since last summer, and it had a new shape now. There was fear of Harry in it, as always, and beside that a fear of what Harry might bring home, the way a man who keeps a dog he does not like is afraid of what it might drag in from the road.

Aunt Petunia was the same as always. Disgust on top, and under it the old thing with no name, aimed at Harry and past him.

Dudley was something else, and Harry did not look at him properly until the eighth morning, when Dudley came in to breakfast with his knuckles taped and ate six slices of toast without a word.

"Who've you been hitting?" said Uncle Vernon, delighted.

"Nobody," said Dudley. "Bag."

"That's my boy."

Harry washed his plate and went upstairs, and lay on his bed for a long time, thinking about what he had found at the bottom of his cousin, under the boredom and under the cruelty, which were both very old. It was small. A year ago he would have missed it. It was aimed at Dudley himself, and it was larger on the mornings his knuckles were taped.

* * *

Evelyn's letters came every eleven days, as she had said they would.

The first came on the twelfth of July, at seven in the morning, by a large grey Selwyn owl that sat on his windowsill and looked at Hedwig as though she were something it had been asked to step over. The letter was one side of a sheet of good cream paper, in her small square hand, and it was about the weather.

*It has not rained in Shropshire for thirty-one days. The yew walk is the only green thing for a mile. Father has had people to dinner four times. I passed the salt on each occasion. Lucan is home and works in the study with the door shut. I am reading The Daughter of Time by Josephine Tey, which is about a detective who is stuck in a hospital bed for the whole book and solves a murder from five hundred years ago by reading what everybody else has already read and noticing what they left out. I recommend it. I think you would like the hospital bed. E.S.*

He read it four times.

Then he read it again, sitting on the floor with his back against the bed, the way he would have read a face.

*People to dinner four times.* Corvin Selwyn did not have people to dinner. Corvin Selwyn had people to his study, one at a time, in the afternoon, and gave them sherry and let them talk too much. If he was having them to dinner, there were a great many of them, and they were the kind you fed.

*Lucan works in the study with the door shut.* Lucan had worked for his father for a year. He had never needed a shut door.

*Solves a murder by reading what everybody has already read and noticing what they left out.*

*I think you would like the hospital bed.*

Harry looked at that for a long time. Owls could be opened. Owls could be stopped on the way, and read, and sealed again, and sent on, and nobody would know. Evelyn knew that better than anyone alive. She had grown up in a house where every letter was read twice. She was telling him to stay where he was and watch.

She was also telling him she was doing the same.

He wrote back that night. He wrote about the hosepipe ban and the hydrangeas and Mr Prentice at number seven, at some length, and about the Nine O'Clock News, and about a programme on Channel 4 about the Normans, which he had heard through the window and which had got the date of the Domesday survey wrong by a year.

*I thought you would want to know,* he wrote. *They have not issued a correction.*

He did not sign it. He did not need to.

* * *

Dumbledore wrote once.

It came on the sixteenth of July, by a large barn owl that would not wait for an answer, and it was four lines long.

*My dear Harry,*

*I have not forgotten what I promised you. I find I cannot keep it this summer, and I am sorrier than I can say on paper. You will be told why, in time. Please stay where you are, and please be patient with those who are keeping you there.*

*A.D.*

Harry read it once and put it in the drawer of the bedside table, under his school socks.

He did not read it again. He did not need to. He knew the shape of a letter that had been written so that whoever opened it on the way would learn nothing, and he knew that the old man had written it at midnight, very tired, and that he had meant every word of the third sentence.

He also knew what *be patient* meant, when it was written by a man who had spent four years asking him to be.

It meant *I am going to keep you in the dark again, and I would like you to forgive me for it in advance.*

He did not forgive him in advance.

He put the letter in the drawer.

* * *

The scar came on eleven nights out of the first thirty.

It was never much. It came up through his forehead the way a sound comes up through a floor, always someone else's, always far away, one thing at a time.

Glee, twice more.

Impatience, which was new, and which went on for most of one night.

Once, at about three in the morning, something Harry could only call satisfaction, very cold and very slow, the satisfaction of a man putting a key into a lock he has been waiting a long time to open.

He lay awake after that one and listened to Uncle Vernon snoring through the wall, and did not go back to sleep until it was light.

He did not tell anybody. There was nobody to tell. Not by owl.

* * *

The play park at the end of Magnolia Road had a roundabout, two swings, a slide and a sign saying NO DOGS. The council had pulled up the second set of swings in the spring, and nobody had replaced them, and there were two rusted stumps in the dead grass where they had been.

Harry sat on the swing that was left on the evening of the second of August, because it was out of the house, and watched the light go.

It was twenty past eight. It was still hot. The sky over the roofs of Magnolia Crescent was the colour of weak tea, and there was no wind at all, and somewhere down the road a car alarm had been going for a quarter of an hour without anybody seeming to mind.

Dudley's gang came across the grass at half past.

He heard them before he saw them. Piers Polkiss's laugh, which always came a second after somebody else's. Malcolm and Gordon, who were large and slow and had been in Dudley's class since they were five. A boy Harry did not know, who was smoking. And Dudley, in the middle, walking in the slow rolling way he had taken to walking since he started boxing, as though he were wearing a very heavy coat.

Dudley was the Junior Heavyweight Inter-School Boxing Champion of the South-East. Aunt Petunia had told the whole of Privet Drive. There was a photograph of him on the mantelpiece in his shorts and gloves, larger than any photograph of anybody else, and he had lost four stone since Christmas, and most of it had gone into his shoulders.

He saw Harry on the swing. Harry saw him see him.

Harry read the gang, because they were there.

Piers was spiteful, and afraid of Dudley, which was what Piers had been since he was seven. Malcolm and Gordon were bored and wanted their tea. The boy Harry did not know wanted the others to like him.

And Dudley.

Dudley was bored. He was bored and cruel, as always, and there was something else on the top tonight, something warm and pleased and loose. He had hit somebody this afternoon. Harry knew it the way he knew it was Wednesday. Dudley had hit somebody, a boy, smaller than him, and it had been easy, and he had liked it.

And underneath, at the very bottom, so small and so steady that Harry had stopped being surprised by it, Dudley was frightened of himself.

It was larger tonight.

The gang went past the swings and out the far gate towards Magnolia Crescent, and Dudley went with them, and he did not say anything to Harry, and Harry did not say anything to him.

Harry sat on the swing until it was nearly dark.

* * *

He caught Dudley up at the corner of Magnolia Crescent at five to nine, because they were going the same way and there was no sense pretending they were not.

The gang had gone. Dudley was on his own, walking home in the heavy coat walk, with his hands in his pockets.

"Big night?" said Harry.

Dudley did not turn round. "Shut up."

"Who was it today?"

"What?"

"Who did you hit? Not one of those." Harry nodded back down the road. "Someone smaller."

Dudley stopped and turned round.

He looked at Harry. He was a head taller than Harry now, and nearly twice as wide, and in the orange light from the street lamp his face was the face of a large, angry, frightened boy. Harry read the anger come up. He read, under it, the thing that was always there, and he read it come up too, just a little.

"Mark Evans," said Dudley. "If you have to know. He was mouthing off."

"How old is he?"

"Ten."

"Right," said Harry.

"He was *mouthing off*."

"I said right."

Dudley stared at him. Then he turned and went on walking, faster, and Harry went after him. They came to the alley between Magnolia Crescent and Wisteria Walk, the one with the garages on one side and the high wooden fence on the other, where Harry had been chased at eight and nine and ten until he learned which nights not to come this way.

Dudley went into it. Harry went into it after him.

"You were talking in your sleep last night," said Dudley.

Harry did not answer.

"I heard you through the wall. Moaning." Dudley laughed. It was not much of a laugh. "*Don't. Cedric.* Who's Cedric? Your boyfriend?"

Harry stopped walking.

He did not do anything. He stood in the dark alley and looked at the back of his cousin's head, and he thought, quite clearly, about what he could put in there. He had not thought it in that way since a Sunday dinner two summers ago, with trifle in the fridge. He thought about the small frightened thing at the bottom of Dudley, the thing that was afraid of itself, and what it would be like if he reached down and took hold of it and made it larger. Larger than the boredom. Larger than the cruelty. So large that there was nothing else in him.

He knew exactly how to do it.

That was the trouble.

"He was a boy at school," said Harry. "He's dead."

Dudley stopped too.

And the stars went out.

* * *

It was not cloud. There was no cloud. There had been no cloud for seven weeks. The stars were there and then they were not, all of them, at once, and the orange light from the street lamp at the end of the alley shrank to a dull red point and went out, and the air went cold.

No summer night on Privet Drive had ever been this cold. This was the cold of a train in the rain, and a Quidditch pitch, and a lake, and he knew it before his mind had caught up, and his mind caught up very fast.

"Dudley," he said. "Don't move."

"What are you doing? Stop it. Stop it!"

"It's not me."

"Turn it back on!"

"I *said* it's not me."

He had his wand out. He did not remember taking it out. He was holding it in the dark, and the dark was so thick that he could not see his own hand, and the cold was coming down the alley from the Wisteria Walk end, slowly, the way a tide comes up a beach.

He reached for it.

He did not mean to. He had not meant to for two years, since a train in the rain. But it was what he did. It was what he had always done, since before he could remember, in every dark place he had ever been in. He reached out to see what was there.

It was appetite.

There was nothing else. There never was. There was no top and no bottom and no aim and no layer. There was only wanting, endless and patient and enormous, and it was coming down the alley towards them, and there were two of it.

Two.

He had felt a hundred of them once, on a Quidditch pitch, and a hundred and fifty on a lake. He had never felt two of them in a place where there should not have been any. Not in Surrey. Not in an alley between two streets of semi-detached houses with a car alarm still going somewhere a long way off.

Dudley made a noise.

A high, thin noise with no word in it, and then there was a scrape and a thud, and Harry knew without seeing that Dudley had fallen down.

He reached for Dudley. He could not help that either.

Dudley was terrified. He was so terrified that there was nothing else in him, not the cruelty, not the boredom, not the warm loose pleasure of Mark Evans, ten. And under the terror, quite suddenly, quite clearly, Dudley was somewhere else. He was in a room Harry did not know. He was being shown something. He was being shown it over and over again, and it was the worst thing he had, and Harry did not want to know what the worst thing Dudley Dursley had was, and he knew he was going to find out.

The cold reached him.

His mother screamed.

She screamed the way she always did, high and pleading, from a long way down, and the alley went away, and for a second there was a green light and a laugh, and then there was a churchyard, and *kill the spare*, and Cedric's face with nothing in it.

He was on his knees. He did not know when that had happened.

He put his hand flat on the ground. It was dry and gritty. It was the alley between Magnolia Crescent and Wisteria Walk. It was the second of August, nineteen ninety-five, and he was fifteen, and there were two Dementors in Little Whinging, and one of them was bending over his cousin.

He needed something.

He had made a stag once out of the other Sirius's love, which was not his and which he had kept. He had made a smaller one out of his own laughter at a story about a stag in a dormitory. Both of those were still in him. He could feel where they were. But the cold was in him too now, and it had its hands on everything, and when he reached for the other Sirius he found a man falling through a black curtain, and when he reached for the laughter he found Cedric.

He reached for something else.

He did not choose it. It came up on its own. It was a flat rock on the far side of a lake, in the last of the sun, and a girl in a grey cardigan saying *I signed for the jar. I'd like that noted*, without any change in her face at all.

He laughed.

He was on his knees in a dark alley with his mother screaming in his head, and he laughed, out loud, a short hoarse sound that he did not recognise, and he took hold of the thing that had made him do it and made it larger and pushed it out through the end of his wand.

"*Expecto patronum.*"

The stag came out of him like a door opening.

It was enormous. It was larger than the one on the lake. It filled the alley from the garages to the fence, white and silver and so bright that every stone in the ground threw a shadow, and it put down its head and went down the alley towards the dark shape bent over Dudley, and the appetite in that shape turned.

Harry felt it turn. He felt the wanting stop wanting Dudley and want, for one second, to be anywhere else. Then the stag's antlers went through it, and it went backwards, fast, like a sheet blown off a line, and was gone over the roofs.

The other one was behind him.

He knew it before he turned. He felt the appetite come up at his back, very close, close enough that the cold was on his neck, and he turned and the stag turned with him and went past him so close that he felt the light of it on his face, and the second one went the way of the first.

The stars came back.

The street lamp at the end of the alley came on, orange, buzzing, as though nothing had happened.

The car alarm was still going.

* * *

Dudley was on the ground on his side, with his arms round his head.

Harry went to him. He knelt down and put his hand on Dudley's shoulder, and Dudley flinched so hard that his whole body jerked, and then he went still.

"It's gone," said Harry. "They've gone. Dudley. They've gone."

Dudley did not move.

Harry read him.

He was terrified still. The terror had not gone. It had nowhere to go. It filled him to the edges, loud and white, and it was aimed at nothing, at the alley, at the dark, at the whole world. It was the terror of somebody who has found out that there are things in the world he cannot hit.

And under the terror, a long way down, there was something else.

Harry nearly missed it. It was very small and very new, and it pointed at him.

It was gratitude.

It was not the gratitude of somebody who says thank you. It had no words in it. It did not know what it was. It was a large boy on the ground who had been somewhere very bad, and had been brought back, and knew, somewhere far below anything he could have said, who had brought him. And it did not know what to do. It turned round and round in him like a dog in a basket, trying to find a way to lie down, and it could not.

Harry took his hand off his cousin's shoulder.

"Get up," he said. "Can you get up?"

Dudley was sick on the ground.

* * *

Mrs Figg came round the corner from Wisteria Walk at a run, in her hairnet and her tartan carpet slippers, with a string bag of cat food tins banging against her legs.

"Put that away!" she said. "Put it away, you silly boy, they might come back! Oh, I'm going to *murder* Mundungus Fletcher!"

Harry looked at her.

He had read Mrs Figg since he was three. He had read her soft grey sadness, aimed at him, in a sitting room that smelled of cabbage and was full of photographs of cats. He had never once known why it was aimed at him.

He read her now.

She was frightened. She was furious. She was frightened and furious and both of them were aimed at somebody who was not there, somebody called Mundungus who had evidently been supposed to be standing in this alley and had not been. And under that, steady and grey and very old, the sadness, still aimed at him.

"You know what they were," said Harry.

"Of course I know what they were. I'm a Squib, aren't I? Mundungus was meant to be watching you. He went off. Something about a load of cauldrons that had fallen off the back of a broom." She took hold of Dudley's arm and tried to lift it, and could not. "Come on. Get him up. We've got to get you home. Oh, there'll be trouble. There'll be trouble about this."

"You're a *Squib*?"

"Don't stand there with your mouth open, Harry, pick up your cousin."

Harry picked up his cousin.

"How long have you known?" he said, with Dudley's arm across his neck. "About me?"

"Since you were a baby, dear. Dumbledore asked me to keep an eye."

"You used to show me photographs of cats. For hours."

"Well, I couldn't have you *enjoying* it," said Mrs Figg. "Your aunt would never have let you come again. Mind his head on the gatepost."

It took both of them, and it took a long time. Dudley could stand, just, if somebody held him up on one side, and Harry was the one holding him. He was very heavy. He was cold all over and he was shaking, and he kept his eyes shut, and his arm round Harry's shoulders was so tight that it hurt.

They went down Wisteria Walk like that, the three of them, under the street lamps, past the dead lawns and the dry flowerbeds and the car in the drive of number forty-two with its alarm still going.

Dudley did not let go once.

At the corner of Privet Drive he opened his eyes and looked at Harry from about six inches away. His face was white and wet and frightened and it did not look like the face of the Junior Heavyweight Inter-School Boxing Champion of the South-East. It looked like the face of a boy who was eleven at a zoo, standing in front of a pane of glass that had gone.

Harry read the gratitude come up in him again, very close to the top this time, close enough to say something.

It did not say anything.

Dudley shut his eyes.

* * *

The kitchen at number four was very bright.

Aunt Petunia had the light on over the cooker and the light on over the table and the light on over the sink, and she was standing by the back door in her dressing gown with her hand at her throat, and when she saw Dudley's face she made a sound Harry had never heard her make in fourteen years.

Uncle Vernon came in from the hall.

Dudley was put into a chair. Aunt Petunia knelt in front of him with a damp tea towel. Dudley was sick again, into the washing-up bowl this time.

"What have you done to him?" said Uncle Vernon.

"Nothing."

"Don't you lie to me, boy. Look at him! *Look* at him! What did you do?"

"I didn't do anything to him. I…"

"Duddy, sweetheart, Duddy, what did he do, did he hit you, did he use his…"

"*Nothing*," said Harry. "It wasn't me. We were attacked."

"Attacked! By who? By what? What, a gang? A gang of…"

"Dementors," said Harry. "Two of them."

"What the devil are…"

"They guard the wizards' prison," said Aunt Petunia. "Azkaban."

The kitchen went quiet.

Uncle Vernon turned round very slowly and looked at his wife.

Harry did not look at her at once. He did not need to. He had been reading her since he came through the door, because he could not help it, and he had read her disgust and her terror for Dudley, which were large and ordinary and both aimed where he expected them. And when she said the word, the word that nobody on Privet Drive had ever said, the word she could not possibly have known, something else came up in her.

He looked at her then.

She was standing by the sink with the tea towel in her hands, and she had gone very white, and she was looking at nothing. She was looking at a place a little to the left of Harry's shoulder, where nobody was standing.

It was the thing.

It was the old thing, the thing with no name, the thing that had been under her disgust every day of his life. He had felt it in the cupboard at four and at the breakfast table at ten and through the bedroom door the summer he turned eleven. He had felt it come near the top when she was alone in the kitchen. He had turned round at seven to see who it was aimed at, and been punished for staring, and never found out.

It had come all the way up now. It was right at the top of her, and he could see it whole for the first time, the way you can see the bottom of a pond when the water goes still.

It was grief.

It was very old, and it was very large, and it had been worn down by being carried for so long until it had no edges at all. It was grief for a girl. A girl with red hair, who had been her sister, who had been better at everything, who had gone away to a school with a castle in it and come home a stranger, and talked at the kitchen table about things called Dementors, to someone else, not to Petunia. Who had died. Who had been dead for fourteen years.

And it was aimed at him.

It had always been aimed at him. Not because of anything he was or did. Because of his eyes.

He had been told he had his mother's eyes so many times that the words had worn smooth. He had never once thought about what it would be like to have to look at them across a breakfast table every morning, for fourteen years, in the face of a boy you did not want, and see your sister looking out.

Aunt Petunia looked at him.

She knew he had seen something. He read that too. She did not know what he could do, she had never known, but she knew that something had happened in her face, and that he had been looking at it, and she drew herself up and her mouth went thin and the disgust came back over the top of everything like a lid.

Harry did not say anything.

Neither did she.

* * *

The first owl came through the kitchen window at nine twenty-six, while Uncle Vernon was shouting.

It was a large screech owl. It dropped a letter on the table in front of Harry, right on the sugar bowl, and went out again through the window before anybody could do anything about it, and Uncle Vernon stopped in the middle of a word and stared at the window as though it had personally insulted him.

Harry opened the letter.

It was from the Improper Use of Magic Office, Ministry of Magic, and it was signed by Mafalda Hopkirk, and it informed him with regret that he had performed the Patronus Charm in the presence of a Muggle at twenty-three minutes past nine that evening, and that as this was a serious breach of the Decree for the Reasonable Restriction of Underage Sorcery, he was expelled from Hogwarts School of Witchcraft and Wizardry. Ministry representatives would be calling at his place of residence shortly to destroy his wand.

He read it twice.

Then he folded it in half and put it on the table.

"Well?" said Uncle Vernon.

"I've been expelled," said Harry.

He read the kitchen as he said it. Uncle Vernon's fear did not go down. It went sideways for a moment, into something like hope, and then it came back, larger. Aunt Petunia's lid stayed on. Dudley was not reading as anything very much, except cold.

The second owl came at nine thirty-four.

It was a small, ruffled brown owl that hit the window frame on the way in. It was from Mr Weasley, in a scrawl that had been written very fast. Dumbledore had just arrived at the Ministry and was trying to sort it all out. Harry was not to leave his aunt and uncle's house. He was not to do any more magic. He was not, on any account, to surrender his wand.

"Good," said Harry, out loud, to nobody.

Uncle Vernon made a sound like a kettle.

The third owl came at ten to ten, and it was the Ministry again, Mafalda Hopkirk again, in rather more words. The Ministry had reconsidered. Harry's wand would not be destroyed at present. He would retain it until a disciplinary hearing on the twelfth of August, at which a decision regarding his expulsion would be taken. Pending that, he was suspended. *With best wishes.*

He laughed at that. He read the *with best wishes* and thought about Mafalda Hopkirk in an office somewhere at ten to ten on a Wednesday night, writing *with best wishes* to a boy she had expelled forty minutes ago, and he laughed, and Uncle Vernon looked at him the way you look at a man who has started laughing at a funeral.

"Right," said Uncle Vernon. "That's it."

He stood up. He was very large and very purple and his fear was right up under the anger now, pushing it, the way it had pushed it in a hut on a rock.

"Out," he said. "You can go. Tonight. I'm not having it in my house. Whatever it is. Whatever you've brought. I'm not having it near *my son*. Pack your things. You're…"

"He stays," said Aunt Petunia.

She did not say it loudly.

Uncle Vernon turned round.

"Petunia…"

"He stays, Vernon." She was still standing by the sink, with the tea towel. She was not looking at Harry. She was not looking at anybody. "He stays here. That's all."

"But they… these things… they were after *Dudley*…"

"They were after him," said Aunt Petunia. "And they'll be after him wherever he goes. And he stays."

Harry read her.

The lid was on. It was on very tight. But under the lid, now that he knew what was there, he could feel the shape of it, the grief, worn smooth and enormous, aimed at his eyes. And next to it, beside it, something he had never once felt in her in fourteen years. It was not love. It was not anything like love. It was smaller and harder and plainer than that.

It was a promise.

He did not know who she had made it to. He did not ask.

Uncle Vernon sat down.

* * *

They came for him six days later.

The Dursleys were out. They had received a letter on the Saturday informing them that they had been shortlisted for the All-England Best-Kept Suburban Lawn Competition, and that the final judging would take place at a hotel in Camberley on the Tuesday evening, dinner included. Uncle Vernon had read it at breakfast three times, aloud. He had not seemed to notice that his lawn was the colour of a digestive biscuit, like every other lawn in the south of England, or that there was no such competition, or that the letter had been posted in London.

Aunt Petunia had noticed. Harry had read her notice. She had not said anything. She had gone upstairs and put on her good navy dress.

Dudley had gone with them. He had not spoken to Harry since the alley. He had not spoken to anybody very much. He had stopped going out in the evenings, and he had stopped taping his knuckles, and twice Harry had come down in the night for a glass of water and found the light on in the kitchen and Dudley sitting at the table in the dark with all the other lights on.

The second time, Dudley had looked up at him.

He had not said anything. Harry had not said anything. Harry had got his glass of water, and turned off the light over the cooker, which was in Dudley's eyes, and gone back up to bed.

The gratitude had been there. It had followed him up the stairs. It still did not know what to do.

* * *

At ten past eight on Tuesday, somebody knocked on his bedroom door.

He had his wand out before the second knock. He was standing at the far side of the room with his back to the window, and he was reading the landing, and on the landing there were five people.

Five people who had come into a locked house without unlocking anything.

He read them one at a time.

One was tired and kind, with old guilt under the kindness, aimed at Harry. He knew it at once. He had known it in a classroom with a Boggart in the wardrobe.

Another was calm. It was the deepest calm Harry had felt in anyone but Dumbledore, and it was not closed, it was simply calm, like a large still river, and under it there was attention, sharp, aimed at the house and the street and the sky.

A man was very slightly drunk and was trying to hide it.

A young woman was delighted. She was delighted about everything. She was delighted about the house and the landing and the door, and she was nervous under it, very slightly, the nervousness of somebody about to meet someone she had heard a great deal about, and under that she was delighted again.

And one of them was the loudest thing on the landing.

He was afraid. He was afraid of everything. He was afraid of the door, and the house, and the street, and the sky, and the floor under his feet, and the people on either side of him, and Harry, and above all of something that was not there at all and that he expected to be there at any moment. Nobody's ordinary fear was like this. This had been fed every day for forty years until it was the largest thing in him, and it went out of him in every direction at once, like the light from a lamp.

Harry went on reading.

It was hard to get past. But it was there. Under the fear, a long way under, there was something steady. It had no particular aim, and none of it was for Harry. It was simply there, the way a floor is there, and Harry had to go down through it twice before he could name it, because he had not expected it.

It was decency.

It was a plain, stubborn, unglamorous decency, the decency of a man who would walk into a burning house for somebody he did not like, and complain all the way in, and all the way out.

Harry had spent a year being taught by a man wearing this man's face. He had read, in that man, a blazing devotion that had never once been for him.

He opened the door.

"Potter," said the real Alastor Moody. He had a magical eye that was spinning in its socket like a marble in a bowl, and a wooden leg, and a face that looked as though someone had taken a chisel to it, and he looked Harry up and down with both eyes and a great deal of suspicion. "Lower your wand, boy, before you take somebody's eye out."

"Prove you're Moody," said Harry.

Something happened in the decency.

Harry read it happen. It was very short. It was the feeling of an old man who has been asked by a boy for the one thing he would have asked for himself, and likes him for it, and is not going to say so.

"What was in the seventh compartment of my trunk?" said Moody.

"You were."

"Good. Now put it away."

Harry put it away.

* * *

"Wotcher, Harry," said the young witch.

She came round Moody and stuck out her hand, and Harry shook it, and she had a heart-shaped face and dark twinkling eyes and short spiky hair of a violent bubblegum pink.

"Tonks," she said. "Just Tonks. Don't call me Nymphadora."

"I wasn't going to."

"Everyone's going to, eventually. My mum's fault." She looked round his bedroom with enormous interest. "Ooh, this is tidy. I was never this tidy. Is this your owl? Hello, owl."

Hedwig looked at her.

"Right," said Lupin, from the doorway. He looked older than he had in the spring. His robes were more patched. He smiled at Harry, and the guilt in him went up and down. "Hello, Harry. We're going to London. All of us, together. Pack quickly, if you can."

"Where in London?"

"We'll show you when we're there."

"*Show* me?"

"It's not a thing you can be told, I'm afraid," said Lupin. "Not until you've seen it. Pack."

The calm man was Kingsley Shacklebolt. He was very tall and black and bald, with one small gold earring, and he said "Harry" in a deep slow voice and nodded once, and went down the stairs to watch the street. The slightly drunk man was Elphias Doge, who shook Harry's hand with both of his and said he had known Harry's grandfather, and then said something else that Harry did not catch, because Tonks was trying to pack his trunk by magic and had brought the wardrobe door down on her own foot.

"Ow," she said. "Ow. No. Fine. Fine. I'm fine."

"Tonks," said Moody, from the landing.

"I said I'm *fine*."

Harry read her.

She was not embarrassed. That was the first thing he noticed about her and he never stopped noticing it. Anybody else would have been embarrassed. Tonks was cross with the wardrobe door, and amused at herself, and under that, quite calm, quite settled, she was perfectly at home in her own body, which was a thing he had felt in very few people, and never in somebody who had just dropped a wardrobe door on her foot.

She saw him looking.

"Clumsy," she said. "Always have been. Mum says I walked into the Christmas tree on my first birthday and it's been downhill ever since." She looked at his face. "You were reading me just then."

Harry went very still.

"Don't worry," said Tonks cheerfully. "I'm not a Legilimens. You just get a look. Like you're listening to something a long way off." She pushed the wardrobe door back on its hinge with her wand. "Dumbledore told us. Some of us. I'm on the list. You can read me all you like, I've got nothing to hide except that I'm terrible at Transfiguration and I failed my Stealth and Tracking twice. Come on. Socks."

* * *

They went down to the kitchen at a quarter to nine, to wait for the signal.

Moody went round it with his magical eye going, looking at the toaster and the microwave and the fridge and muttering. Elphias Doge sat at the table and looked at a photograph of Dudley on the fridge door, in his boxing gloves, with an air of polite bewilderment. Kingsley stood at the back door with his hands behind his back and watched the garden.

Tonks went over to look at the washing machine and tripped over the kitchen bin.

It was the pedal bin by the back door. It went over with a clang that was loud enough to make Moody's wand come out of his sleeve and point at it, and it rolled, and the lid came off, and a quantity of potato peelings and teabags and the remains of last night's shepherd's pie went across Aunt Petunia's clean linoleum in a long brown fan.

There was a short silence.

"Sorry," said Tonks.

"*Tergeo*," said Lupin, quite gently, and the floor was clean.

Harry found that he was laughing again.

He had laughed more in four days than in a month. He did not know why. He looked at Tonks, standing in Aunt Petunia's kitchen with a teabag stuck to her shoe and her hair gone, quite suddenly, from bubblegum pink to a deep embarrassed red, and he laughed, and she looked at him and laughed too, and Moody said "Constant vigilance" in a voice like a door shutting, and that only made it worse.

At nine o'clock exactly, a shower of red sparks went up over the roofs of Little Whinging.

"That's us," said Moody.

* * *

They flew.

Moody had his own idea of how to get to London, and it involved going nowhere near anywhere anybody might be watching, which in Moody's opinion was everywhere. They went up off the lawn of number four on their brooms in a tight square round Harry, and kept going up until Little Whinging was a grid of orange lights below them and then a smudge, and then they turned north-east, and then south, and then north again, and then over a reservoir that Moody said they would have to go round twice in case they were being followed, and then they were over London.

It was very cold up there. It was the first time Harry had been cold all summer.

Tonks flew beside him.

She flew on his left, a little ahead, and she talked the whole way.

"Three years, Auror training," she said. "Nearly failed it. Concealment and Disguise, top marks. Stealth and Tracking, twice, because I fell over a bin in the practical. Different bin. There's a theme. Mad-Eye was my supervisor. He once made me stand in a broom cupboard for eleven hours to see if I'd complain."

"Did you?"

"Not out loud. That was the test. I got a seven out of ten because I hummed." She swerved round a pigeon. "My mum's a Black. Andromeda. Sirius's favourite cousin, he says, which isn't saying much, the rest of them being what they are. My dad's Muggle-born. Ted. He's got a record collection that takes up a whole wall, and when I was nine he made me listen to the whole of *Sergeant Pepper's* backwards to prove a point. Neither of us can remember what the point was."

"Sirius," said Harry. "You know Sirius."

"Everybody where we're going knows Sirius," said Tonks. "You can't not. He's very loud."

"You're a Metamorphmagus," said Harry.

"Born one." She looked round at him on her broom. Her face changed. Her nose went long and pointed and then short and snubbed and then, quite suddenly, it was Harry's nose, and she grinned. "Dumbledore said you do something like it. But inside out."

"I don't change my face."

"No. You change what people see. Which is better, if you think about it. I can only do mine." Her nose went back. "We'll have to talk about that. I've got loads of questions. Not now, though. Mad-Eye says no talking about business on brooms. Mad-Eye says no talking full stop, actually, but I've decided he doesn't mean me."

"I DO MEAN YOU," said Moody, from about thirty feet ahead.

"See?" said Tonks. "Can't hear a word."

Harry read the others.

He did it without meaning to, flying through the cold, with the lights of London spreading out underneath him, all the way to the edges of the sky. Lupin, on his right, was tired and quiet and glad. Kingsley, ahead, was calm, and watching. Elphias Doge was frightened of falling off. Moody was afraid of everything, all the time, in every direction, and under it the plain stubborn thing was still there, steady as a floor, and it had extended itself without asking anybody, for the length of the flight, to include Harry.

And Tonks was happy.

She was simply, loudly happy to be up here in the cold at night with London underneath her, and it had no layer under it at all. It was happiness all the way down.

Harry had read a great many people. He did not think he had ever read anybody who was happy all the way down.

He found, flying, that he wanted very much to be near it.

* * *

They came down in a small, dirty square in north London, a little after eleven o'clock. It had a patch of dead grass in the middle with railings round it, and a bin overflowing onto the pavement, and tall shabby houses on all four sides, most of them with peeling front doors and dark windows. There was a smell of rubbish and hot pavement. Somewhere, a long way off, somebody was playing music very loudly with the windows open.

Moody put a piece of paper into Harry's hand and held his lit wand over it.

It was a piece of parchment. There was a line of handwriting on it, very small and very neat, and Harry knew the hand. He had seen it on a letter in a drawer under his school socks.

*The Headquarters of the Order of the Phoenix may be found at number twelve, Grimmauld Place, London.*

"Read it," said Moody. "Memorise it. Now look."

Harry looked.

He looked at the houses in front of him. There was a house with a brass number eleven on the door, with a dustbin outside it, and there was a house with a brass number thirteen, with a broken window on the first floor.

There was nothing between them.

He read the square. Lupin was tired. Kingsley was watching. Tonks was cold and grinning. And Moody was afraid, the way he was always afraid, but there was something under it now that Harry had not felt in him on the landing or on the broom, and that had nothing to do with Harry.

It was grief. It was quite old, and it was for the space between eleven and thirteen.

Harry looked at the space again.

And the wall moved.

It was not quick. It did not happen with any noise. The wall between the two houses simply bulged, as though something were pushing through it from behind, gently, and then it was not a wall any more. There was a grimy door with flaking black paint, and a set of worn stone steps, and a silver knocker in the shape of a twisted serpent, and windows above it, dark, with the curtains drawn.

Numbers eleven and thirteen had moved apart to let it in.

And neither of them, as far as Harry could tell, had noticed.
